## Working Title

How Does the Brain Construct Reality?

## Subtitle

Perception, prediction, uncertainty, and the experience of the world

---

## Central Question

If perception is not simply a recording of the external world,
how does the brain construct what we experience as reality?

And what might happen when the processes involved in perception,
prediction, uncertainty, and inference become altered?

## Core Thesis

Perception is not a passive recording of reality.

The brain continuously combines sensory information with
prior information, expectations, context, and uncertainty.

This provides a powerful framework for understanding perception.

However:

**Predictive processing is not itself an established complete
theory of schizophrenia.**

The article will distinguish:

- established neuroscience;
- computational frameworks;
- empirical findings in psychosis;
- theoretical interpretations;
- unresolved questions;
- original PIF hypotheses.

## Article Structure

### 1. The Brain Does Not Simply Record Reality

Introduce the intuitive but incomplete camera metaphor.

### 2. What Is Perception?

Define perception as an active process of interpreting sensory
information.

### 3. Sensory Information Is Incomplete

Explain ambiguity, noise, limited sensory sampling, and context.

### 4. The Brain Uses Prior Information

Introduce expectations and prior beliefs.

### 5. Prediction and Prediction Error

Introduce predictive processing carefully.

### 6. Why Perception Can Be Stable

Explain how expectations help create stable interpretations
despite noisy sensory information.

### 7. When Perception and Expectation Disagree

Introduce prediction error and model updating.

### 8. What Happens When the Balance Changes?

Discuss competing explanations without assuming a single mechanism.

### 9. Predictive Processing and Psychosis

Review evidence and theories connecting predictive processing
with psychosis.

### 10. Hallucinations

Examine the relationship between internally generated
representations and perception.

### 11. Delusions

Examine belief updating, evidence, uncertainty, and salience.

### 12. The Role of Uncertainty

Explain why uncertainty is central to inference.

### 13. From Perception to Computation

Move from phenomenological description to formal computational
questions.

### 14. Where Precision Enters the Picture

Introduce precision as a computational concept.

Do NOT yet present PIF as established.

### 15. What Predictive Processing Can—and Cannot—Explain

Explicit evidence governance.

### 16. The Unresolved Problem

Identify gaps in current knowledge.

### 17. From Constructed Reality to Schizophrenia

Connect the framework back to the clinical problem.

### 18. Conclusion

The brain constructs an interpretation of the world, but the
mechanisms linking this process to psychosis remain an open
scientific problem.

## Scientific Guardrails

The article must NOT claim:

- that the brain literally "hallucinates reality";
- that predictive processing explains all perception;
- that predictive processing is an established theory of schizophrenia;
- that hallucinations are simply failed predictions;
- that delusions are simply excessive priors;
- that dopamine is equivalent to precision;
- that PIF has been empirically demonstrated.

---

## PIF Position

PIF remains a proposed research hypothesis.

Article 03 may introduce the concept of precision and explain why
precision could become relevant to the research journey.

It should NOT claim that precision instability causes schizophrenia.

The hypothesis will be developed progressively and tested against
competing models in later articles.
