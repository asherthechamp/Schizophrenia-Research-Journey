# Article 04 — Curriculum Plan

## Working Title

What Is Predictive Processing?

## Subtitle

How the brain may use predictions to make sense of an uncertain world

---

## Position in the Research Journey

→ Establish the clinical and scientific problem.

Article 02 — The Phenomenology of Schizophrenia
→ Examine schizophrenia as lived experience.

Article 03 — How Does the Brain Construct Reality?
→ Introduce perception as an interaction between sensory evidence,

Article 04 — What Is Predictive Processing?
→ Introduce predictive processing as a computational framework for

Article 05 — What Is Prediction Error?
→ Examine the mismatch between predictions and sensory evidence.

Article 06 — What Is Precision?
→ Examine uncertainty, reliability, and precision weighting.

---

## Central Question

How might the brain use predictions to interpret incomplete,
uncertain, and constantly changing sensory information?

---

## Primary Objective

Introduce predictive processing carefully as a scientific and
computational framework.

The article should explain why prediction may be useful for biological
systems, how predictions can interact with sensory evidence, and how
prediction error can contribute to updating internal models.

The article should establish the conceptual foundation needed for the
later discussion of prediction error, precision, Bayesian inference,
and computational psychiatry.

---

## Learning Objectives

By the end of the article, the reader should understand:

1. Why sensory information is incomplete and uncertain.
2. Why biological systems may benefit from generating predictions.
3. What is meant by prediction in predictive-processing theories.
4. The distinction between bottom-up sensory information and
   top-down expectations.
5. The concept of hierarchical processing.
6. What prediction error means.
7. Why prediction error can provide information for updating models.
8. Why prediction does not mean conscious guessing.
9. The relationship between predictive processing and Bayesian
   inference.
10. Why predictive processing should not be confused with the
    free-energy principle.
11. Why predictive processing has become relevant to computational
    psychiatry.
12. Why predictive processing should not be presented as an established
    complete explanation of schizophrenia.

---

## Core Conceptual Model

The article should introduce the following simplified progression:

Sensory Evidence
        ↓
Prediction
        ↓
Comparison
        ↓
Prediction Error
        ↓
Model Updating
        ↓
New Prediction

This is a conceptual representation rather than a literal complete
description of neural computation.

---

## Major Concepts

### Prediction

Expectations generated by a biological system based on prior
information, context, and learned regularities.

### Prediction Error

A representation of the mismatch between expected and incoming
information.

### Hierarchical Processing

The idea that information may be represented at multiple levels of
abstraction, with interactions between higher- and lower-level
representations.

### Top-Down Processing

Influence from higher-level representations, expectations, or context
on lower-level processing.

### Bottom-Up Processing

Influence originating from sensory information and progressing toward
higher-level representations.

### Model Updating

Adjustment of internal representations in response to new evidence.

### Uncertainty

Incomplete knowledge about the state of the external world or the
reliability of available information.

### Precision

A computational concept related to the estimated reliability or
uncertainty of information.

Precision should be introduced only sufficiently to prepare the reader
for Article 06, where it will receive dedicated treatment.

---

## Important Conceptual Distinctions

### Predictive Processing ≠ Conscious Guessing

Predictions in computational models do not necessarily represent
conscious expectations.

### Prediction ≠ Imagination

Predictive processing does not imply that the brain simply invents
the external world.

### Prediction ≠ Hallucination

Ordinary predictive processing should not be equated with
hallucination.

### Predictive Processing ≠ Bayesian Inference

Bayesian inference is a mathematical framework for probabilistic
inference.

Predictive processing is a family of computational and theoretical
approaches concerning prediction, prediction error, and hierarchical
processing.

They are related but not identical.

### Predictive Processing ≠ Free-Energy Principle

These concepts overlap in some theoretical approaches but should not
be treated as synonyms.

---

## Relationship to Schizophrenia

The article should introduce the relevance of predictive processing to
psychiatry without claiming that it explains schizophrenia.

Possible areas of relevance include:

- hallucinations;
- delusions;
- altered salience;
- perception;
- belief updating;
- uncertainty;
- self-experience.

These should be presented as research questions and areas of active
investigation.

---

## Scientific Boundary

The article must explicitly avoid claims such as:

"Schizophrenia is caused by excessive prediction."

"Psychosis is caused by overly strong priors."

"The brain hallucinates reality."

Instead, the article should emphasize that predictive-processing
models provide frameworks for generating and testing hypotheses about
perception and psychiatric symptoms.

---

## Relationship to PIF

The Precision Instability Framework (PIF) should NOT be presented as
part of established predictive-processing theory.

PIF is an original proposed research hypothesis developed within this
research journey.

The article may introduce the conceptual question that eventually
motivates PIF:

> What happens if a system becomes unstable in determining how much
> weight different sources of information should receive?

However, the detailed PIF hypothesis should be developed later.

PIF must remain explicitly identified as:

**A proposed research hypothesis, not an established theory of
schizophrenia.**

---

## Evidence Hierarchy

The article should distinguish three levels:

### Established Scientific Findings

Empirical findings supported by substantial evidence.

### Scientific Frameworks and Theories

Models used to organize evidence and generate predictions.

### Proposed Hypotheses

Ideas that require empirical testing.

Predictive processing belongs primarily to the second category.

PIF belongs to the third category.

---

## Article 4 Thesis

A working thesis for the article:

> Perception can be understood as an ongoing process in which the brain
> uses predictions and sensory evidence to construct and update
> interpretations of an uncertain world. Predictive processing provides
> one influential computational framework for describing this process,
> but it is not a complete or established explanation of schizophrenia.

---

## What Article 4 Should NOT Do

The article should not:

- attempt to explain all of schizophrenia;
- present predictive processing as settled science;
- equate predictive processing with hallucination;
- reduce psychosis to "strong priors";
- present PIF as established;
- replace biological explanations with computational explanations;
- prematurely discuss precision in the depth reserved for Article 06.

---

> predictions do not match incoming sensory evidence?

## Scientific Status

Predictive processing:

**Active scientific framework / family of theories**

PIF:

**Original proposed research hypothesis**

PIF is not an established theory of schizophrenia.

---

## Research Principle

Throughout the Schizophrenia Research Journey:

**Established findings → clearly identified**

**Theoretical frameworks → clearly identified**

**Original hypotheses → clearly identified**

The project should never present an original hypothesis as an
established scientific finding.
