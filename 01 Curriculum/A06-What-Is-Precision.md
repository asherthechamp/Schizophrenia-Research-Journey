# Article 06 — What Is Precision?

## Curriculum Plan

**Series:** The Schizophrenia Research Journey

## Central Question

If prediction errors tell the brain that expectations and evidence do
not match, how does the brain determine how much that mismatch should
matter?

**Answer:** This leads us to the computational concept of precision.

# Article Structure

## 1. The Problem of Uncertainty

Introduce the fundamental problem:

The brain rarely receives perfectly reliable information.

Sensory signals are noisy and incomplete.

Therefore the brain needs not only information about what a signal says,
but also how reliable that signal is.

---

## 2. What Does Precision Mean?

Introduce the statistical concept:

**Precision = inverse variance**

\[
Precision = 1 / Variance
\]

High precision corresponds to lower uncertainty.

Low precision corresponds to greater uncertainty.

---

## 3. Precision Is Not Accuracy

Distinguish:

**Accuracy:** closeness to the true value.

**Precision:** consistency/reliability of measurements or estimates.

Explain that something can be precise without being accurate.

---

## 4. Precision in Bayesian Inference

Introduce:

**Prior + Evidence → Posterior**

Explain how relative uncertainty influences the contribution of prior
beliefs and incoming evidence.

Avoid excessive mathematics initially.

---

## 5. When Evidence and Beliefs Compete

Use intuitive examples showing how different levels of confidence can
change inference.

Example:

A faint visual signal versus a clear visual signal.

A reliable measurement versus a noisy measurement.

---

## 6. Precision-Weighted Prediction Error

Connect Article 05 to Article 06.

Introduce the conceptual relationship:

**Precision × Prediction Error**

Explain that the computational significance of a prediction error may
depend on estimated reliability.

Clarify that this is a computational formulation and not necessarily a
literal biological multiplication operation.

---

## 7. Precision and Attention

Explore the relationship between:

- attention;
- uncertainty;
- precision;
- precision-weighted prediction errors.

Important distinction:

**Attention is not identical to precision.**

Some predictive-processing theories model aspects of attention as
precision allocation/modulation.

---

## 8. Precision in Predictive Processing

Explain precision within hierarchical generative models.

Discuss the possibility that different levels of a hierarchy may assign
different precision to prediction errors.

---

## 9. What Could Abnormal Precision Mean?

Introduce competing possibilities:

- excessive precision assigned to sensory evidence;
- excessive precision assigned to prior expectations;
- insufficient precision assigned to sensory evidence;
- unstable precision;
- context-dependent precision abnormalities.

Do not assume a single direction.

---

## 10. Precision and Psychosis

Explain why precision has become relevant to computational theories of
psychosis.

Potential domains:

- perception;
- salience;
- belief updating;
- agency;
- hallucinations;
- delusions.

Present these as active research questions.

---

## 11. Hallucinations, Delusions, and Precision

Discuss predictive-processing interpretations carefully.

Do not claim:

**hallucination = high precision**

or

**delusion = abnormal precision**

Instead describe competing hypotheses involving the relative weighting
of sensory evidence and prior beliefs.

---

## 12. Is There Too Much Precision—or the Wrong Precision?

Challenge simplistic explanations.

Ask:

- Too much precision where?
- Too little precision where?
- At what hierarchical level?
- Over what timescale?
- For which information?
- Relative to which competing signal?

This section prepares the reader for a dynamic view.

---

## 13. From Precision to Precision Instability

Introduce the possibility that the important variable may not be a fixed
level of precision.

Instead:

**precision may fluctuate across time, context, domain, or hierarchy.**

This provides the conceptual bridge to PIF.

---

## 14. Where Does PIF Fit?

Define PIF as a proposed research hypothesis:

**Precision Instability Framework**

The hypothesis proposes that instability in precision maintenance or
allocation could contribute to unstable inference across multiple
domains.

Potential domains:

- perception;
- attention;
- salience;
- motivation;
- belief updating;
- agency.

PIF must remain explicitly labeled as a hypothesis.

---

## 15. What We Know—and What We Don't

Separate:

### Established / relatively well supported

- Precision is a standard statistical concept.
- Precision is closely related to uncertainty.
- Bayesian models use uncertainty/precision concepts.
- Predictive-processing theories use precision-weighted prediction
  errors.

### Active research

- Neural implementation of precision.
- Relationship between attention and precision.
- Precision abnormalities in psychosis.
- Computational mechanisms of hallucinations and delusions.

### Proposed hypothesis

- Precision instability as a cross-domain mechanism.
- PIF.

---

## 16. The Next Question

End with:

> If precision can change how strongly information influences inference,
> what happens when precision itself becomes unstable?

This leads to the next stage of the research journey.

---

# Scientific Guardrails

Do not claim:

- schizophrenia is caused by abnormal precision;
- people with schizophrenia simply have too much precision;
- hallucinations are caused by high precision;
- delusions are caused by high precision;
- predictive processing has already explained schizophrenia.

Prefer:

> Predictive-processing theories propose that altered precision
> weighting may contribute to some aspects of psychosis.

And:

> The direction, location, timescale, and computational meaning of altered
> precision remain active research questions.
