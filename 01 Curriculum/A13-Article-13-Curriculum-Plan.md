## Central Thesis

Consciousness is not simply information being processed by the brain. It concerns the presence and organization of subjective experience. Yet conscious experience normally arrives from a particular point of view, implicitly organized around a body, a perspective, and a sense of mineness. Understanding consciousness therefore leads naturally to the study of the self—and ultimately to the question of what happens when this normally stable first-person structure becomes disturbed.

## Scientific Guardrails

- Do not claim that consciousness has one universally accepted neural mechanism.
- Do not present one theory of consciousness as conclusively established.
- Do not equate consciousness with attention.
- Do not equate consciousness with working memory or reportability.
- Do not equate consciousness with selfhood.
- Do not reduce the self to one brain region or network.
- Do not describe schizophrenia simply as a disorder of consciousness.
- Do not treat self-disorder theories as complete causal explanations of schizophrenia.
- Do not reduce phenomenological experience directly to neural measurements.
- Do not treat neural correlates as automatically establishing causal mechanisms.
