# Article 03 — How Does the Brain Construct Reality?

## Research Dossier

### Working Title

How Does the Brain Construct Reality?

### Central Question

If perception is not simply a recording of the external world,
how does the brain construct the experience of reality?

And what might happen when perception, prediction, uncertainty,
and inference become altered?

---

# 1. Research Scope

This article investigates the relationship between:

- sensory evidence;
- perception;
- prior information;
- prediction;
- prediction error;
- uncertainty;
- Bayesian inference;
- predictive processing;
- precision;
- hallucinations;
- delusions;
- psychosis.

The article moves from basic neuroscience toward computational
psychiatry.

It does not assume that one computational framework explains
schizophrenia.

---

# 2. Evidence Hierarchy

The research should distinguish four levels.

## Level 1 — Established Scientific Findings

Examples:

- sensory systems transform incoming information;
- perception depends on both sensory input and context;
- sensory information is noisy and incomplete;
- prior knowledge can influence perception;
- uncertainty affects inference and decision-making;
- hallucinations and delusions are heterogeneous phenomena.

## Level 2 — Established Computational Frameworks

Examples:

- Bayesian inference;
- predictive processing;
- hierarchical generative models;
- prediction error;
- precision-weighted inference.

These are scientific frameworks, not automatically explanations
of schizophrenia.

## Level 3 — Research Hypotheses About Psychosis

Examples:

- altered predictive processing;
- abnormal precision weighting;
- aberrant salience;
- altered belief updating;
- disturbances of self-modeling.

These require empirical testing.

## Level 4 — Original Research Hypothesis

The Precision Instability Framework (PIF).

PIF is an original proposed hypothesis of this research journey.

It is NOT an established theory of schizophrenia.

---

# 3. Perception Is Not a Passive Recording

The intuitive model of perception is similar to a camera:

World → eyes → brain → perception.

Modern neuroscience suggests a more complicated process.

Sensory systems transform information before it reaches conscious
experience.

Perception depends on:

- sensory signals;
- context;
- prior knowledge;
- attention;
- expectations;
- internal models.

Therefore, perception should not be treated as a simple copy of
the external world.

---

# 4. Sensory Evidence Is Noisy

Real-world sensory information is incomplete.

Signals can be:

- ambiguous;
- degraded;
- noisy;
- incomplete;
- conflicting.

The brain must therefore infer what is most likely causing
the available sensory input.

This is one reason computational models of perception use
probabilistic inference.

---

# 5. Bayesian Inference

A simplified Bayesian formulation is:

P(hypothesis | evidence)
∝
P(evidence | hypothesis) × P(hypothesis)

In plain language:

Posterior belief depends on both:

- the incoming evidence;
- prior expectations.

This provides a mathematical framework for combining information.

Bayesian inference is broader than predictive processing.

They should not be treated as identical concepts.

---

# 6. Prediction

In predictive-processing frameworks, the brain is modeled as
maintaining hierarchical representations that generate predictions
about incoming sensory information.

Incoming signals can then be compared with predictions.

The mismatch can be represented as prediction error.

Conceptually:

Prediction
↓
Sensory input
↓
Mismatch
↓
Prediction error
↓
Model updating

This is a simplified conceptual representation, not a complete
description of neural implementation.

---

# 7. Prediction Error

Prediction error refers to the discrepancy between expected and
incoming information.

A large discrepancy can signal that the internal model needs
updating.

But prediction error should not be confused with surprise in every
context, nor should it automatically be equated with a particular
neurotransmitter or brain signal.

Different theories make different claims about how prediction errors
are implemented.

---

# 8. Precision

Precision can be understood, broadly, as the inverse of uncertainty.

High precision means information is treated as relatively reliable.

Low precision means information is treated as relatively uncertain.

In computational models, precision can influence how strongly
different sources of information affect inference.

A simplified conceptual expression is:

weighted information ≈ precision × prediction error

This is a conceptual simplification rather than a universal neural
equation.

---

# 9. Why Precision Matters

Consider two possibilities.

### Strong sensory evidence

The sensory signal is highly reliable.

The system may give it greater weight.

### Weak sensory evidence

The signal is noisy or ambiguous.

Prior expectations may exert greater influence.

This does not mean that the brain consciously calculates a numerical
precision value.

Precision is a computational description.

---

# 10. Predictive Processing

Predictive processing proposes that perception and cognition can be
understood partly in terms of hierarchical prediction and error
minimization.

Important researchers and traditions include:

- Rao & Ballard;
- Friston;
- Clark;
- Hohwy;
- Bastos and colleagues;
- related computational neuroscience traditions.

The exact interpretation differs between models.

Therefore the article should avoid presenting "predictive processing"
as one single universally agreed theory.

---

# 11. Predictive Processing and Perception

Predictive processing can help explain why perception is often stable
despite incomplete sensory evidence.

The brain does not have to reconstruct the world from raw data alone.

It can combine:

- current sensory evidence;
- learned structure;
- expectations;
- contextual information.

This makes perception an inference problem.

---

# 12. Perceptual Illusions

Illusions provide useful demonstrations that perception is not a
simple copy of sensory input.

The same physical stimulus can sometimes be interpreted differently
depending on:

- context;
- prior knowledge;
- surrounding information;
- expectations.

Illusions therefore demonstrate that perception involves
interpretation.

However, an illusion in a healthy person should not automatically
be treated as a model of schizophrenia.

---

# 13. Hallucinations

Hallucinations involve perception-like experiences in the absence
of an appropriate external stimulus.

They are clinically heterogeneous.

Possible dimensions include:

- sensory modality;
- perceived location;
- agency;
- controllability;
- emotional character;
- frequency;
- relation to thought.

Computational theories have proposed that internally generated
predictions or representations can sometimes acquire unusually strong
influence over perception.

But this is a theoretical explanation, not a settled complete
mechanism of hallucination.

---

# 14. Hallucinations and Predictive Processing

One influential family of hypotheses proposes that hallucinations
could arise when internally generated predictions or priors exert
unusually strong influence relative to sensory evidence.

A simplified conceptual model is:

Strong prior
+
weak or uncertain sensory evidence
→
perception strongly influenced by prior

This can be useful as an intuition.

But it should not be presented as:

"hallucinations are caused by excessive predictions."

The empirical literature is more complicated.

Alternative explanations and competing models exist.

---

# 15. Delusions

Delusions involve persistent beliefs that are not adequately explained
by available evidence and are held with varying degrees of conviction.

Computational approaches have investigated:

- abnormal belief updating;
- altered uncertainty;
- unusual learning rates;
- aberrant salience;
- reasoning biases;
- altered priors;
- changes in information sampling.

No single computational mechanism explains all delusions.

---

# 16. Aberrant Salience

Aberrant-salience theory proposes that inappropriate stimuli can
acquire excessive significance.

Dopamine has been particularly important in this literature.

Kapur's framework connected abnormal dopamine signaling with
inappropriate assignment of salience.

Important distinction:

The aberrant-salience framework is influential but does not provide
a complete explanation of every psychotic experience.

---

# 17. Uncertainty

Uncertainty is central to inference.

The brain must continually estimate:

- how reliable sensory information is;
- how reliable prior beliefs are;
- whether a model remains adequate;
- whether new evidence requires updating.

Psychosis may involve altered processing of uncertainty.

However, "uncertainty" is not itself a diagnosis or a single biological
mechanism.

---

# 18. Bayesian Models of Psychosis

Bayesian computational psychiatry has investigated whether aspects
of psychosis can be represented as changes in:

- priors;
- likelihood precision;
- uncertainty;
- learning rates;
- belief updating.

Different models make different predictions.

There is no single Bayesian model of schizophrenia.

---

# 19. Active Inference

Active inference extends ideas from predictive processing into
action and perception.

The system does not merely infer what the world is like.

It can also act in ways that change sensory input.

This creates a perception-action loop:

Action
↓
changes sensory input
↓
new evidence
↓
inference
↓
new action

Active inference is a theoretical framework.

It should not be presented as an established explanation of
schizophrenia.

---

# 20. Self and Predictive Processing

The self can also be approached as a model.

The brain maintains expectations concerning:

- the body;
- agency;
- action;
- internal states;
- social relationships;
- perspective.

Disturbances in self-experience may therefore be investigated using
computational models.

However:

A computational model of selfhood is not equivalent to proving that
schizophrenia is caused by a defective self-model.

---

# 21. The Phenomenological Connection

Article 2 examined subjective experience.

Article 3 now asks how experience might relate to inference.

The conceptual bridge is:

Experience
↓
Perception
↓
Inference
↓
Computation
↓
Neural implementation

This is a research strategy rather than an established hierarchy.

---

# 22. Important Distinctions

The article should repeatedly distinguish:

### Perception

The experience and processing of sensory information.

### Inference

The process of estimating hidden causes from available information.

### Prediction

An expectation generated by an internal model.

### Prediction Error

A mismatch between prediction and incoming information.

### Precision

A computational measure related to uncertainty/reliability.

### Predictive Processing

A family of computational and theoretical approaches.

### Psychosis

A clinical syndrome involving phenomena such as hallucinations,
delusions, and disorganization.

### PIF

An original proposed research hypothesis.

These terms must not be collapsed into one another.

---

# 23. What Predictive Processing Can Explain

Potential strengths:

- provides a formal language for perception;
- explains the importance of context and prior information;
- links perception with inference;
- provides computational variables;
- generates experimentally testable predictions;
- creates bridges between cognition and neural models.

---

# 24. What Predictive Processing Cannot Yet Explain

The framework does not by itself establish:

- a single cause of schizophrenia;
- one mechanism for hallucinations;
- one mechanism for delusions;
- why schizophrenia is heterogeneous;
- why symptoms differ across individuals;
- why some symptoms respond differently to treatment;
- why psychosis emerges developmentally.

Therefore predictive processing should remain one framework among
several.

---

# 25. The Precision Instability Framework

PIF proposes that instability in maintaining or allocating precision
could contribute to disturbances across multiple domains.

Potential domains include:

- perception;
- attention;
- sensory evidence;
- salience;
- cognition;
- motivation;
- self-processing.

However, this is currently a proposed hypothesis.

It has not been established empirically.

PIF must therefore be treated as a question:

> Could instability in precision maintenance help explain
> cross-domain disturbances in schizophrenia?

Not as a conclusion:

> Precision instability causes schizophrenia.

---

# 26. Competing Explanations

The research journey should compare PIF against:

- aberrant salience models;
- predictive-processing models;
- Bayesian belief-updating models;
- dopamine-centered models;
- glutamatergic models;
- E/I balance models;
- neurodevelopmental models;
- cognitive-control models;
- network-level models.

No model should receive preferential treatment simply because it fits
the project's original hypothesis.

---

# 27. Major Research Questions

1. How much of perception can be explained by predictive processing?
2. How are priors represented neurally?
3. How is precision implemented biologically?
4. How does uncertainty influence perception?
5. How do hallucinations arise?
6. How do delusions arise?
7. How does salience interact with belief?
8. How does agency emerge?
9. How does self-experience emerge?
10. Are psychotic symptoms generated by common mechanisms?
11. Can computational parameters predict symptoms?
12. Can computational models improve treatment?

---

# 28. Evidence Governance

Every claim in the final article should be classified mentally as:

**Established**
**Supported but incomplete**
**Theoretical**
**Contested**
**Unknown**
**Original hypothesis**

The article should never blur these categories.

# 30. Final Research Principle

The article should end with an open scientific question:

> If the brain constructs its perception of the world by combining
> evidence with expectations, what happens when the system becomes
> uncertain about which information deserves to be trusted?

This question leads naturally to Article 4.

---

# Scientific Status

This dossier deliberately separates established findings,
computational frameworks, hypotheses about psychosis, and the
original PIF hypothesis.

PIF remains a proposed research hypothesis.

It is not an established theory of schizophrenia.
