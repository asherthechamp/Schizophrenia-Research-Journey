# Article 04 — Predictive Processing Research Dossier

## Working Title

What Is Predictive Processing?

## Research Question

How might the brain use predictions to interpret incomplete,
uncertain, and constantly changing sensory information?

---

# 1. Executive Summary

Predictive processing is a family of computational and theoretical
approaches proposing that perception and cognition involve the use of
internal models to predict sensory input and update those models in
response to mismatches between predicted and incoming information.

A simplified formulation is:

Prediction
↓
Sensory evidence
↓
Comparison
↓
Prediction error
↓
Model updating
↓
New prediction

This should be understood as a conceptual and computational framework,
not as a literal complete description of every neural process.

Predictive processing has become influential because it offers a
potentially unified account of perception, learning, action, and some
aspects of cognition.

However, the empirical status of predictive processing remains an
active scientific question.

Evidence exists for neural and behavioral phenomena compatible with
predictive-processing models, but important theoretical claims remain
under debate and some findings can be explained by competing models.

Therefore:

Predictive processing = influential scientific framework/family of
models.

It is NOT:

- a completed theory of the brain;
- a complete explanation of consciousness;
- a complete explanation of schizophrenia;
- synonymous with Bayesian inference;
- synonymous with the free-energy principle;
- synonymous with hallucination.

---

# 2. Historical Background

## 2.1 Helmholtz and Unconscious Inference

The general idea that perception involves inference predates modern
predictive-processing theories.

Hermann von Helmholtz argued that perception involves unconscious
inference: the nervous system uses sensory information together with
previous knowledge to infer the causes of sensory stimulation.

This historical idea provides an important conceptual ancestor of
modern Bayesian and predictive approaches.

The modern predictive-processing framework should therefore not be
presented as appearing from nowhere.

A longer intellectual lineage exists:

Unconscious inference
→ probabilistic inference
→ computational models of perception
→ predictive coding
→ predictive processing

---

# 3. Predictive Coding

One influential computational formulation is predictive coding.

In a simplified hierarchical model:

Higher levels generate predictions about lower-level activity.

Lower levels provide information about mismatches between predictions
and incoming activity.

This creates reciprocal information flow:

Top-down:
Predictions

Bottom-up:
Prediction errors / residual signals

A landmark computational model by Rao and Ballard (1999) proposed a
visual system in which feedback connections carried predictions from
higher to lower visual areas while feedforward connections carried
residual errors.

Their model demonstrated that a hierarchical predictive-coding
network could reproduce several properties of visual receptive fields.

This was a computational model, not proof that the visual cortex
literally implements the model in exactly that form.

---

# 4. Predictive Processing

Predictive processing is broader than one particular predictive-coding
implementation.

The general framework proposes that biological systems can be
understood as continuously generating expectations about sensory input
and updating internal models when observations differ from those
expectations.

Andy Clark's 2013 review describes the predictive brain in terms of
hierarchical generative models, top-down predictions, prediction error,
perception, attention, and action.

The framework has therefore expanded beyond a narrowly visual theory
toward a broader theory of perception and cognition.

---

# 5. Generative Models

A central concept is the generative model.

A generative model represents possible relationships between causes
and observations.

In simplified form:

Hidden causes
↓
Generative model
↓
Predicted sensory consequences

For example:

Object
↓
Predicted visual features
↓
Sensory input

The brain can then compare the predicted consequences with actual
sensory observations.

The purpose is not necessarily to reconstruct the world perfectly.

The system must construct useful estimates of what is causing its
sensory inputs.

---

# 6. Prediction

Prediction in predictive processing does not necessarily mean
conscious guessing.

Predictions can be automatic and implicit.

Examples may include:

- visual features;
- sounds;
- movement;
- timing;
- language;
- bodily sensations;
- consequences of actions.

The system can therefore behave as if it is continuously estimating
what sensory information should arrive next.

---

# 7. Prediction Error

When incoming information differs from the predicted information, the
difference can be represented as prediction error.

Conceptually:

Prediction error
=
Observed information
−
Predicted information

The exact mathematical and neural meaning varies between models.

Prediction error should therefore not be treated as one universally
defined neural signal.

In predictive-coding accounts, prediction errors can be propagated
through hierarchical systems and used to update internal models.

---

# 8. Why Prediction Error Matters

Prediction error is not simply a problem.

It can provide information.

If the world behaves exactly as expected, little model revision may be
required.

If something unexpected occurs, the mismatch may indicate that the
current model is incomplete or inaccurate.

Therefore:

Prediction
→
Prediction error
→
Learning/model updating

can form a continuous adaptive cycle.

---

# 9. Hierarchical Processing

Predictive-processing theories commonly use hierarchical models.

Different levels can represent different degrees of abstraction.

A simplified example:

High-level causes
↓
Objects
↓
Shapes
↓
Features
↓
Sensory signals

Higher levels can provide predictions about lower-level states.

Lower levels can provide information about discrepancies between
predictions and sensory observations.

The actual organization of cortical systems is considerably more
complex than this simplified hierarchy.

The diagram should therefore be treated as conceptual.

---

# 10. Top-Down and Bottom-Up Processing

Predictive processing emphasizes reciprocal interactions.

## Bottom-Up

Information derived from sensory input influences higher-level
representations.

## Top-Down

Higher-level expectations and contextual information influence
lower-level processing.

Predictive-processing theories therefore challenge a purely
feedforward picture of perception.

However, predictive processing does not imply that all perception is
top-down.

Sensory evidence remains fundamental.

The framework concerns interaction between evidence and internal
models.

---

# 11. Prediction Does Not Mean Imagination

A major communication risk is the phrase:

"The brain hallucinates reality."

This can be useful metaphorically but is scientifically dangerous if
taken literally.

Predictive processing does not mean that the brain simply invents
the external world.

Perception remains constrained by sensory evidence.

A more scientifically defensible formulation is:

> The brain may construct perceptual interpretations through the
> interaction of sensory evidence, prior information, context, and
> inference.

---

# 12. Predictive Processing and Bayesian Inference

Predictive processing is related to Bayesian inference but should not
be treated as identical to it.

Bayesian inference provides a mathematical framework for combining
prior information and evidence.

A simplified expression is:

P(hypothesis | evidence)
∝
P(evidence | hypothesis) × P(hypothesis)

Predictive-processing theories can use probabilistic generative models
and Bayesian ideas to describe how an organism could infer hidden
causes of sensory information.

However:

Bayesian inference
≠
predictive processing

Bayesian inference is a mathematical framework.

Predictive processing is a broader family of computational and
neuroscientific approaches.

Different predictive-processing theories make different assumptions
about how probabilistic inference could be implemented biologically.

---

# 13. Predictive Processing and the Free-Energy Principle

Predictive processing is also frequently discussed alongside Karl
Friston's free-energy principle.

The relationship is important but should not be simplified into:

Predictive processing = free-energy principle

The free-energy principle is a broad theoretical framework concerning
how biological systems maintain themselves within uncertain
environments.

Predictive coding and active inference can be formulated within
free-energy-based approaches under particular assumptions.

Nevertheless, these terms refer to different theoretical levels and
should not be used interchangeably.

---

# 14. Prediction and Action

Predictive-processing ideas have expanded beyond passive perception.

In some formulations, organisms do not merely update internal models
to explain sensory input.

They also act on the world.

Action can change sensory input so that it conforms more closely to
predictions.

This provides a bridge between:

Perception
+
Action
+
Learning

Some theories describe this more specifically as active inference.

This broader action-oriented framework is important but should not
dominate Article 4.

---

# 15. Precision

Precision is an important concept in predictive-processing models.

It is broadly related to the estimated reliability of information.

In statistical terms, precision is commonly associated with inverse
variance.

Conceptually:

High precision
→
greater estimated reliability

Low precision
→
greater uncertainty

Precision can therefore influence how strongly different information
sources contribute to inference.

However, precision should not be treated as identical to attention.

Some predictive-processing theories connect attention with
precision-weighting
