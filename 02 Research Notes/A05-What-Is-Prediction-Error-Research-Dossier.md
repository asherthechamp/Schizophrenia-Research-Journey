# Article 05 — Research Dossier

## What Is Prediction Error?

### Series

The Schizophrenia Research Journey

### Article

05

---

# 1. Central Research Question

What is a prediction error, how does it influence perception and
learning, and what might altered prediction-error processing contribute
to psychosis?

---

# 2. Central Scientific Claim

A prediction error can be understood computationally as a discrepancy
between an expected state or outcome and the information actually
received.

However, "prediction error" is not one single biological signal.

Its meaning depends on the computational domain.

Examples include:

- sensory prediction error;
- perceptual prediction error;
- reward prediction error;
- value prediction error;
- behavioral prediction error;
- hierarchical prediction errors.

These should not be treated as interchangeable.

---

# 3. Foundational Predictive-Coding Model

Rao and Ballard (1999) proposed a hierarchical model of visual
processing in which higher cortical levels send predictions toward
lower levels while lower levels communicate residual errors between
predicted and actual activity.

The model demonstrated how hierarchical predictive coding could account
for aspects of visual receptive-field organization.

Important distinction:

This was a computational model.

It did not establish that the brain literally implements prediction
error in exactly the proposed architecture.

---

# 4. Hierarchical Predictive Coding

In predictive-coding frameworks:

Higher levels:

- generate predictions;
- represent increasingly abstract causes;
- influence lower-level processing.

Lower levels:

- receive sensory input;
- compare incoming information with predictions;
- transmit prediction-error information upward.

Simplified:

Prediction
↓
Sensory input
↓
Comparison
↓
Prediction error
↓
Belief/model updating
↓
New prediction

This is a conceptual and computational framework, not a literal
one-to-one anatomical map.

---

# 5. Prediction Error Is Not "An Error" in the Ordinary Sense

The word "error" can be misleading.

A prediction error does not necessarily mean that the brain has made a
mistake.

It means that there is a discrepancy between expectation and
observation.

That discrepancy can be informative.

For example:

Expected: 10

Observed: 12

Prediction error:

12 - 10 = +2

The positive discrepancy indicates that the observed value exceeded the
prediction.

In another situation:

Expected: 10

Observed: 7

Prediction error:

7 - 10 = -3

The negative discrepancy indicates that the observed value was below
the prediction.

The exact mathematical formulation depends on the model.

---

# 6. Prediction Error and Learning

Prediction errors can provide information that drives learning.

If an outcome is completely expected, there may be little new
information for updating the relevant model.

If an outcome differs from expectation, the discrepancy can indicate
that the model should be revised.

Simplified:

Prediction
↓
Outcome
↓
Mismatch
↓
Updating
↓
New prediction

This principle appears in several learning frameworks.

---

# 7. Reinforcement Learning Prediction Error

In reinforcement learning, reward prediction error has a more specific
definition.

A simplified formulation is:

Reward prediction error =
received reward - expected reward

Positive reward prediction error:

Received reward > expected reward

Negative reward prediction error:

Received reward < expected reward

Approximately zero reward prediction error:

Received reward ≈ expected reward

These errors can function as teaching signals for learning value.

---

# 8. Dopamine and Reward Prediction Error

Dopamine neurons have provided some of the strongest biological
evidence for reward-prediction-error-like signaling.

Classic findings include:

- unexpected rewards can produce phasic dopamine increases;
- predicted rewards shift dopamine responses toward predictive cues;
- omission of an expected reward can produce a reduction in activity.

This is consistent with reinforcement-learning models.

However:

Dopamine ≠ prediction error in general.

Dopamine neurons are heterogeneous and dopamine participates in
multiple functions.

Prediction-error coding is therefore one important aspect of dopamine
function rather than a complete account of dopamine biology.

---

# 9. Sensory Prediction Error vs Reward Prediction Error

These concepts should be explicitly separated.

### Sensory prediction error

Discrepancy between predicted sensory input and incoming sensory
information.

Example:

Expected sound → actual sound differs.

### Reward prediction error

Discrepancy between predicted and received reward/value.

Example:

Expected reward = 10

Received reward = 15

Reward prediction error = +5

They share a computational structure but are not necessarily the same
neural process.

---

# 10. Prediction Error vs Surprise

Prediction error and surprise are related but not identical.

A prediction error describes a discrepancy between expectation and
observation.

Surprise can refer to the information-theoretic unexpectedness of an
observation.

A commonly used information-theoretic formulation is:

Surprise = -log P(observation)

Therefore:

Low probability event → high surprise

High probability event → low surprise

A large numerical mismatch does not automatically imply maximal
subjective surprise.

Likewise, an event can be surprising while the computational response
depends strongly on the context and precision assigned to the signal.

---

# 11. Subjective Surprise Is Not Neural Prediction Error

The psychological experience:

"I am surprised"

should not automatically be equated with a neural prediction-error
signal.

Predictive-processing theories operate at computational and neural
levels.

Subjective experience is a different level of analysis.

This distinction should be preserved throughout the article.

---

# 12. Precision-Weighted Prediction Error

Not every prediction error should receive equal influence.

A central idea in predictive-processing frameworks is precision.

Precision is commonly formalized as inverse variance.

Precision ≈ 1 / variance

Higher precision:

Greater estimated reliability.

Lower precision:

Greater estimated uncertainty.

A simplified conceptual expression is:

Precision-weighted prediction error
=
precision × prediction error

Therefore:

Large error + low precision
→ potentially limited influence

Smaller error + high precision
→ potentially substantial influence

This is one of the most important concepts leading into Article 06.

---

# 13. Prediction Error Does Not Simply Need to Be Minimized

Predictive-processing literature often uses language of prediction-error
minimization.

This should not be interpreted as:

"The brain wants every unexpected event to disappear."

Organisms can seek novelty.

Learning requires encountering information that differs from current
expectations.

The computational question is therefore not simply:

"How can the brain eliminate all prediction errors?"

It is:

"How should the system use discrepancies between predictions and
observations to maintain useful models of the world?"

---

# 14. Hierarchical Prediction Errors

Prediction errors can exist at multiple levels of a hierarchy.

For example:

Low level:

Unexpected sensory feature.

Intermediate level:

Unexpected object or event.

Higher level:

Unexpected context or cause.

A discrepancy at one level can be explained by changing representations
at another level.

Therefore:

Prediction error is not necessarily a single scalar quantity.

There may be multiple prediction errors operating across different
levels and timescales.

---

# 15. Why Context Matters

The same physical event can have different informational significance
depending on context.

An unexpected sound in a quiet laboratory may be highly informative.

The same sound during a noisy concert may be relatively uninformative.

Predictive-processing models therefore introduce precision and
context-sensitive weighting.

The brain must estimate not only:

"What happened?"

but also:

"How reliable or informative is this discrepancy?"

---

# 16. Prediction Error and Attention

Prediction error is closely related theoretically to attention and
precision.

Attention can alter the effective influence of information.

In predictive-processing accounts, precision weighting provides a
possible computational bridge between:

- attention;
- uncertainty;
- salience;
- sensory evidence;
- prediction error.

However, these concepts should not be treated as synonyms.

Attention is not simply prediction error.

Salience is not simply prediction error.

Precision is not simply attention.

They are related concepts at different explanatory levels.

---

# 17. Prediction Error and Psychosis

Predictive-coding theories have been applied to psychosis because
psychotic experiences involve disturbances of inference, perception,
belief, salience, and agency.

One influential hypothesis is that abnormal precision weighting could
alter the influence of prediction errors.

Possible consequences could include:

- excessive influence of sensory discrepancies;
- insufficient influence of sensory discrepancies;
- inappropriate updating;
- persistence of maladaptive beliefs;
- altered interpretation of ambiguous information.

These are hypotheses rather than established explanations.

---

# 18. Hallucinations

One possible predictive-processing account of hallucination involves an
altered balance between sensory evidence and internally generated
predictions.

However, several competing formulations exist.

Possibilities include:

- altered precision of sensory prediction errors;
- altered precision of higher-level predictions;
- abnormal hierarchical inference;
- altered sensory attenuation;
- interactions among several mechanisms.

The evidence does not justify reducing hallucinations to one simple
prediction-error abnormality.

---

# 19. Delusions

Delusions involve belief formation and updating.

Prediction-error frameworks therefore ask whether unusual responses to
new evidence could contribute to:

- excessive belief updating;
- insufficient belief updating;
- inappropriate persistence;
- unusual weighting of evidence;
- altered uncertainty estimation.

But delusions are heterogeneous.

Different delusional phenomena may involve different computational
mechanisms.

---

# 20. Aberrant Salience

The aberrant-salience hypothesis proposed an important connection among
dopamine, salience, and psychosis.

The general idea is that abnormal dopamine signaling could cause
otherwise neutral events to acquire excessive motivational or
behavioral significance.

Prediction-error concepts have subsequently been incorporated into
computational accounts of salience.

However, the simple statement:

"Psychosis = excessive dopamine = prediction error"

is not scientifically adequate.

Modern accounts emphasize:

- dopamine heterogeneity;
- reward and utility;
- salience;
- learning;
- context;
- precision;
- uncertainty;
- individual differences.

---

# 21. Evidence From Psychosis Research

Predictive-coding accounts of psychosis have proposed abnormalities in
the precision assigned to priors and sensory evidence.

However, empirical findings are heterogeneous.

Some studies support altered sensory prediction or prediction-error
responses.

Other findings point toward altered higher-level expectations.

Different symptoms may involve different computational alterations.

Therefore the evidence currently supports predictive processing as a
useful research framework, not as a complete theory of schizophrenia.

---

# 22. Important 2026 Perspective

Recent reviews emphasize that the empirical status of predictive coding
remains unsettled.

A major problem is that similar neural responses can sometimes be
interpreted as prediction errors while potentially reflecting different
underlying computations.

Therefore:

Observed neural response
≠ automatically established prediction-error computation.

The computational interpretation requires careful modeling and
experimental design.

---

# 23. What We Can Say With Confidence

### Strong conceptual foundation

Prediction-error computations are central to several influential
learning and inference frameworks.

### Strong evidence in reinforcement learning

Reward prediction-error-like signals are strongly associated with
dopaminergic systems and reinforcement learning.

### Strong theoretical role in predictive coding

Prediction errors provide a central mechanism for updating hierarchical
generative models.

### Active empirical question

Exactly how sensory prediction errors are represented in cortical
circuits remains an active research area.

### Active psychiatric research question

Whether altered prediction-error processing contributes causally to
specific psychotic symptoms remains unresolved.

---

# 24. What We Should Not Claim

Do not claim:

"Prediction errors are simply what the brain uses to detect mistakes."

Do not claim:

"All prediction errors are the same."

Do not claim:

"Dopamine is the prediction-error chemical."

Do not claim:

"Prediction error causes schizophrenia."

Do not claim:

"Hallucinations are prediction errors."

Do not claim:

"Psychosis is caused by excessive prediction errors."

Do not claim:

"Predictive processing has been proven to explain schizophrenia."

---

# 25. PIF Connection

The Precision Instability Framework can be introduced as a hypothesis
building on the relationship between prediction error and precision.

Conceptual sequence:

Prediction
↓
Prediction error
↓
Precision weighting
↓
Updating
↓
Perception / belief
↓
Experience

PIF proposes that instability in precision maintenance or allocation
could produce unstable weighting of information across psychological
domains.

This could potentially affect:

- sensory inference;
- attention;
- salience;
- belief updating;
- motivation;
- agency.

However, PIF is a proposed research hypothesis.

It requires:

- operational definitions;
- measurable computational parameters;
- empirical tests;
- comparison with alternative models;
- falsifiable predictions.

---

# 26. Research Questions for PIF

Potential future questions:

1. Can precision instability be computationally operationalized?

2. Can it be distinguished from simply low precision?

3. Can it be distinguished from excessive precision?

4. Does precision instability vary over time?

5. Does it occur across multiple psychological domains?

6. Does temporal instability predict psychotic symptoms?

7. Can dynamic measures outperform static measures?

8. Can EEG or fMRI provide measurable proxies?

9. Can computational parameters predict individual differences?

10. What observations would falsify the PIF hypothesis?

# 29. Candidate Opening

"Imagine expecting one thing and encountering another.

You expect the light to stay green.

It turns yellow.

You expect a familiar voice.

The sound is different.

You expect a reward.

Nothing arrives.

Something has changed.

The difference between what we expected and what actually happens can
carry information.

In computational neuroscience, this discrepancy is often called a
prediction error."

---

# 30. Candidate Closing

"Prediction error tells us that something did not match our
expectation.

But it does not tell us how much that mismatch should matter.

A noisy signal may deserve little weight.

A reliable signal may demand a major update.

The brain therefore needs more than predictions and errors.

It needs a way of estimating the reliability of those errors.

That brings us to the next question:

What is precision?"

# 33. Article 05 Scientific Position

Central message:

Prediction error is not simply "a mistake."

It is a computational description of a discrepancy between expectation
and observation.

Its significance depends on context, uncertainty, computational level,
and—within predictive-processing frameworks—the precision assigned to
the error.

The scientific challenge is to determine what particular prediction
errors represent biologically and how they contribute to perception,
learning, and psychiatric symptoms.
