# Article 06 — What Is Precision?

## Research Dossier

**Series:** The Schizophrenia Research Journey

**Article:** 06

---

# Central Research Question

If prediction errors tell the brain that expectations and evidence do
not match, how does the system determine how much that mismatch should
matter?

The computational concept of **precision** provides one framework for
addressing this question.

---

# 1. Statistical Definition

Precision is commonly defined as the inverse of variance:

Precision = 1 / Variance

High precision corresponds to lower uncertainty.

Low precision corresponds to greater uncertainty.

---

# 2. Precision vs Accuracy

Accuracy concerns closeness to the true value.

Precision concerns consistency or uncertainty.

A measurement can be precise but inaccurate.

Therefore:

Precision ≠ correctness.

This distinction is essential when discussing psychiatric inference.

---

# 3. Bayesian Inference

A simplified Bayesian structure is:

Prior + Evidence → Posterior

The relative uncertainty of prior beliefs and sensory evidence affects
their influence on the resulting inference.

Higher-precision information has greater influence than lower-precision
information, all else being equal.

---

# 4. Precision-Weighted Prediction Error

Predictive-processing models frequently represent prediction errors in
precision-weighted form.

Conceptually:

Precision × Prediction Error

The computational interpretation is that the estimated reliability of
information influences how strongly a prediction error affects inference
and updating.

This should not be interpreted as a claim that the brain literally
performs a simple multiplication at a single anatomical location.

---

# 5. Precision and Attention

Feldman & Friston (2010) proposed a framework in which attention can be
understood partly in terms of inference about uncertainty or precision.

Relevant concepts include:

- attention;
- uncertainty;
- precision;
- sensory weighting;
- predictive coding.

Important distinction:

Attention ≠ precision.

Some predictive-processing theories instead propose that attentional
mechanisms can modulate or estimate precision.

---

# 6. Hierarchical Precision

Predictive-processing models describe hierarchical inference.

Different levels of the hierarchy may have different predictions,
prediction errors, and precision estimates.

Therefore:

Precision at one processing level does not necessarily imply the same
precision at another level.

---

# 7. Precision and Psychosis

Predictive-coding accounts have proposed that altered precision
weighting may contribute to psychosis.

Possible mechanisms include:

- altered precision of sensory evidence;
- altered precision of prior beliefs;
- altered precision of prediction errors;
- altered hierarchical weighting;
- altered uncertainty estimation.

These are research hypotheses rather than established explanations of
schizophrenia.

---

# 8. Hallucinations

Possible predictive-processing explanations include:

- excessive influence of high-level predictions;
- altered precision of sensory prediction errors;
- hierarchical mismatches;
- altered sensory attenuation.

Do not claim:

Hallucination = high precision.

Instead:

Altered precision weighting is one proposed computational mechanism
that may contribute to some hallucinations.

---

# 9. Delusions

Possible computational mechanisms include:

- altered prior precision;
- altered sensory precision;
- altered learning rates;
- altered prediction-error signaling;
- abnormal belief updating;
- hierarchical inference abnormalities.

Delusions should not be reduced to a single precision abnormality.

---

# 10. Competing Accounts

Current research does not support one universal direction of precision
abnormality.

Possible top-down account:

Overly precise priors and/or noisy sensory evidence.

Possible bottom-up account:

Noisy priors and/or overly precise sensory evidence.

Recent reviews emphasize that different stages and manifestations of
psychosis may involve different computational alterations.

---

# 11. Hierarchical and Methodological Constraints

Predictive-coding models of psychosis remain insufficiently constrained.

Different alterations in precision at different hierarchical levels can
sometimes explain similar phenomena.

Layer-specific neuroimaging and computational modeling may help
distinguish competing hypotheses.

---

# 12. Precision Is Dynamic

Precision should not necessarily be treated as a fixed quantity.

It can vary with:

- sensory reliability;
- context;
- attention;
- environmental uncertainty;
- internal state;
- task demands.

Therefore the important question may involve how precision changes over
time.

---

# 13. Precision vs Instability

A healthy system should be flexible.

Precision should change when environmental reliability changes.

Therefore:

Precision variability ≠ pathological instability.

PIF should distinguish adaptive precision adjustment from excessive,
inappropriate, or poorly regulated fluctuations.

---

# 14. PIF Hypothesis

The Precision Instability Framework proposes the possibility that an
important computational abnormality could involve instability in the
maintenance or allocation of precision.

Conceptual sequence:

Uncertainty

↓

Precision

↓

Precision weighting

↓

Prediction-error influence

↓

Inference

↓

Experience

PIF adds:

Precision

↓

Temporal stability?

↓

Inference

The question is whether abnormal temporal variability in effective
precision weighting could contribute to instability across multiple
domains.

---

# 15. Potential PIF Operationalization

Potential approaches include:

## Computational Modeling

Estimate:

- precision;
- learning rate;
- volatility;
- prediction-error weighting;
- trial-by-trial parameter variability.

## EEG/MEG

Investigate:

- sensory responses;
- oscillatory dynamics;
- prediction-error-related responses;
- neural variability.

## fMRI

Investigate:

- dynamic functional connectivity;
- network-state transitions;
- hierarchical interactions;
- temporal variability.

## Behavioral Tasks

Measure:

- perceptual inference;
- uncertainty estimation;
- reversal learning;
- volatility tracking;
- belief updating.

---

# 16. Precision Instability vs Noise

PIF should not define instability as simple random noise.

A stronger operational definition would involve excessive temporal
variability in effective information weighting beyond what would be
expected from measurement noise, task variability, or ordinary adaptive
flexibility.

---

# 17. Precision Instability vs Flexibility

Healthy cognition requires flexibility.

Therefore PIF must distinguish:

Adaptive precision adjustment

from

Maladaptive precision instability.

The key question is whether changes in precision remain appropriately
coupled to environmental uncertainty.

---

# 18. Evidence Classification

## Relatively Established

- Precision is a standard statistical concept.
- Precision is related mathematically to uncertainty.
- Bayesian inference uses precision/variance concepts.
- Predictive-processing theories incorporate precision-weighted
  prediction errors.

## Strong Theoretical Literature

- Precision-weighted predictive coding.
- Attention and uncertainty.
- Bayesian perceptual inference.
- Predictive-processing accounts of psychosis.

## Active Research

- Neural implementation of precision.
- Precision and attention.
- Precision abnormalities in psychosis.
- Hierarchical precision.
- Sensory versus prior precision.
- Precision across stages of psychosis.

## Proposed Hypothesis

- Precision instability as a cross-domain mechanism.
- Precision Instability Framework (PIF).

---

# 19. Core Scientific Message

Prediction error tells us that something does not match expectation.

Precision tells us how much that discrepancy should matter.

The next question is whether a system can maintain appropriate
precision over time and across changing contexts.
