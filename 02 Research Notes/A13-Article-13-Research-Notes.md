# Article 13 — Research Notes

## What Is Consciousness — and What Is the Self?

### Subtitle
How does experience become conscious and organized from a particular point of view?

---

# 1. Central Research Question

How does experience become something that is not only processed by the brain, but consciously experienced from a particular point of view?

This question has two connected but distinct components:

1. What is consciousness?
2. Why is conscious experience normally organized from a first-person perspective?

The second question leads toward the concept of the self.

# 3. Working Definition of Consciousness

For this article, consciousness will be treated provisionally as the presence of subjective experience.

In simple terms:

There is something it is like to experience something.

Examples include:

- seeing a color
- hearing a sound
- feeling pain
- experiencing an emotion
- remembering an event
- imagining a scene
- experiencing a thought

This is a working scientific/philosophical definition, not a complete theory.

---

# 4. Consciousness Is Not Simply Neural Processing

The brain performs enormous amounts of processing without the contents necessarily becoming conscious.

Therefore:

Neural processing ≠ conscious experience

This distinction is fundamental.

The scientific problem is not merely:

"What does the brain process?"

It is:

"Which processes are associated with conscious experience, under what conditions, and through what mechanisms?"

---

# 5. Consciousness and Wakefulness

Do not equate consciousness with wakefulness.

Wakefulness concerns a broad state of arousal.

Consciousness concerns the presence and character of experience.

Someone can be awake while experiencing very different contents of consciousness.

Conversely, altered states such as dreaming demonstrate that conscious experience can occur without ordinary waking interaction with the external environment.

---

# 6. Consciousness and Awareness

Awareness can refer to several related but distinct concepts.

Examples:

- awareness of a stimulus
- awareness of one's own mental state
- self-awareness
- metacognitive awareness

The article should avoid treating "awareness" as a single precisely defined mechanism.

---

# 7. Consciousness and Attention

Article 9 established:

Attention:

"What receives priority?"

Consciousness:

"What is experienced?"

Attention and consciousness interact, but they are not identical.

Some theories propose strong relationships between attention and conscious access, while empirical work has shown that they can sometimes be experimentally dissociated.

Important guardrail:

Do not define consciousness as attention.

---

# 8. Consciousness and Perception

Perception involves constructing or inferring representations of the environment and body.

Conscious perception concerns when some perceptual content becomes part of subjective experience.

Therefore:

Perception ≠ necessarily conscious perception.

This distinction connects Article 3's perception framework to Article 13.

---

# 9. Conscious and Unconscious Processing

Important examples include:

- subliminal processing
- priming
- automatic motor processes
- implicit memory
- unconscious perceptual processing
- procedural processing

The brain does not need conscious awareness for every useful computation.

This supports the broader principle:

> Consciousness is one mode of information processing, not synonymous with information processing itself.

---

# 10. Access Consciousness

Access consciousness refers to information becoming available for functions such as:

- reasoning
- decision-making
- working memory
- verbal report
- deliberate behavioral control

This concept is especially relevant to Global Workspace theories.

---

# 11. Phenomenal Consciousness

Phenomenal consciousness concerns the subjective character of experience.

Examples:

- what red looks like
- what pain feels like
- what music sounds like
- what anxiety feels like
- what it is like to remember

This is often summarized by the phrase:

"What is it like?"

Important distinction:

Phenomenal consciousness is not simply equivalent to being able to verbally report something.

---

# 12. Global Workspace Theory

Global Workspace theories propose that information becomes conscious when it becomes broadly available to multiple cognitive systems.

Potential consequences include availability to:

- working memory
- reasoning
- decision-making
- language
- voluntary control

Global Neuronal Workspace Theory proposes a corresponding large-scale neural architecture.

Important guardrail:

Global Workspace is a major theory, not an established final explanation of consciousness.

---

# 13. Higher-Order Theories

Higher-Order theories propose that consciousness involves some form of representation of a mental state by another mental state or higher-order representation.

Basic conceptual distinction:

First-order representation:

"There is a visual representation."

Higher-order representation:

"I am consciously seeing."

Different higher-order theories make different claims about the necessary architecture.

Do not reduce Higher-Order theories to simple introspection.

---

# 14. Recurrent Processing

Recurrent Processing Theory emphasizes recurrent interactions rather than only feedforward processing.

The general idea is that conscious experience may depend on recurrent or reentrant processing within neural systems.

This provides an important contrast with theories emphasizing global availability.

---

# 15. Integrated Information Theory

Integrated Information Theory begins from proposed structural properties of conscious experience and attempts to relate them to the causal organization of physical systems.

Important concepts include:

- integration
- differentiation
- information
- unity
- exclusion

IIT differs substantially from Global Workspace and Higher-Order approaches.

Important guardrail:

IIT remains controversial and should be presented as a competing theoretical framework rather than established fact.

---

# 16. Predictive Processing

Predictive processing describes the brain as a system that continually generates predictions and updates them in relation to sensory evidence.

Relevant concepts:

- generative models
- prediction
- prediction error
- priors
- inference
- precision

The relationship between predictive processing and consciousness is an active theoretical area.

Important distinction:

Predictive processing is broader than consciousness.

It should not be presented as a complete established theory of conscious experience.

---

# 17. Major Consciousness Theories

Current major approaches include:

1. Global Workspace / Global Neuronal Workspace
2. Higher-Order theories
3. Recurrent Processing theories
4. Integrated Information Theory
5. Predictive Processing approaches

A major contemporary review emphasizes that these theories differ in what they attempt to explain and how they propose to explain it.

The field has not converged on a single accepted theory.

---

# 18. Theory Comparison

| Theory | Central emphasis |
|---|---|
| Global Workspace | Global availability |
| Higher-Order | Representation of mental states |
| Recurrent Processing | Recurrent neural interactions |
| IIT | Integrated causal/informational structure |
| Predictive Processing | Inference and generative modeling |

These categories simplify complex theoretical traditions.

They should be used as orientation rather than exhaustive descriptions.

---

# 19. Empirical Theory Testing

A mature consciousness science should distinguish:

- finding a neural correlate
- proposing a mechanism
- testing a theory
- distinguishing competing theories

A neural correlate does not automatically establish a theory.

A theory becomes scientifically stronger when it produces predictions that can potentially distinguish it from alternatives.

---

# 20. Adversarial Testing

Recent consciousness research has increasingly emphasized direct comparison between competing theories.

The important methodological question is:

> What observation would support one theory over another?

This is more informative than collecting evidence that can be interpreted as compatible with every theory.

---

# 21. Neural Correlates of Consciousness

Neural correlates of consciousness (NCCs) are neural processes associated with conscious experience.

Important distinction:

NCC ≠ necessarily sufficient cause of consciousness.

Possible relationships include:

- necessary conditions
- sufficient conditions
- enabling conditions
- consequences
- correlated processes

Careful causal inference is therefore essential.

---

# 22. The Hard Problem

The hard problem asks why physical and functional processes are accompanied by subjective experience at all.

Neuroscience can investigate:

- neural activity
- information processing
- behavior
- reports
- brain states
- causal manipulations

But the philosophical question of why these processes should have subjective character remains unresolved.

Do not claim that neuroscience has solved the hard problem.

---

# 23. The Easy Problems

"Easy problems" in consciousness science concern functions that can potentially be explained mechanistically, such as:

- discrimination
- report
- information access
- integration
- attention
- behavioral control

"Easy" does not mean scientifically trivial.

It means they are framed as functional/mechanistic problems rather than the philosophical problem of why experience exists.

---

# 24. Consciousness Is Multi-Level

Consciousness research can be approached at multiple levels:

Phenomenology
↓
Psychology
↓
Computation
↓
Neural dynamics
↓
Cellular mechanisms

The Schizophrenia Research Journey should preserve these distinctions.

---

# 25. From Experience to First-Person Perspective

A conscious experience normally appears from somewhere.

There is:

- a here
- a now
- a perspective
- an embodied point of view

This does not necessarily mean there is a separate internal observer.

Instead, first-person organization may be built into the structure of ordinary experience.

---

# 26. First-Person Perspective

First-person perspective refers to the organization of experience from the subject's point of view.

Examples:

- seeing the world from one's visual perspective
- experiencing one's body from within
- experiencing thoughts as occurring in one's own mental life
- experiencing sensations as belonging to one's body

First-person perspective is related to but not identical with autobiographical identity.

---

# 27. Consciousness Does Not Require an Explicit Narrative Self

A person can consciously:

- see
- hear
- feel
- remember
- imagine

without explicitly thinking about their biography.

Therefore:

Consciousness ≠ narrative self

This is an important conceptual step.

---

# 28. The Self as a Layered Concept

The term "self" can refer to multiple phenomena.

Potential layers include:

1. Minimal/pre-reflective self
2. Bodily self
3. Sense of ownership
4. Sense of agency
5. Reflective self-awareness
6. Autobiographical/narrative self
7. Social self

These layers should not be assumed to represent a universally accepted hierarchy.

---

# 29. Mineness

"Mineness" refers to the implicit sense that an experience is one's own.

For example:

Instead of explicitly thinking:

"This visual experience belongs to me."

ordinary experience simply has a first-person character.

Mineness is therefore often described as pre-reflective.

---

# 30. Pre-Reflective Selfhood

Pre-reflective selfhood means that self-related structure can be present before deliberate reflection on oneself.

It does not require:

- verbal self-description
- autobiographical reasoning
- explicit introspection

This concept becomes central to Article 14.

---

# 31. Minimal Self

The minimal self is a philosophical and phenomenological concept referring to the most basic form of selfhood.

Potential features include:

- first-person perspective
- mineness
- embodiment
- self-presence
- temporal continuity
- agency
- ownership

There is disagreement about exactly which features are fundamental.

Do not treat the minimal self as a universally established scientific entity.

---

# 32. Minimal Self and Consciousness

One important theoretical question is:

> Does consciousness necessarily involve a minimal self?

Some theories propose that conscious experience is inherently first-personal.

Others distinguish consciousness from self-representation more strongly.

This remains an open theoretical issue.

---

# 33. Embodiment

The body contributes to self-experience through:

- interoception
- proprioception
- vestibular signals
- tactile signals
- visual information
- motor signals

The embodied self is therefore not merely a conceptual belief about one's body.

It involves continuous multisensory organization.

---

# 34. Body Ownership

Body ownership concerns the experience that a body or body part belongs to oneself.

Experimental paradigms such as the rubber-hand illusion demonstrate that ownership experiences can be experimentally manipulated.

This is important because it suggests that aspects of self-experience depend on multisensory integration.

---

# 35. Agency

Agency concerns the experience of being the author or cause of an action.

Important distinction:

Ownership:

"This is my body/action."

Agency:

"I caused this action."

They can dissociate.

Article 14 will explore this distinction in depth.

---

# 36. Consciousness, Selfhood, and Agency

A useful conceptual sequence is:

Conscious experience
↓
First-person perspective
↓
Mineness
↓
Embodied self
↓
Agency

This is a conceptual map, not a claim that the brain computes these stages sequentially.

---

# 37. Self-Reference

Higher-level self-related cognition includes:

- thinking about oneself
- autobiographical memory
- self-evaluation
- social self-awareness
- introspection
- narrative identity

These are more reflective than minimal selfhood.

---

# 38. Narrative Self

The narrative self organizes experiences across time.

It can include:

- personal memories
- identity
- values
- relationships
- goals
- life story

This is substantially different from the immediate first-person structure of experience.

---

# 39. Consciousness and Internal Cognition

Conscious experience is not limited to external sensory input.

It can include:

- thoughts
- inner speech
- imagery
- memories
- intentions
- emotions
- bodily sensations

This creates a crucial bridge to schizophrenia research.

---

# 40. Internally Generated Experience

A central later question is:

> How does the brain distinguish internally generated mental events from externally generated events?

Potentially relevant processes include:

- source monitoring
- self-monitoring
- motor prediction
- corollary discharge
- sensory prediction
- agency
- metacognition

These should not yet be collapsed into one mechanism.

---

# 41. Consciousness and Schizophrenia

Article 13 should introduce schizophrenia only after establishing normal consciousness and selfhood.

The key question is not:

"Is schizophrenia a disorder of consciousness?"

Instead:

> Which aspects of conscious self-experience may become altered in schizophrenia?

---

# 42. Self-Disorder Literature

Phenomenological research has proposed that disturbances of basic self-experience may be important in schizophrenia-spectrum conditions.

The basic-self or ipseity-disorder literature describes phenomena including:

- hyperreflexivity
- diminished self-affection/self-presence
- disturbed grip or hold on the experiential world

These are theoretical constructs, not universally accepted diagnostic mechanisms.

---

# 43. Hyperreflexivity

Hyperreflexivity refers, in the relevant phenomenological literature, to excessive attention or awareness directed toward processes that would ordinarily remain implicit.

Important distinction:

Hyperreflexivity ≠ simply "thinking too much."

It refers to a particular alteration in the relationship between experience and awareness.

---

# 44. Diminished Self-Affection

Diminished self-affection or diminished self-presence refers to a weakening or alteration of the normally immediate sense of being a self-present subject.

It should not be simplified to:

"I have no self."

The phenomenological concept is subtler.

---

# 45. Disturbed Grip on the World

Some self-disorder models include altered "grip" or "hold" on the perceptual/cognitive world.

This concerns the way experience is normally organized as a coherent field in which objects, meanings, and one's own position are immediately structured.

---

# 46. Evidence for Self-Disturbance in Schizophrenia

A meta-analysis of 25 studies involving 690 people with schizophrenia and 979 healthy controls reported group differences in basic self-disturbance, including measures related to body ownership, agency, and subjective self-experience.

However:

- the literature is heterogeneous
- methods vary
- self-experience is difficult to measure
- group differences do not establish one causal mechanism

The finding should therefore be treated as evidence supporting further investigation, not as proof of a single schizophrenia mechanism.

---

# 47. Contemporary Self-Disorder Models

Recent reviews describe self-disorders as an active research area.

The revised ipseity-disorder model emphasizes hyperreflexivity as a possible organizing concept while also acknowledging heterogeneity and apparently contradictory self-experiences.

This is a theoretical model, not an established complete explanation of schizophrenia.

---

# 48. Important 2026 Perspective

Recent integrative work distinguishes between:

- minimal/pre-reflective self
- narrative/reflective self

It also emphasizes that the relationship between these levels remains incompletely understood.

This is particularly useful for the article because it prevents the self from being treated as a single unitary construct.

---

# 49. Phenomenology and Neuroscience

Phenomenology asks:

"What does the experience feel like?"

Neuroscience asks:

"What processes accompany or generate it?"

Computational psychiatry asks:

"What computational principles could explain the observed pattern?"

A strong schizophrenia research program should connect these levels rather than replacing one with another.

# 51. Consciousness and Computational Psychiatry

Potential computational questions include:

- How are self-related predictions generated?
- How is sensory evidence weighted?
- How are internal and external causes inferred?
- How is agency inferred?
- How are body and environment modeled?
- How stable are self-related representations?
- How does precision influence self-related inference?

These are future research questions rather than established answers.

# 54. Major Conceptual Distinctions

### Consciousness vs Attention

Experience vs priority.

### Consciousness vs Perception

Experience vs perceptual processing.

### Consciousness vs Cognition

Experience vs broader information processing.

### Consciousness vs Self

Subjective experience vs first-person organization/selfhood.

### Minimal Self vs Narrative Self

Pre-reflective subjectivity vs autobiographical identity.

### Ownership vs Agency

"This is mine" vs "I caused it."

---

# 55. Scientific Guardrails

Do not claim:

1. Consciousness has one universally accepted mechanism.
2. Global Workspace Theory is proven.
3. IIT is proven.
4. Higher-Order Theory is proven.
5. Predictive Processing explains consciousness completely.
6. Consciousness equals attention.
7. Consciousness equals working memory.
8. Consciousness equals reportability.
9. Consciousness equals selfhood.
10. The self is located in one brain region.
11. Schizophrenia is simply a consciousness disorder.
12. Self-disorder is the sole cause of schizophrenia.
13. Phenomenological reports directly reveal neural mechanisms.
14. Neural correlates automatically establish causality.
15. Minimal selfhood is a single universally defined construct.

---

# 56. Core Scientific Message

Consciousness concerns subjective experience.

But conscious experience normally has structure:

It happens somewhere.

It happens now.

It happens from a perspective.

It feels like it is happening to someone.

The final step in this sequence introduces the self.

# 60. Final Research Position

Article 13 should not attempt to solve consciousness.

Its purpose is to establish a scientifically disciplined conceptual foundation.

The central position is:

> Consciousness is a multidimensional scientific and philosophical problem involving subjective experience, access, neural dynamics, and first-person organization. No single theory currently provides universally accepted closure. Understanding the self requires going one step further: explaining how conscious experience is normally organized as occurring from a particular embodied point of view.

This provides the foundation for Article 14:

**What Is the Minimal Self — and What Is Agency?**
