# Knowledge Base — Precision

## Concept

**Precision**

---

## Core Definition

Precision is a measure of the reliability or certainty associated with
an estimate or signal.

In statistics, precision is commonly represented as the inverse of
variance:

Precision = 1 / Variance

Higher precision corresponds to lower variance and therefore lower
uncertainty.

Lower precision corresponds to higher variance and greater uncertainty.

---

## Precision vs Accuracy

Precision and accuracy are different concepts.

**Accuracy** refers to closeness to the true value.

**Precision** refers to consistency or uncertainty.

A measurement can therefore be:

- accurate and precise;
- accurate but imprecise;
- precise but inaccurate;
- neither accurate nor precise.

This distinction is important when discussing beliefs and inference.

A highly precise belief is not necessarily a true belief.

---

## Precision in Bayesian Inference

In Bayesian inference, information from different sources is combined
according to their relative uncertainty.

A simplified representation is:

Prior + Evidence → Posterior

When one source has greater precision than another, it generally exerts
greater influence on the resulting estimate, all else being equal.

Therefore:

**Precision determines how strongly information can influence
inference.**

---

## Precision and Uncertainty

Precision is closely related to uncertainty.

Conceptually:

High precision → low uncertainty

Low precision → high uncertainty

However, precision should not be treated as identical to confidence in
the everyday psychological sense.

Its meaning depends on the computational/statistical model being used.

---

## Precision-Weighted Prediction Error

Predictive-processing theories frequently describe prediction errors in
precision-weighted form.

Conceptually:

Precision × Prediction Error

This means that the estimated reliability of a prediction error can
affect how strongly it influences inference and updating.

This is a computational formulation, not a claim that the brain
necessarily performs a literal multiplication operation at one
anatomical location.

---

## Precision and Predictive Processing

Within predictive-processing frameworks, the brain is modeled as
performing hierarchical inference.

Higher levels generate predictions.

Lower levels provide prediction-error information.

Precision determines the effective influence of prediction errors on the
inference process.

Simplified:

Prediction
↓
Sensory comparison
↓
Prediction error
↓
Precision weighting
↓
Inference/update

---

## Precision and Attention

Some predictive-processing theories propose that attention can be
understood partly in terms of estimating, allocating, or modulating
precision.

Important distinction:

**Attention ≠ precision**

Rather, attention may alter the effective precision assigned to
particular information.

---

## Relative Precision

The computational importance of precision is often relative.

The question is not simply:

"How much precision is there?"

It may instead be:

"Which source of information has greater precision relative to its
competitors?"

For example:

Strong prior + weak sensory evidence

can produce a different inference from:

Weak prior + strong sensory evidence.

---

## Hierarchical Precision

Precision may differ across levels of hierarchical inference.

Potential levels include:

- low-level sensory representations;
- perceptual representations;
- contextual representations;
- conceptual representations;
- beliefs.

An alteration at one level does not necessarily imply the same
alteration at every other level.

---

## Precision and Psychosis

Predictive-processing accounts of psychosis have proposed that altered
precision weighting may contribute to some psychotic experiences.

Potential mechanisms include:

- altered sensory precision;
- altered prior precision;
- altered prediction-error precision;
- altered hierarchical weighting;
- altered uncertainty estimation.

These remain research hypotheses.

---

## Precision and Hallucinations

Possible predictive-processing interpretations include altered weighting
between sensory evidence and internally generated predictions.

Potential mechanisms include:

- excessive influence of internally generated predictions;
- altered sensory prediction-error precision;
- altered sensory attenuation;
- hierarchical inference abnormalities.

Do not equate hallucinations directly with high precision.

---

## Precision and Delusions

Possible computational mechanisms include:

- altered prior precision;
- altered sensory precision;
- altered learning rates;
- altered prediction-error signaling;
- abnormal belief updating;
- hierarchical inference abnormalities.

Delusions should not be reduced to a single precision abnormality.

---

## Competing Top-Down and Bottom-Up Accounts

There is no established universal direction of precision abnormality in
psychosis.

Possible top-down accounts involve:

- overly precise priors;
- insufficiently precise sensory evidence;
- excessive influence of prior expectations.

Possible bottom-up accounts involve:

- imprecise priors;
- excessively precise sensory evidence;
- excessive influence of sensory prediction errors.

Different symptoms, stages, contexts, and hierarchical levels may
potentially involve different computational alterations.

---

## Dynamic Precision

Precision should not necessarily be treated as fixed.

Precision estimates may change according to:

- sensory reliability;
- environmental volatility;
- context;
- attention;
- task demands;
- internal state.

Therefore precision has both a level and potentially a temporal
dynamics.

---

## Precision Variability

Precision can vary without being pathological.

Adaptive systems should change precision when environmental reliability
changes.

Therefore:

**Precision variability ≠ pathological instability**

---

## Precision Instability

### Proposed Concept

Precision instability refers, within the proposed PIF framework, to
excessive, inappropriate, or poorly regulated variation in effective
precision weighting over time, context, or domain.

This is a proposed research concept.

It is not an established clinical feature or validated mechanism of
schizophrenia.

---

## Precision Instability vs Flexibility

Healthy cognition requires adaptive flexibility.

Therefore PIF should distinguish:

**Adaptive precision adjustment**

from:

**Maladaptive precision instability**

A central research question is whether changes in precision remain
appropriately coupled to environmental uncertainty.

---

## Precision Instability vs Noise

Precision instability should not simply mean random neural noise.

A scientifically useful operationalization would require measurable
temporal or contextual variability in precision-related parameters
beyond what would be expected from:

- measurement noise;
- task variability;
- ordinary adaptive flexibility.

---

## PIF Relationship

The Precision Instability Framework proposes:

Uncertainty
↓
Precision
↓
Precision weighting
↓
Prediction-error influence
↓
Inference
↓
Experience

PIF adds the possibility that instability in precision maintenance or
allocation could contribute to instability in inference.

Potential domains include:

- perception;
- attention;
- salience;
- motivation;
- belief updating;
- agency.

---

## PIF Evidence Status

**Status: Proposed research hypothesis**

PIF should not be presented as an established scientific theory or
validated explanation of schizophrenia.

---

## Evidence Hierarchy

### Established Concepts

- Precision is a statistical concept.
- Precision is related mathematically to variance.
- Precision is related to uncertainty.
- Bayesian inference can represent uncertainty using precision.
- Predictive-processing models can incorporate precision-weighted
  prediction errors.

### Active Research

- Neural implementation of precision.
- Relationship between attention and precision.
- Precision in hierarchical cortical processing.
- Precision abnormalities in psychosis.
- Relative weighting of sensory evidence and prior beliefs.
- Temporal dynamics of precision.

### Proposed

- Precision instability as a cross-domain computational mechanism.
- Precision Instability Framework (PIF).

---

## Key Relationships

### Precision ↔ Variance

Precision is inversely related to variance.

### Precision ↔ Uncertainty

Higher precision generally corresponds to lower uncertainty.

### Precision ↔ Prediction Error

Precision determines how strongly a prediction error may influence
inference in predictive-processing formulations.

### Precision ↔ Attention

Some theories connect attention with modulation or allocation of
precision.

### Precision ↔ Bayesian Inference

Relative precision influences the weighting of competing information
sources.

### Precision ↔ Psychosis

Altered precision weighting is one proposed computational mechanism in
theories of psychosis.

### Precision ↔ PIF

PIF proposes that the temporal stability and regulation of precision may
be important variables for investigation.

## Important Caveats

Do not state:

"Schizophrenia is caused by abnormal precision."

Do not state:

"People with schizophrenia have too much precision."

Do not state:

"Hallucinations are caused by high precision."

Do not state:

"Delusions are caused by high prior precision."

Prefer:

"Predictive-processing theories propose that altered precision
weighting may contribute to some aspects of psychosis."

And:

"The direction, location, timescale, and computational meaning of altered
precision remain active research questions."

---

## Future Research Questions

1. Can precision-related computational parameters be estimated reliably
   in humans?

2. Do people with schizophrenia show altered precision weighting?

3. Does precision differ across sensory, cognitive, and motivational
   domains?

4. Does precision fluctuate more over time in schizophrenia?

5. Can temporal variability in precision be distinguished from ordinary
   neural noise?

6. Does precision instability correlate with symptom dimensions?

7. Does precision instability vary with context or environmental
   uncertainty?

8. Can computational measures of precision predict symptom changes?

9. Can EEG/MEG provide temporal proxies for precision-related
   processing?

10. Can fMRI reveal network-level correlates of dynamic precision?
