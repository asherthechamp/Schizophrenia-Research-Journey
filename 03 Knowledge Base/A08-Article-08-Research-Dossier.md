# Article 08 — Research Dossier

## What Is Salience—and What Happens When It Goes Wrong?

### Series

The Schizophrenia Research Journey

### Article Number

08

### Research Focus

Salience, incentive salience, dopamine, aberrant salience, predictive
processing, large-scale brain networks, psychosis, and schizophrenia.

---

# 1. Executive Summary

Salience is a broad concept referring to the extent to which information
stands out, attracts processing resources, becomes behaviorally relevant,
or acquires motivational importance.

The concept is scientifically complicated because the word "salience" is
used at several levels of explanation.

It may refer to:

- perceptual prominence;
- attentional capture;
- behavioral relevance;
- motivational significance;
- incentive value;
- personal significance;
- large-scale neural network organization.

These should not be treated as identical processes.

One of the most influential applications of salience to psychosis research
is Shitij Kapur's aberrant salience hypothesis. The hypothesis proposes
that dysregulated dopamine signaling may contribute to the inappropriate
assignment of significance to otherwise ordinary events.

The subjective experience of unusual significance may then create a need
for explanation. Delusional beliefs may, in some cases, emerge partly as
attempts to interpret and explain these unusual experiences.

However, aberrant salience is not a complete explanation of schizophrenia.

Schizophrenia is heterogeneous and involves multiple domains, including:

- psychosis;
- cognition;
- motivation;
- social functioning;
- perception;
- learning;
- large-scale brain dynamics.

The relationship between dopamine, prediction errors, precision, salience,
attention, meaning, and belief remains an active area of research.

---

# 2. The Central Scientific Problem

The brain receives far more information than it can process equally.

It must prioritize.

At every moment, an organism must determine:

- What should be noticed?
- What should be ignored?
- What is surprising?
- What is important?
- What is motivationally relevant?
- What requires action?
- What should change existing beliefs?

Salience is one of the concepts used to describe aspects of this
prioritization problem.

However, there is no single universal definition of salience that covers
all scientific contexts.

---

# 3. Different Forms of Salience

## 3.1 Perceptual Salience

Perceptual salience concerns features that make a stimulus stand out
against its surroundings.

Examples include:

- brightness;
- contrast;
- movement;
- sudden onset;
- novelty;
- loudness.

A bright object against a dark background may be perceptually salient.

This form of salience can influence attention without necessarily
involving personal meaning or motivation.

---

## 3.2 Attentional Salience

Attentional salience concerns the ability of information to capture or
direct attention.

A sudden loud sound may immediately capture attention.

However:

Attention ≠ Salience

Attention can also be voluntarily directed toward information that is
not naturally prominent.

For example, a scientist may deliberately examine a subtle pattern in a
dataset that would not automatically capture attention.

---

## 3.3 Motivational Salience

Motivational salience concerns the importance of information relative to
needs, goals, and behavioral priorities.

Food may become highly salient when hungry.

Water may become highly salient when thirsty.

A threat may become highly salient when safety is at risk.

Motivational salience therefore depends partly on internal state and
context.

---

## 3.4 Incentive Salience

Incentive salience refers to the process through which cues associated
with rewards acquire motivational properties.

A cue can become capable of:

- attracting attention;
- motivating approach;
- triggering desire;
- energizing behavior.

This concept is particularly associated with research by Kent Berridge
and Terry Robinson.

A major distinction in this framework is:

Liking

versus

Wanting.

---

# 4. Incentive Salience: Wanting Is Not the Same as Liking

Berridge and Robinson emphasized an important distinction between:

## Liking

The hedonic experience of pleasure.

## Wanting

The motivational drive or incentive value associated with obtaining
something.

These processes can be dissociated.

An organism may strongly pursue something without experiencing a
proportionally increased hedonic response.

This distinction became particularly important in addiction research,
where drug-related cues can become extremely powerful motivational
triggers.

The incentive salience framework demonstrates that motivational
importance cannot simply be reduced to pleasure.

---

# 5. Why Dopamine Cannot Be Reduced to a "Pleasure Chemical"

Popular descriptions often call dopamine:

"The pleasure chemical."

This is misleading.

Dopamine participates in multiple functions, including:

- reinforcement learning;
- reward processing;
- motivation;
- action selection;
- movement;
- cognitive processes.

Different dopamine systems can perform different functions.

Dopamine signaling also varies according to:

- anatomical pathway;
- receptor system;
- timing;
- behavioral context;
- task demands.

Therefore:

Dopamine ≠ Pleasure

and:

Dopamine ≠ Salience

in any simple universal sense.

---

# 6. Dopamine and Reward Prediction Errors

Influential computational neuroscience research has linked some dopamine
activity to reward prediction errors.

A reward prediction error represents the difference between expected and
received reward.

Conceptually:

Reward Prediction Error

=

Received Reward

−

Expected Reward

A positive prediction error occurs when an outcome is better than
expected.

A negative prediction error occurs when an outcome is worse than
expected.

Unexpected rewards can therefore produce positive reward prediction
errors.

The absence of an expected reward can produce negative reward prediction
errors.

These ideas became foundational in computational reinforcement learning.

---

# 7. Important Limitation: Dopamine Neurons Are Heterogeneous

The relationship between dopamine and reward prediction errors should
not be treated as a complete description of all dopamine activity.

Dopamine neurons are heterogeneous.

Different dopamine pathways and populations may carry different kinds of
information.

Dopamine can participate in:

- movement;
- motivation;
- novelty responses;
- aversive processing;
- learning;
- behavioral activation.

Therefore, the statement:

"Dopamine neurons encode reward prediction error"

is useful as a description of an influential research framework but is
too broad if interpreted as applying identically to every dopamine neuron
under every condition.

---

# 8. Dopamine and Incentive Salience

The incentive salience framework proposes an important role for dopamine
in the motivational properties of reward-associated cues.

Dopamine may contribute to transforming a cue into something that is:

- attractive;
- motivationally powerful;
- capable of triggering approach behavior;
- capable of generating "wanting."

This differs from saying that dopamine simply creates pleasure.

The distinction between reward learning, motivation, and hedonic pleasure
is essential.

---

# 9. Aberrant Salience

Aberrant salience refers to the inappropriate assignment of significance
or importance.

An event that would normally be ignored may begin to feel:

- unusually important;
- personally relevant;
- meaningful;
- significant;
- connected to the self.

This concept became highly influential in attempts to connect dopamine
dysregulation with the subjective experience of psychosis.

---

# 10. Kapur's Aberrant Salience Hypothesis

Shitij Kapur proposed one of the most influential theoretical frameworks
connecting dopamine dysregulation with psychosis.

The simplified conceptual model is:

Dysregulated Dopamine Signaling
↓
Inappropriate Assignment of Salience
↓
Ordinary Events Feel Unusually Significant
↓
The Person Attempts to Explain the Experience
↓
Meaning-Making
↓
Possible Delusional Belief Formation

The important contribution of this hypothesis is that it attempts to
bridge:

Neurobiology

with

Subjective Experience.

Rather than proposing that a delusion simply appears as an isolated false
belief, the framework asks whether unusual experiences of significance
may create a psychological need for explanation.

---

# 11. From Experience to Meaning

The aberrant salience framework suggests a possible process:

Ordinary Event
↓
Unexpected Subjective Significance
↓
Attention
↓
Interpretation
↓
Meaning-Making
↓
Belief Formation

This should not be interpreted as a fixed universal sequence.

Meaning-making involves many interacting factors, including:

- prior beliefs;
- culture;
- social context;
- language;
- memory;
- emotion;
- reasoning;
- interpersonal experience.

Therefore:

Aberrant Salience ≠ Delusion

A more careful formulation is:

Aberrant Salience

+

Interpretation

+

Prior Knowledge

+

Context

+

Cognitive Processes

↓

Possible Contribution to Delusional Belief Formation

---

# 12. Delusions Are Not Explained by One Mechanism

Delusions are heterogeneous.

Different people may arrive at unusual beliefs through different
combinations of:

- anomalous experiences;
- unusual perceptual events;
- altered inference;
- social experiences;
- emotional states;
- cognitive biases;
- cultural interpretations.

Aberrant salience may therefore represent one possible contributing
mechanism rather than a universal explanation.

---

# 13. Prediction Error and Salience

Prediction error and salience are related concepts but are not identical.

Prediction error asks:

> How different is the observation from what was expected?

Salience asks, broadly:

> How much priority or importance should this information receive?

A surprising event can be highly salient.

But not every surprising event is important.

Likewise, something can be highly important without being surprising.

Therefore:

Prediction Error ≠ Salience

---

# 14. Precision and Salience

Precision, particularly in predictive-processing frameworks, concerns the
estimated reliability or confidence associated with information.

A simplified conceptual relationship is:

Prediction Error
×
Precision
↓
Influence on Belief Updating

Salience may interact with this process.

Information that receives greater computational weighting may exert
greater influence.

However:

Precision ≠ Salience

Precision refers primarily to estimated reliability or weighting.

Salience refers more broadly to prominence, priority, or importance.

The relationship between these constructs remains theoretically complex.

---

# 15. Predictive Processing and Aberrant Salience

Predictive-processing frameworks and aberrant-salience frameworks both
attempt to explain how information influences perception and belief.

Predictive processing emphasizes:

- predictions;
- prediction errors;
- precision weighting;
- hierarchical inference;
- belief updating.

Aberrant salience emphasizes:

- inappropriate significance;
- dopamine dysregulation;
- subjective importance;
- meaning-making.

Possible conceptual overlap exists.

For example:

Unexpected Observation
↓
Prediction Error
↓
Precision Weighting
↓
Influence on Inference
↓
Attention and Processing Priority

However, these theories should not be treated as identical.

The precise relationship between:

- prediction error;
- precision;
- attention;
- salience;
- dopamine;

remains an important research question.

---

# 16. Attention and Salience

Attention is the selective allocation of limited processing resources.

Salience can influence attention.

But attention can also be voluntarily directed.

Therefore:

Salience → may influence attention

Attention → may influence what becomes behaviorally important

The relationship is bidirectional and context-dependent.

A useful distinction is:

Salience:

What stands out or receives priority?

Attention:

Where are processing resources allocated?

These processes overlap but are not identical.

---

# 17. The Salience Network

Neuroscience research identifies a large-scale brain network commonly
called the salience network.

Key regions often include:

- anterior insula;
- dorsal anterior cingulate cortex.

The network has been associated with:

- detecting behaviorally relevant information;
- integrating internal and external signals;
- coordinating responses;
- allocating processing resources;
- switching between large-scale networks.

However, the existence of a "salience network" does not mean that all
forms of psychological salience are produced by one brain network.

---

# 18. The Anterior Insula

The anterior insula has been associated with:

- interoception;
- subjective awareness;
- emotional processing;
- integration of internal bodily signals;
- detection of behaviorally relevant events.

It is often discussed as a major node of the salience network.

However, assigning a single psychological function to the anterior insula
would be misleading.

Brain regions participate in multiple functions depending on network and
task context.

---

# 19. The Dorsal Anterior Cingulate Cortex

The dorsal anterior cingulate cortex is another important region
associated with salience-related network function.

It has been linked to processes involving:

- cognitive control;
- performance monitoring;
- behavioral adjustment;
- action selection;
- processing of relevant information.

Like the anterior insula, it should not be treated as a single-purpose
brain region.

---

# 20. The Triple-Network Model

The salience network is often discussed together with:

- the default mode network;
- the central executive network.

The triple-network model proposes that interactions among these
large-scale networks are important for cognition and psychiatric
disorders.

In simplified terms:

Default Mode Network
→ internally oriented processing

Central Executive Network
→ cognitive control and goal-directed processing

Salience Network
→ detection and coordination of behaviorally relevant information

The salience network has been proposed to participate in switching
between internally and externally oriented modes of processing.

This framework has been influential but should not be treated as a final
or complete model of psychiatric illness.

---

# 21. Salience Network Research in Schizophrenia

Research has investigated possible alterations in:

- salience-network connectivity;
- anterior insula activity;
- anterior cingulate function;
- interactions among large-scale networks.

Potential consequences may involve:

- altered attentional control;
- unusual processing of relevant information;
- cognitive dysfunction;
- altered integration of internal and external signals.

However, findings across schizophrenia research are heterogeneous.

A network-level difference does not automatically correspond to one
specific subjective experience.

---

# 22. Dopamine and Psychosis

Dopamine has a central role in major biological models of psychosis.

Evidence supporting dopamine involvement includes:

- the effects of dopamine-blocking antipsychotic medications;
- the psychosis-inducing potential of some dopamine-enhancing drugs;
- molecular imaging evidence involving dopamine systems.

However, dopamine models do not explain every feature of schizophrenia.

Schizophrenia also involves research on:

- glutamate;
- GABA;
- neural development;
- cognition;
- brain networks;
- environmental factors.

A modern account should therefore avoid reducing schizophrenia to:

"Too much dopamine."

---

# 23. Aberrant Salience and Positive Symptoms

Aberrant salience has primarily been discussed in relation to positive
psychotic symptoms.

These include:

- delusions;
- hallucinations;
- unusual beliefs;
- altered experiences of personal significance.

The hypothesis proposes that ordinary events may acquire unusual
importance or relevance.

However, positive symptoms themselves are heterogeneous.

Different mechanisms may contribute to different symptom profiles.

---

# 24. Salience and Hallucinations

The relationship between aberrant salience and hallucinations is more
complex than a simple one-to-one mechanism.

Hallucinations involve:

- perception;
- sensory processing;
- memory;
- prediction;
- source monitoring;
- attention.

Some theoretical frameworks propose that abnormal weighting of internally
generated information may contribute to hallucination-like experiences.

However, aberrant salience alone should not be presented as a complete
explanation of hallucinations.

---

# 25. Salience and Negative Symptoms

Salience-related concepts may also be relevant to negative symptoms.

Motivation is necessary for:

- pursuing goals;
- initiating behavior;
- allocating effort;
- responding to incentives.

Schizophrenia can involve difficulties in:

- motivation;
- goal-directed behavior;
- effort allocation;
- reward anticipation.

These processes may involve different forms of motivational dysfunction
than those proposed in aberrant-salience models of psychosis.

A person may theoretically experience:

- unusual significance attached to some events;

while also experiencing:

- reduced motivation toward ordinary goals.

This illustrates the complexity of schizophrenia.

---

# 26. Key Conceptual Map

The following concepts should remain distinct:

## Prediction Error

What differs from expectation?

## Precision

How reliable or influential is the information believed to be?

## Uncertainty

How unsure is the system about its current model?

## Volatility

How rapidly is the environment believed to be changing?

## Learning Rate

How strongly should beliefs be updated?

## Salience

How much priority or importance does information receive?

## Attention

Where are processing resources directed?

## Incentive Salience

How strongly does a cue motivate approach or wanting?

## Meaning

How is an event interpreted?

## Belief

What conclusion is formed about reality?

These processes interact but should not be collapsed into one mechanism.

# 28. PIF Connection

The Precision Instability Framework is a proposed research hypothesis.

A possible conceptual extension is:

Instability in Precision Regulation
↓
Instability in Prediction-Error Weighting
↓
Variable Influence of Sensory Evidence
↓
Inconsistent Processing Priority
↓
Potential Instability in Salience Assignment
↓
Altered Attention and Belief Updating

This is not an established explanation of schizophrenia.

It is a proposed research direction.

---

# 29. Potential PIF Predictions

If temporal instability in precision regulation contributes to unstable
salience assignment, possible empirical predictions might include:

- increased trial-to-trial variability in responses to relevant events;
- unstable relationships between surprise and attentional allocation;
- inconsistent significance assigned to similar events;
- altered temporal coupling between uncertainty and attention;
- dynamic measures that provide information beyond average learning
  parameters.

These predictions require:

- operational definitions;
- computational models;
- behavioral experiments;
- neural measurements;
- replication.

---

# 30. Possible Experimental Paradigm

A useful experiment could include several categories of stimuli:

## Predictable and Relevant

Expected information that matters.

## Predictable and Irrelevant

Expected information with little behavioral importance.

## Surprising and Relevant

Unexpected information requiring potential action.

## Surprising and Irrelevant

Unexpected information that does not matter.

## Motivationally Valuable

Cues associated with reward or goals.

Participants could be measured on:

- reaction time;
- attention;
- choices;
- subjective significance;
- learning rate;
- belief updating.

The goal would be to separate:

Surprise

from

Importance.

---

# 31. Potential Computational Approach

A computational model could estimate:

- prediction errors;
- uncertainty;
- volatility;
- learning rates;
- precision weighting.

Behavioral measures could then test whether these quantities predict:

- attentional allocation;
- subjective significance;
- decision-making.

A central question would be:

Do average computational parameters adequately describe the system?

Or:

Does temporal instability contain additional information?

This question connects directly to the PIF research direction.

---

Precision is not identical to salience.

# 35. Core Message for the Article

The brain must continuously decide what deserves priority.

This requires distinguishing:

- what is noticeable;
- what is surprising;
- what is reliable;
- what is important;
- what is motivationally valuable.

Salience is part of this prioritization problem.

The aberrant salience hypothesis provides an influential way of thinking
about how ordinary events may acquire unusual significance in psychosis.

But unusual significance alone does not explain schizophrenia.

The deeper scientific challenge is understanding how:

Prediction
+
Prediction Error
+
Precision
+
Uncertainty
+
Learning
+
Attention
+
Motivation
+
Meaning

interact across time to produce human experience.
