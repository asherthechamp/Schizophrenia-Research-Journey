# 1. Core Definition

## Attention

Attention refers to a family of cognitive and neural processes involved in the selective prioritization of information, representations, locations, objects, features, thoughts, or actions.

A useful working definition is:

> **Attention is the selective prioritization of information for enhanced processing.**

Attention is not best understood as one single mental faculty or one single brain mechanism.

Instead, the term covers multiple partially distinguishable processes involved in:

- Selection
- Prioritization
- Enhancement
- Maintenance
- Reorientation
- Control

---

# 2. The Fundamental Problem Attention Solves

An organism encounters more potentially relevant information than it can treat equally.

At any moment, information may come from:

- Vision
- Hearing
- Touch
- Internal bodily signals
- Thoughts
- Memories
- Emotions
- Social signals

The cognitive problem is therefore:

> **What should receive priority right now?**

Attention helps solve this problem by selectively enhancing some information relative to competing information.

---

# 3. Attention Is Not a Single Thing

The term "attention" covers several different processes.

Important forms include:

- Selective attention
- Sustained attention
- Spatial attention
- Feature-based attention
- Voluntary attention
- Involuntary attention
- Goal-directed attention
- Stimulus-driven attention

These processes overlap but should not automatically be treated as identical.

---

# 4. Selective Attention

## Definition

Selective attention is the prioritization of some information while competing information receives less processing priority.

Examples include:

- Listening to one conversation in a noisy environment.
- Searching for a familiar face in a crowd.
- Reading while ignoring distractions.

Selective attention does not necessarily mean that unattended information is completely eliminated.

Unattended information may still influence processing under some conditions.

Therefore:

> **Selection ≠ Complete Exclusion**

---

# 5. Sustained Attention

## Definition

Sustained attention refers to the ability to maintain attentional engagement over time.

It is important for:

- Vigilance
- Monitoring
- Long-duration tasks
- Detecting infrequent events

Sustained attention should be distinguished from selective attention.

A person may successfully select the correct information at one moment while having difficulty maintaining performance over a prolonged period.

---

# 6. Spatial Attention

## Definition

Spatial attention is the prioritization of information from a particular location in space.

Example:

A person expecting a car to emerge from the left side of the road may allocate attention toward that location.

Spatial attention can influence processing before the expected object appears.

---

# 7. Feature-Based Attention

## Definition

Feature-based attention prioritizes particular properties of information.

Examples include:

- Color
- Motion
- Shape
- Sound characteristics

A person searching for a red object may give greater priority to red features across the visual environment.

---

# 8. Top-Down Attention

## Definition

Top-down attention is influenced by internal factors such as:

- Goals
- Expectations
- Intentions
- Knowledge
- Task demands

Example:

A person searching for their friend in a crowd directs attention according to their goal.

Top-down attention is often described as:

> **Goal-directed attention**

or:

> **Endogenous attention**

---

# 9. Bottom-Up Attention

## Definition

Bottom-up attention is influenced by incoming sensory events and properties of stimuli.

Examples include:

- Sudden sounds
- Sudden movement
- Abrupt changes
- Novel events

Bottom-up attention is often described as:

> **Stimulus-driven attention**

or:

> **Exogenous attention**

---

# 10. Important Qualification: Top-Down vs Bottom-Up

The distinction is useful but should not be oversimplified.

A simple model such as:

> Top-down = completely voluntary

and:

> Bottom-up = completely automatic

is too simplistic.

Attentional capture can depend on:

- Context
- Goals
- Expectations
- Learning
- Emotional relevance

Therefore:

> **Attentional control emerges from interactions between internal goals and external events.**

---

# 11. Voluntary Attention

## Definition

Voluntary attention refers to attention intentionally directed according to goals or intentions.

Example:

> "I am going to focus on reading this page."

Voluntary control is important but limited.

People cannot completely control where attention goes.

---

# 12. Involuntary Attention

## Definition

Involuntary attention occurs when attention is captured or redirected by events.

Examples include:

- A sudden loud sound
- Unexpected movement
- A potentially threatening event

This illustrates that attention is not completely under conscious voluntary control.

---

# 13. Attentional Capture

## Definition

Attentional capture refers to the redirection of attention by a stimulus or event.

Attentional capture may be influenced by:

- Novelty
- Sudden change
- Sensory prominence
- Behavioral relevance
- Context

The exact conditions under which stimuli capture attention remain an active area of research.

---

# 14. Attention vs Salience

## Core Distinction

> **Salience concerns why something stands out as significant, relevant, or important.**

> **Attention concerns selective prioritization for processing.**

These processes interact but are not identical.

---

## Relationship

A salient event may influence attentional allocation.

For example:

> A sudden loud sound may become salient and capture attention.

However, attention can also be voluntarily directed toward information that is not naturally salient.

Example:

> A scientist carefully examining a subtle pattern.

Therefore:

> **Salience can influence attention.**

But:

> **Salience ≠ Attention**

---

# 15. Attention vs Consciousness

## Core Distinction

Attention and consciousness frequently occur together but should not be treated as identical.

A useful conceptual distinction is:

> **Attention concerns prioritization.**

> **Consciousness concerns subjective experience and awareness.**

Attention can strongly influence conscious experience.

However, evidence from experimental psychology and neuroscience indicates that attention and awareness can sometimes be dissociated.

Therefore:

> **Attention ≠ Consciousness**

The precise relationship between attention and consciousness remains an important scientific and philosophical question.

---

# 16. Attention vs Perception

## Core Distinction

Perception concerns the interpretation and experience of sensory information.

Attention concerns which information receives processing priority.

Attention can influence perception.

But:

> **Attention ≠ Perception**

---

# 17. Attention vs Prediction Error

## Core Distinction

Prediction error refers to a mismatch between:

- What was predicted

and:

- What was observed

Attention refers to prioritization.

Unexpected events may influence attention.

But:

> **Prediction Error ≠ Attention**

---

# 18. Attention vs Precision

## Core Distinction

Precision refers broadly to expected reliability, confidence, or inverse uncertainty within probabilistic and computational frameworks.

Attention refers to selective prioritization.

Some predictive-processing theories propose a relationship between attention and the precision weighting of prediction errors.

However:

> **Attention is not universally defined as precision.**

Therefore:

> **Attention ≠ Precision**

But:

> **Attention may be modeled partly through precision weighting within particular computational frameworks.**

---

# 19. Attention and Predictive Processing

Within predictive-processing frameworks, the brain continuously generates predictions and compares them with incoming information.

Some prediction errors may receive greater influence during updating.

One theoretical proposal is that attention can modulate:

> **The expected precision of particular prediction errors.**

In this framework, attentional allocation may influence which information has greater impact on the system.

Scientific status:

> **Theoretical and computational framework.**

It should not be treated as a universally accepted complete explanation of attention.

# 21. Attention as a Priority System

A useful conceptual model is that multiple factors contribute to attentional priority.

Possible influences include:

- Goals
- Expectations
- Sensory events
- Novelty
- Salience
- Motivation
- Emotional relevance
- Context

The resulting question is:

> **What deserves priority right now?**

This can be described conceptually as a dynamic priority system.

---

# 22. Attention and Brain Systems

Attention does not appear to depend on one isolated brain location.

Instead, attentional functions involve coordinated activity across distributed systems.

Important research areas include interactions involving:

- Frontal systems
- Parietal systems
- Sensory systems
- Subcortical systems

Different attentional functions may involve partially overlapping neural systems.

Therefore:

> **There is no single universal "attention center" in the brain.**

---

# 23. Attention and Schizophrenia

Attention is an important domain of cognitive research in schizophrenia.

Research has investigated:

- Alertness
- Sustained attention
- Selective attention
- Attentional control
- Filtering
- Distractibility

However, the term:

> "Attention deficit"

is often too broad.

A better scientific question is:

> **Which specific attentional processes are altered, under which conditions, and in which individuals?**

---

# 24. Sustained Attention in Schizophrenia

Research has often reported difficulties in sustained attention in schizophrenia.

However, findings depend partly on:

- The task used
- The measure used
- Participant characteristics
- Whether overall performance or change over time is measured

Therefore:

> **Sustained attention abnormalities should not be described as a single simple deficit.**

---

# 25. Selective Attention in Schizophrenia

Research has found evidence of differences in selective attention and related processes in schizophrenia.

However, findings can vary depending on:

- Sensory modality
- Task design
- Clinical state
- Illness characteristics
- Cognitive demands

Some contemporary research has also introduced the concept of:

> **Hyperfocusing**

This refers to unusually narrow and intense allocation of processing resources.

This creates an important scientific complication:

Schizophrenia may not always involve simply:

> "Too little attention."

In some circumstances, the problem may involve:

> **Attention directed too narrowly or toward inappropriate information.**

---

# 26. Hyperfocusing

## Definition

Hyperfocusing refers to unusually narrow and intense concentration of processing resources.

In schizophrenia research, hyperfocusing has been proposed as one possible explanation for apparently conflicting findings involving attention.

A person may show:

- Intense focus

while also having:

- Difficulty directing that focus appropriately

This distinction is important.

The problem may sometimes involve:

> **Where attention is directed**

rather than simply:

> **How much attention exists.**

Scientific status:

> Important research hypothesis and framework.

Not a complete explanation of attentional dysfunction in schizophrenia.

---

# 27. Attention and Psychosis

Attention may interact with psychotic experience through several pathways.

A conceptual sequence might be:

> **Unexpected event**

↓

> **Increased salience**

↓

> **Attentional capture**

↓

> **Search for explanation**

↓

> **Meaning-making**

This should be treated as:

> **A conceptual model**

rather than a complete established causal explanation of psychosis.

Psychotic experiences are heterogeneous.

Different individuals may reach similar experiences through different mechanisms.

# 28. Attention and Filtering

Attention involves prioritization.

But prioritization creates another problem:

> What happens to competing information?

The brain must often:

- Suppress
- Filter
- Ignore
- Deprioritize

These processes are closely related to attention but should not automatically be treated as identical.

# 30. Common Misconceptions

## Misconception

> Attention is a literal spotlight inside the brain.

### Correction

The spotlight is a metaphor.

---

## Misconception

> Unattended information is completely ignored.

### Correction

Unattended information can still undergo processing.

---

## Misconception

> Attention equals consciousness.

### Correction

They interact but are distinct concepts.

---

## Misconception

> Attention equals salience.

### Correction

Salience can influence attention, but they are not identical.

---

## Misconception

> Attention is completely voluntary.

### Correction

Attention can be captured by events.

---

## Misconception

> Schizophrenia simply causes poor attention.

### Correction

Attentional abnormalities are complex and heterogeneous.

# Final Summary

Attention is best understood not as a single thing but as a family of processes involved in determining:

> **What should receive priority right now?**

It is influenced by:

- Goals
- Expectations
- Sensory events
- Salience
- Context

Attention interacts with:

- Perception
- Consciousness
- Prediction
- Precision
- Salience

But it should not be reduced to any one of them.

For schizophrenia research, the central question is not simply:

> **Do people with schizophrenia have attention problems?**

A better question is:

> **Which attentional processes are altered, how are they altered, and how do they interact with perception, cognition, salience, and subjective experience?**
