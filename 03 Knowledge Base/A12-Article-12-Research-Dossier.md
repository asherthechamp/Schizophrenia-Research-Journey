# Article 12 — Cognitive Control
## Research Dossier

**Article:** 12  
**Title:** Cognitive Control  
**Subtitle:** How does the brain maintain a goal when many competing signals are active at once?

---

# 1. Central Question

How does the brain maintain a goal when many competing signals are active at once?

Article 11 asked:

> How does the brain stop something that it has already started?

Article 12 moves one level upward.

Inhibition is one operation that can constrain an action or process. Cognitive control is broader: it concerns how the cognitive system organizes processing and behavior around goals while handling competition, maintaining relevant information, monitoring performance, and adapting to changing circumstances.

---

# 2. Central Thesis

Cognitive control is the capacity to organize thought and behavior around currently relevant goals.

It involves multiple interacting operations, including:

- selecting task-relevant information,
- maintaining goals and task representations,
- managing competition,
- monitoring conflict and errors,
- suppressing inappropriate responses,
- updating information,
- switching strategies,
- adapting behavior following feedback.

Cognitive control should therefore not be reduced to inhibition.

---

# 3. Working Definition

A useful working definition is:

> Cognitive control is the flexible regulation of information processing and behavior according to currently relevant goals and task demands.

This definition emphasizes:

1. goals,
2. context,
3. competition,
4. flexibility,
5. monitoring,
6. adaptation.

Classic computational work similarly described control as flexible adaptation to task demands, favoring task-relevant information over competing information and task-relevant behavior over habitual or prepotent responses.

Reference:
Cohen, Braver & O'Reilly (1996)
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8941963/

---

# 4. Cognitive Control Is Not Identical to Executive Function

The terms cognitive control and executive function overlap substantially, but they are not always used identically.

Executive-function frameworks often include:

- inhibition,
- working-memory updating,
- task switching,
- cognitive flexibility,
- planning,
- monitoring.

Cognitive-control theories often emphasize:

- goal maintenance,
- selection,
- context sensitivity,
- conflict resolution,
- monitoring,
- adaptation.

Different researchers use the terminology differently.

Therefore:

> Cognitive control should be treated as a major theoretical construct rather than as a universally fixed list of mental abilities.

---

# 5. Goal-Directed Behavior

A central function of cognitive control is allowing behavior to be organized around goals rather than being determined entirely by:

- immediate stimuli,
- habitual responses,
- distractions,
- competing goals,
- previously learned associations.

A person may have many possible actions available at any moment.

Control helps determine:

> Which action should be selected given the current goal and context?

---

# 6. Goal Maintenance

Goals are useful only if they remain available to influence processing.

Goal maintenance therefore refers to maintaining task-relevant information over time.

Examples include maintaining:

- the current task rule,
- the desired outcome,
- relevant contextual information,
- the current strategy,
- the appropriate response criteria.

Failure of goal maintenance can allow competing information or habitual responses to dominate behavior.

---

# 7. Task Representations

A goal usually does not operate in isolation.

The brain must represent aspects of the current task, including:

- what the task requires,
- what information matters,
- what responses are appropriate,
- what responses should be avoided,
- what context is currently relevant.

Computational models of prefrontal function have emphasized the importance of maintaining contextual representations that guide processing.

Cohen, Braver & O'Reilly (1996):
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8941963/

---

# 8. Competition

At any moment, multiple representations can compete for processing and behavioral expression.

Competition can occur between:

- stimuli,
- responses,
- goals,
- memories,
- interpretations,
- task rules,
- habitual and goal-directed actions.

Cognitive control helps bias processing toward information and actions that are relevant to the current goal.

---

# 9. Conflict

Conflict occurs when competing representations or responses are simultaneously activated.

Examples include:

- Stroop conflict,
- flanker conflict,
- incompatible response tendencies,
- competing task rules.

Conflict is therefore not simply "difficulty."

It refers to competition among representations or response tendencies that are relevant to control.

---

# 10. Conflict Monitoring

Conflict monitoring concerns detecting situations in which competing representations or response tendencies create a need for increased control.

The medial frontal cortex, including anterior cingulate regions, has frequently been studied in relation to conflict and performance monitoring.

However:

> Detecting conflict is not identical to resolving conflict.

This distinction is important.

---

# 11. Error Monitoring

Cognitive control also requires detecting when behavior has not produced the intended result.

An error can provide information that:

- the current strategy was insufficient,
- the response was inappropriate,
- the task representation needs updating,
- more control may be required.

Error monitoring can therefore become part of an adaptive control loop.

---

# 12. Post-Error Adjustment

After an error, behavior can change.

Possible adjustments include:

- slowing responses,
- increasing attention,
- changing response criteria,
- increasing caution,
- modifying the current strategy.

These changes illustrate a central principle:

> Cognitive control is not only about controlling the current action; it can use the consequences of previous actions to influence future behavior.

---

# 13. Cognitive Flexibility

Cognitive flexibility allows the system to change its processing strategy when circumstances change.

Examples include:

- switching task rules,
- changing strategies,
- updating task representations,
- abandoning an ineffective approach,
- adapting to new contingencies.

Cognitive flexibility is therefore closely related to adaptive control.

A recent scoping review of schizophrenia research found evidence of impairment in cognitive flexibility, while also emphasizing that the literature remains fragmented and heterogeneous.

https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41880970/

---

# 14. Task Switching

Task switching requires changing the currently active task set.

For example:

Task A:
> Respond according to color.

Task B:
> Respond according to shape.

Successful switching requires:

1. disengaging from the previous task set,
2. activating the new rule,
3. maintaining the new rule,
4. applying it to incoming information.

Switch costs can provide a behavioral measure of the additional control required when switching task sets.

---

# 15. Stability and Flexibility

Cognitive control faces a fundamental tension:

> The system must remain stable enough to maintain a goal, but flexible enough to change the goal or strategy when circumstances require it.

Too little stability can produce:

- distraction,
- goal drift,
- interference,
- unstable task representations.

Too little flexibility can produce:

- perseveration,
- inability to update,
- rigid behavior,
- difficulty switching strategies.

This stability-flexibility problem is a major theme in cognitive-control theory.

---

# 16. Proactive Control

Proactive control involves maintaining goal-relevant information in anticipation of upcoming demands.

The person prepares before the critical stimulus or conflict occurs.

Examples:

- keeping the task rule active,
- anticipating a likely distractor,
- preparing the appropriate response strategy.

Braver's Dual Mechanisms of Control framework distinguishes proactive control from reactive control.

Reference:
Braver (2012)
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22245618/

---

# 17. Reactive Control

Reactive control is recruited when control becomes necessary because of an immediately occurring event.

For example:

A distracting stimulus appears.

The system detects the need for control.

Control is then recruited to handle the current situation.

Thus:

Proactive:
> Prepare control in advance.

Reactive:
> Recruit control when the situation demands it.

These are complementary modes rather than mutually exclusive mechanisms.

---

# 18. Working Memory and Cognitive Control

Working memory provides a temporary workspace for maintaining information relevant to current behavior.

Cognitive control can use working memory to maintain:

- goals,
- task rules,
- context,
- intermediate information,
- response plans.

Working memory and cognitive control therefore interact closely.

However:

> Working memory is not identical to cognitive control.

Working memory provides representations that control processes can maintain and manipulate.

---

# 19. Attention and Cognitive Control

Article 9 established:

> Attention asks: What should receive priority?

Cognitive control asks a broader question:

> How should processing and behavior be organized around the current goal?

Attention can therefore be one mechanism through which control is implemented.

Control may determine:

- what should receive attention,
- when attention should shift,
- what information should remain relevant,
- when an attentional priority should change.

---

# 20. Inhibition as One Component of Control

Article 11 established:

> Inhibition is one operation within a broader control architecture.

Inhibition can constrain:

- a prepotent response,
- distracting information,
- an inappropriate action,
- an activated representation.

But cognitive control additionally includes:

- selection,
- maintenance,
- monitoring,
- updating,
- adaptation,
- switching.

Therefore:

> Cognitive control is broader than inhibition.

---

# 21. Selection

Control requires selecting information and actions that are relevant to the current goal.

Selection can operate over:

- perceptual information,
- memories,
- task rules,
- responses,
- strategies.

Selection does not necessarily mean choosing only one item.

It can mean increasing the relative influence of goal-relevant representations.

---

# 22. Updating

A control system must sometimes update its internal representation.

Examples:

- a task rule changes,
- feedback indicates a previous strategy was ineffective,
- a new goal becomes active,
- contextual information changes.

Updating allows the system to avoid simply maintaining an obsolete representation.

---

# 23. Monitoring and Adaptation

A useful conceptual sequence is:

Goal
↓
Selection
↓
Action
↓
Outcome
↓
Monitoring
↓
Error/conflict detection
↓
Adjustment
↓
New action

This makes cognitive control a dynamic process rather than a static ability.

---

# 24. Prefrontal Cortex

The prefrontal cortex is strongly associated with cognitive control.

However:

> Cognitive control is not located in one brain region.

Different prefrontal systems interact with:

- parietal cortex,
- anterior cingulate/medial frontal regions,
- anterior insula,
- basal ganglia,
- thalamic systems,
- sensory systems,
- motor systems,
- memory systems.

Modern models increasingly emphasize distributed networks.

Menon & D'Esposito (2021):
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34408276/

Friedman & Robbins (2022):
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34408280/

---

# 25. Distributed Brain Networks

Cognitive control depends on interactions among large-scale systems.

Important systems include:

- frontoparietal control networks,
- cingulo-opercular/salience-related systems,
- default-mode network,
- basal ganglia,
- medial frontal regions.

The relevant network configuration can change depending on task demands.

---

# 26. Cognitive Control Is Dynamic

Control is not a fixed amount of "mental power."

The system dynamically adjusts according to:

- task demands,
- uncertainty,
- conflict,
- errors,
- reward,
- effort,
- context,
- changing goals.

This is one reason cognitive-control tasks should not be interpreted as measuring a single underlying capacity.

---

# 27. Cognitive Control and Schizophrenia

Cognitive-control abnormalities are well documented in schizophrenia.

However, the evidence does not support a simple statement:

> "Schizophrenia is a cognitive-control disorder."

Schizophrenia involves heterogeneous abnormalities across:

- attention,
- working memory,
- learning,
- motivation,
- decision-making,
- cognitive flexibility,
- inhibition,
- monitoring,
- network organization.

Cognitive control is therefore an important domain of investigation rather than a complete explanation.

---

# 28. Cognitive-Control Deficits Are Heterogeneous

Different people can show different patterns of impairment.

Different tasks can also place different demands on control.

For example:

- inhibition tasks,
- working-memory tasks,
- task-switching tasks,
- conflict tasks,
- goal-maintenance tasks

do not necessarily measure the same mechanism.

Recent research explicitly emphasizes that cognitive-control impairments in schizophrenia vary considerably according to the particular control process being assessed.

---

# 29. Conflict Adaptation in Schizophrenia

Conflict adaptation provides an important example of why simplistic interpretations should be avoided.

Earlier reviews found abnormalities in neural systems associated with conflict adaptation but concluded that behavioral evidence did not provide a clear-cut answer about which conflict-adaptation processes were impaired.

Reference:
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27100079/

Some subsequent studies have found preserved conflict adaptation.

A 2026 ERP study reported comparable behavioral and neural conflict-adaptation patterns in medicated participants with schizophrenia and healthy controls, despite slower overall responding in the schizophrenia group.

Reference:
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41968641/

Therefore:

> Abnormalities in a brain region or network associated with cognitive control do not automatically imply that every behavioral control process is impaired.

---

# 30. Monitoring and Error Processing in Schizophrenia

Research has examined how schizophrenia affects:

- error monitoring,
- conflict processing,
- behavioral adjustment,
- neural responses to errors.

A study of conflict and error processing described impairments in monitoring ongoing behavior and adjusting responses based on advance information or feedback, while examining an extended cingulo-opercular and cerebellar network.

Reference:
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24273729/

The important point is that control involves both:

> detecting that something happened

and

> using that information to change what happens next.

---

# 31. Goal Maintenance in Schizophrenia

Goal maintenance has been a major theme in schizophrenia research.

A person may know what the task requires but have difficulty maintaining the relevant representation sufficiently strongly over time.

This has motivated computational theories involving:

- context representations,
- prefrontal maintenance,
- gating,
- dopamine,
- working memory.

Braver et al. (1999) developed a computational model linking dopamine and prefrontal function to context maintenance and updating.

Reference:
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10435197/

---

# 32. Computational Control Models

Computational modeling can break apparently unitary cognitive-control deficits into potentially distinguishable mechanisms.

For example:

Observed slower responses

could potentially arise from different processes:

- slower evidence accumulation,
- weaker goal maintenance,
- increased noise,
- altered response threshold,
- reduced motivation,
- inefficient updating,
- impaired learning.

This is one reason computational psychiatry is valuable.

Instead of asking only:

> Did the person perform worse?

we can ask:

> Which computational process could have produced the observed pattern?

---

# 33. Computational Psychiatry and Cognitive Control

Recent computational work has specifically examined cognitive control in schizophrenia.

Barch, Culbreth & Sheffield (2024) reviewed computational approaches including:

- biologically plausible connectionist models,
- mathematical models,
- parameter estimation,
- proactive control,
- motivation and effort.

They emphasize that computational approaches can help separate psychological and neural processes that may produce similar behavioral impairments.

Reference:
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38371195/

---

# 34. Cognitive Control and Motivation

Control is not independent of motivation.

A person may possess a control mechanism but fail to deploy it when:

- the expected reward is low,
- effort is perceived as costly,
- the task has little value,
- the goal representation is weak.

Therefore:

> Poor performance does not automatically prove that the underlying control mechanism is incapable of functioning.

This distinction is particularly important in schizophrenia research, where cognition, motivation, and reward processing can interact.

---

# 35. Cognitive Maps and Goal-Directed Behavior

A newer line of research proposes that some apparent control or decision-making problems may arise from how people represent task structure.

A 2025 study found evidence that people with schizophrenia represented task structure differently, emphasizing simpler visual features and less abstract, higher-order planning-relevant features, while showing similar performance on the particular task studied.

Reference:
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40113740/

This supports an important methodological principle:

> Similar behavior can arise from different internal representations.

---

# 36. Triple-Network Perspective

A major network framework for schizophrenia involves interaction among:

1. Salience network
2. Frontoparietal control network
3. Default-mode network

The salience network is often discussed in relation to identifying behaviorally relevant internal and external events.

The frontoparietal network contributes to cognitive control.

The default-mode network is strongly associated with internally directed cognition.

Menon and colleagues have proposed an integrated model in which abnormalities in interactions among these networks contribute to cognitive dysfunction and aberrant salience processing in schizophrenia.

Reference:
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36702660/

This should be presented as a model/framework rather than as a complete established causal explanation.

---

# 37. Working Memory and Network Organization

Meta-analytic work examining working-memory studies in schizophrenia has identified abnormalities involving:

- dorsolateral prefrontal cortex,
- posterior parietal cortex,
- anterior insula,
- anterior cingulate cortex,
- supplementary motor area,
- default-mode network regions.

This illustrates that cognitive-control abnormalities can involve both:

- task-positive activation,
- task-related suppression/deactivation.

Reference:
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30820860/

---

# 38. Cognitive Control and Internal Cognition

This article is an important bridge toward later questions about internally generated thought.

Cognitive control does not operate only on external sensory information.

It can regulate:

- memories,
- plans,
- mental imagery,
- inner speech,
- self-generated predictions,
- task representations,
- internally directed attention.

Therefore:

> Control is relevant to both outward-directed and inward-directed cognition.

This becomes important when considering schizophrenia, where disturbances can involve both perception and internally generated experience.

---

# 39. Control and Internal Mental Events

A useful conceptual distinction is:

External event
→ perceptual processing
→ selection
→ action

versus:

Internal event
→ memory/thought/imagery
→ selection
→ monitoring
→ evaluation
→ action or further thought

Both pathways can require:

- selection,
- maintenance,
- monitoring,
- inhibition,
- updating,
- adaptation.

This provides a conceptual bridge between cognitive control and the architecture of thought.

---

# 40. Cognitive Control and Precision

Precision can be introduced cautiously as a computational concept.

A representation with higher effective precision exerts greater influence on inference or decision-making.

Cognitive control may therefore be conceptualized, in some computational frameworks, as dynamically regulating the relative influence of competing representations.

However:

> Cognitive control should not simply be defined as precision.

And:

> Precision should not be treated as a proven single mechanism underlying cognitive control in schizophrenia.

The precision connection should remain a theoretical bridge.

---

# 41. Stability and Variability

A control system must maintain useful representations while remaining capable of changing them.

This creates a computational tension:

Stable enough:
→ maintain the current goal.

Flexible enough:
→ update when evidence or context changes.

This stability-flexibility problem is potentially important for understanding schizophrenia without reducing the disorder to either:

- excessive instability,
- excessive rigidity.

Different cognitive processes may show different combinations of stability and flexibility.

---

# 42. Dynamic Control Loop

A useful conceptual model for the article is:

**Goal**
↓
**Maintain task representation**
↓
**Select relevant information**
↓
**Manage competition**
↓
**Act**
↓
**Monitor outcome**
↓
**Detect conflict/error**
↓
**Adapt**
↓
**Update goal or strategy**
↓
**Continue**

This model emphasizes that cognitive control is a loop.

---

# 43. Conceptual Formula

The Article 11 formula can be extended:

**Control = Selection + Maintenance + Monitoring + Suppression + Adaptation**

This is not a literal additive mathematical equation.

It is a conceptual decomposition.

The components represent different operations that can interact:

### Selection
Increase the influence of goal-relevant information.

### Maintenance
Keep goals and task representations active.

### Monitoring
Track current behavior and outcomes.

### Suppression
Constrain competing or inappropriate processes.

### Adaptation
Change processing or behavior based on new information.

Important:

> Inhibition is one operation within this broader control architecture.

---

# 44. Measurement Problem

Different cognitive-control tasks emphasize different processes.

Examples include:

### Stroop
Measures interference under competing response dimensions.

### Flanker
Measures interference from surrounding stimuli.

### Stop-signal task
Measures the ability to cancel an already initiated response.

### Go/No-Go
Measures response restraint under task-specific conditions.

### Task switching
Measures changing task sets.

### Working-memory tasks
Measure maintenance and manipulation of information.

### Conflict adaptation
Measures changes in performance following previous conflict.

### Error adaptation
Measures behavioral adjustments following mistakes.

Therefore:

> Performance on one control task should not automatically be generalized to the entire cognitive-control system.

---

# 45. Evidence Hierarchy

When interpreting cognitive-control research, distinguish:

### Level 1 — Behavioral observation
Example:
A participant responds more slowly.

### Level 2 — Cognitive interpretation
Example:
The task may place greater demands on control.

### Level 3 — Mechanistic interpretation
Example:
The pattern may be consistent with impaired goal maintenance.

### Level 4 — Neural interpretation
Example:
The effect may involve altered frontoparietal or cingulo-opercular network activity.

### Level 5 — Computational interpretation
Example:
A model may attribute the behavior to altered context maintenance, noise, gating, learning, or decision thresholds.

These levels should not be collapsed into one another.

---

# 46. Important Scientific Distinction

A group difference on a cognitive-control task does not establish:

- a single cognitive-control deficit,
- a single neural mechanism,
- a causal relationship,
- a diagnostic biomarker,
- a universal feature of schizophrenia.

It establishes a difference under particular experimental conditions.

The interpretation requires:

- task analysis,
- computational modeling,
- neural evidence,
- replication,
- consideration of medication,
- clinical heterogeneity,
- demographic and cognitive factors.

---

# 47. Network Findings vs Psychological Mechanisms

Finding altered connectivity between two brain regions does not automatically tell us:

> What psychological computation has changed?

Conversely, finding poorer behavioral performance does not automatically tell us:

> Which neural mechanism caused it?

The strongest research connects:

**behavior**
↕
**cognitive process**
↕
**computation**
↕
**neural implementation**

This is particularly important for computational neuroscience.

---

# 48. Avoiding the "Broken Control System" Metaphor

Do not describe schizophrenia as if the entire control system simply turns off.

A better model is:

> Different control operations can be altered to different degrees, in different people, under different task demands.

Some functions may be impaired.

Others may be relatively preserved.

Some may become impaired primarily under high cognitive load.

Some apparent deficits may reflect interactions between cognition, motivation, learning, and task representation.

---

# 49. Key Scientific Guardrails

The article must not claim:

1. Cognitive control is located entirely in the prefrontal cortex.
2. Cognitive control is identical to executive function.
3. Cognitive control is identical to inhibition.
4. Schizophrenia is simply a cognitive-control disorder.
5. All cognitive-control tasks measure the same mechanism.
6. Poor task performance proves a specific cognitive deficit.
7. Altered brain activity proves a behavioral deficit.
8. A network abnormality proves causality.
9. Cognitive-control impairment is identical across all people with schizophrenia.
10. Conflict adaptation is universally impaired in schizophrenia.
11. Cognitive control is simply "top-down control."
12. Precision is equivalent to cognitive control.
13. Predictive processing has already established a complete causal explanation of schizophrenia.

---

# 50. Central Conceptual Distinctions

### Attention
What deserves priority?

### Filtering
What should receive less priority?

### Inhibition
What should be constrained or stopped?

### Cognitive Control
How should processing and behavior be organized around the goal?

### Monitoring
What is happening, and is it consistent with the goal?

### Adaptation
What should change because of what just happened?

### Cognitive Flexibility
When should the current strategy or representation change?

# 52. Article 11 → Article 12 Transition

Article 11:

> How does the brain stop something that it has already started?

Answer:

Inhibition can constrain or stop an ongoing tendency.

But that immediately raises another question:

> Who decides what should be stopped?

And even more importantly:

> What determines what should happen instead?

That is the problem of cognitive control.

Control does not merely stop behavior.

It organizes behavior.

---

# 53. Article 12 Central Message

The central message should be:

> Cognitive control is not simply the ability to stop an action. It is the broader capacity to organize thought and behavior around goals while managing competition, maintaining relevant information, monitoring performance, and adapting when circumstances change.

# 57. Final Research Position

The article should present cognitive control as:

- distributed,
- dynamic,
- context-sensitive,
- goal-dependent,
- computationally decomposable,
- behaviorally measurable,
- neurally implemented across interacting systems,
- heterogeneous in schizophrenia.

It should avoid presenting cognitive control as a single brain mechanism.

The strongest conceptual framing is:

> Cognitive control is an adaptive architecture for keeping behavior aligned with goals while continuously negotiating competition, uncertainty, conflict, errors, and changing circumstances.
