# Article 13 — Research Dossier

# What Is Consciousness — and What Is the Self?

## Subtitle

How does experience become conscious and organized from a particular point of view?

---

# 1. Central Question

How does experience become something that is not only processed by the brain, but consciously experienced from a particular point of view?

This question contains two connected but distinct problems:

1. What is consciousness?
2. Why is conscious experience normally organized from a first-person perspective?

The second question leads naturally toward the concept of the self.

---

# 2. Central Thesis

Consciousness concerns the presence and organization of subjective experience.

But conscious experience normally has a particular structure:

- it happens here
- it happens now
- it is experienced from a perspective
- it is associated with a body
- it normally feels like it is happening to or being experienced by oneself

Therefore, understanding consciousness leads naturally toward understanding selfhood.

However:

> Consciousness and selfhood are related but not identical.

# 4. Working Definition of Consciousness

For this article:

> Consciousness is the presence of subjective experience.

Examples:

- seeing
- hearing
- feeling pain
- experiencing emotion
- remembering
- imagining
- thinking

This is a working definition rather than a complete theory.

---

# 5. Consciousness Is Not Identical to Information Processing

The brain performs enormous amounts of processing without conscious awareness.

Therefore:

**Neural processing ≠ conscious experience**

The scientific problem is to identify:

- which processes are associated with consciousness
- under what conditions
- through which mechanisms
- and whether those mechanisms are causal

---

# 6. Consciousness and Wakefulness

Wakefulness concerns global arousal or state.

Consciousness concerns experience.

They interact but are not identical.

Examples of altered states demonstrate that consciousness can change independently of ordinary waking behavior:

- dreaming
- anesthesia
- minimally conscious states
- altered states

---

# 7. Consciousness and Awareness

Awareness can refer to several constructs:

- stimulus awareness
- perceptual awareness
- self-awareness
- metacognitive awareness

Therefore "awareness" should not be treated as one single mechanism.

---

# 8. Consciousness and Attention

Article 9:

**Attention = What receives priority?**

Article 13:

**Consciousness = What is experienced?**

Attention and consciousness interact.

They are not identical.

Important guardrail:

> Do not define consciousness as attention.

---

# 9. Consciousness and Perception

Perception concerns the construction/inference of representations about the world and body.

Conscious perception concerns when perceptual content becomes part of subjective experience.

Therefore:

**Perception ≠ necessarily conscious perception**

---

# 10. Conscious and Unconscious Processing

Evidence demonstrates substantial processing without conscious awareness.

Examples:

- priming
- subliminal processing
- implicit memory
- automatic motor processing
- unconscious perceptual processing

This establishes an important principle:

> Conscious experience is one aspect of brain function, not synonymous with brain processing itself.

---

# 11. Access Consciousness

Access consciousness concerns information being available for:

- reasoning
- working memory
- decision-making
- verbal report
- flexible behavioral control

This concept is especially relevant to Global Workspace approaches.

---

# 12. Phenomenal Consciousness

Phenomenal consciousness concerns the subjective character of experience.

Examples:

- what red looks like
- what pain feels like
- what music sounds like
- what an emotion feels like

Often summarized as:

> "What is it like?"

Important distinction:

Phenomenal consciousness is not identical to verbal report.

---

# 13. Global Workspace Theory

Global Workspace theories propose that information becomes conscious when it becomes broadly available to multiple cognitive systems.

Potentially available to:

- working memory
- reasoning
- decision-making
- language
- cognitive control

Global Neuronal Workspace Theory proposes a large-scale neural implementation.

Important guardrail:

> Global Workspace is a theory, not a settled explanation of consciousness.

---

# 14. Higher-Order Theories

Higher-Order theories propose that consciousness involves some form of representation of a mental state by another mental representation.

Conceptual distinction:

First-order:

**There is a perceptual representation.**

Higher-order:

**I am consciously experiencing that perception.**

Different higher-order theories make different claims about the required architecture.

---

# 15. Recurrent Processing

Recurrent Processing Theory emphasizes recurrent or reentrant interactions.

The basic proposal is that conscious experience may depend on recurrent processing rather than merely initial feedforward sensory processing.

---

# 16. Integrated Information Theory

IIT begins from proposed structural properties of conscious experience.

Relevant concepts:

- integration
- differentiation
- information
- unity
- exclusion

It attempts to connect these properties with causal organization in physical systems.

Important:

> IIT remains theoretically and empirically contested.

---

# 17. Predictive Processing

Predictive processing describes the brain as a system that generates predictions and updates them based on sensory evidence.

Relevant concepts:

- generative models
- priors
- prediction
- prediction error
- inference
- precision

Predictive approaches have been applied to consciousness.

However:

> Predictive processing is a broad framework, not an established complete theory of consciousness.

---

# 18. Major Theory Comparison

| Theory | Main emphasis | Article 13 role |
|---|---|---|
| Global Workspace | Global availability | Conscious access |
| Higher-Order | Representation of mental states | Awareness of mental states |
| Recurrent Processing | Recurrent neural interactions | Conscious perceptual processing |
| IIT | Integrated causal structure | Structural properties of experience |
| Predictive approaches | Inference/generative modeling | Relation between prediction and experience |

This table is deliberately simplified.

Each theory contains substantially more detail than the table captures.

---

# 19. Theories Are Not Yet Unified

There is no universally accepted theory explaining consciousness.

Major theories make overlapping but distinct claims.

The scientific objective should therefore include:

- theory comparison
- competing predictions
- experimental discrimination
- causal testing

rather than merely collecting compatible evidence.

---

# 20. Adversarial Theory Testing

A strong test of consciousness theories asks:

> What result would distinguish one theory from another?

This is stronger than asking whether a finding is compatible with a theory.

Recent adversarial collaborations in consciousness science illustrate this approach.

---

# 21. Neural Correlates of Consciousness

Neural correlates of consciousness are neural processes associated with conscious experience.

But:

**Correlation ≠ causation**

A neural correlate might be:

- necessary
- sufficient
- enabling
- downstream
- merely associated

Therefore causal manipulation is important.

---

# 22. The Hard Problem

The hard problem asks:

> Why are physical processes accompanied by subjective experience at all?

Neuroscience can investigate:

- neural activity
- computation
- behavior
- reports
- information processing
- causal brain manipulation

But the philosophical problem of why experience exists remains unresolved.

---

# 23. The "Easy Problems"

The so-called easy problems concern functional mechanisms such as:

- discrimination
- access
- report
- integration
- attention
- behavioral control

"Easy" does not mean unimportant.

It means these problems can be framed as mechanistic or functional problems.

---

# 24. Limits of Consciousness Science

Major methodological challenges include:

- reliance on subjective reports
- report-related confounds
- attention/consciousness confounding
- working-memory confounding
- difficulty distinguishing neural correlates from mechanisms
- difficulty comparing theories with different assumptions

Therefore epistemic caution is essential.

---

# 25. Consciousness Has Multiple Levels of Explanation

A useful research hierarchy is:

**Phenomenology**

↓

**Psychology**

↓

**Computation**

↓

**Neural dynamics**

↓

**Cellular mechanisms**

No single level should automatically replace the others.

---

# 26. From Consciousness to First-Person Perspective

Conscious experience normally appears organized from a point of view.

There is:

- a here
- a now
- a perspective
- a body-centered frame

This does not require a separate internal observer.

Instead, first-person organization may be an intrinsic feature of ordinary experience.

---

# 27. First-Person Perspective

First-person perspective refers to experience organized from the subject's point of view.

Examples:

- seeing from one's visual perspective
- feeling one's body
- experiencing sensations as occurring to one's body
- experiencing thoughts within one's own mental life

It is related to but distinct from autobiographical identity.

---

# 28. Consciousness Does Not Require an Explicit Narrative Self

A person can consciously:

- see
- hear
- feel
- remember
- imagine

without explicitly thinking about personal identity.

Therefore:

**Consciousness ≠ narrative self**

---

# 29. Mineness

Mineness refers to the implicit sense that an experience is one's own.

Ordinarily, we do not explicitly think:

> "This experience belongs to me."

The experience simply occurs in a first-person mode.

Mineness is therefore often described as pre-reflective.

---

# 30. Pre-Reflective Selfhood

Pre-reflective selfhood means that self-related structure may be present before deliberate reflection.

It does not require:

- verbal self-description
- explicit introspection
- autobiographical reasoning

This concept is central to the minimal-self literature.

---

# 31. The Self Is Layered

The word "self" can refer to several different phenomena.

Possible layers:

1. Minimal/pre-reflective self
2. Bodily self
3. Ownership
4. Agency
5. Reflective self-awareness
6. Autobiographical self
7. Narrative identity
8. Social self

These should be treated as related but distinguishable constructs.

---

# 32. Minimal Self

The minimal self refers to the most basic form of selfhood.

Possible components include:

- first-person perspective
- mineness
- embodiment
- self-presence
- temporal continuity
- agency

There is no universal agreement about which components are necessary.

Therefore:

> The minimal self is a theoretical and phenomenological construct, not a single universally established neural entity.

---

# 33. Minimal Self and Consciousness

Important theoretical question:

> Is consciousness inherently first-personal?

Some theories argue that conscious experience necessarily has a first-person structure.

Other approaches distinguish consciousness more strongly from self-representation.

This remains an open issue.

---

# 34. Embodiment

Self-experience depends partly on multisensory and bodily information.

Relevant systems include:

- interoception
- proprioception
- vestibular information
- tactile information
- visual information
- motor signals

The embodied self is therefore an active multisensory organization.

---

# 35. Body Ownership

Body ownership is the experience that a body or body part belongs to oneself.

Experimental work such as the rubber-hand illusion demonstrates that ownership can be manipulated through multisensory integration.

This suggests that at least some aspects of self-experience are constructed rather than completely fixed.

---

# 36. Agency

Agency is the experience of being the author or cause of an action.

Important distinction:

**Ownership**

"This is my body/action."

**Agency**

"I caused this action."

These experiences can dissociate.

Article 14 will examine this distinction in detail.

---

# 37. Consciousness → Self → Agency

Conceptual sequence:

**Conscious experience**

↓

**First-person perspective**

↓

**Mineness**

↓

**Embodied self**

↓

**Agency**

This is a conceptual map, not a claim that the brain literally computes these stages in sequence.

---

# 38. Self-Reference

More reflective self-related cognition includes:

- self-evaluation
- autobiographical memory
- introspection
- social self-awareness
- thinking about oneself
- narrative identity

These are more elaborate than minimal selfhood.

---

# 39. Narrative Self

The narrative self organizes experience across time.

It includes:

- memories
- identity
- relationships
- values
- goals
- personal history

This differs from the immediate first-person character of experience.

---

# 40. Consciousness and Internal Cognition

Conscious experience includes internally generated events:

- thoughts
- inner speech
- memories
- imagery
- intentions
- emotions
- bodily sensations

This becomes crucial for schizophrenia research.

---

# 41. Internally Generated Mental Events

Later articles will investigate how the brain distinguishes:

**internally generated**

from

**externally generated**

events.

Potential mechanisms include:

- source monitoring
- self-monitoring
- motor prediction
- corollary discharge
- agency inference
- sensory prediction
- metacognition

These mechanisms should not be collapsed into a single explanation.

---

# 42. Consciousness and Schizophrenia

Do not characterize schizophrenia as simply a consciousness disorder.

Instead ask:

> Which aspects of conscious self-experience may become altered in schizophrenia?

Potential domains:

- agency
- ownership
- self-presence
- first-person perspective
- self-reference
- source attribution
- self-world boundaries

---

# 43. Self-Disorder

Phenomenological models propose disturbances in basic self-experience in schizophrenia-spectrum conditions.

Important concepts include:

- hyperreflexivity
- diminished self-affection
- disturbed first-person perspective
- altered agency
- altered ownership
- self-world boundary disturbances

These remain theoretical constructs with ongoing empirical investigation.

---

# 44. Hyperreflexivity

In phenomenological models, hyperreflexivity describes an unusual tendency for normally implicit processes to become objects of heightened awareness or attention.

It is not simply:

"thinking too much."

It concerns a change in the relationship between experience and awareness.

---

# 45. Diminished Self-Affection

Diminished self-affection/self-presence describes a weakening or alteration of the normally immediate sense of being a self-present subject.

It should not be simplified to:

"I have no self."

---

# 46. Evidence for Basic Self Disturbance

A meta-analytic literature has reported differences between schizophrenia and healthy-control groups in measures of basic self-disturbance.

Relevant domains include:

- body ownership
- agency
- self-experience
- self-reflection

However:

- studies vary in methodology
- constructs vary
- effect estimates may be heterogeneous
- group differences do not establish causality

---

# 47. Contemporary Self-Disorder Models

The ipseity-disorder framework proposes that schizophrenia may involve alterations in basic selfhood.

Two concepts are particularly important:

### Hyperreflexivity

Normally implicit processes become unusually explicit or focal.

### Diminished self-affection

The immediate sense of self-presence becomes weakened or unstable.

The model is influential but remains a theoretical framework rather than a complete causal theory.

---

# 48. Phenomenology and Neuroscience

Phenomenology asks:

> What does the experience feel like?

Psychology asks:

> What cognitive processes characterize it?

Computation asks:

> What computational principles could produce it?

Neuroscience asks:

> What neural processes implement it?

A major objective of computational psychiatry is to connect these levels.

- How is self-related evidence weighted?
- How are internal causes inferred?

The broader journey has established:

**Prediction → Prediction Error → Precision → Inference**

Possible later questions:

- Does precision influence conscious access?
- Does precision influence self-related inference?
- Does precision influence agency?
- Does unstable precision contribute to abnormal self-experience?

These should remain hypotheses rather than conclusions.

# 53. Major Conceptual Distinctions

## Consciousness vs Attention

Experience vs priority.

## Consciousness vs Perception

Experience vs perceptual processing.

## Consciousness vs Cognition

Subjective experience vs broader information processing.

## Consciousness vs Self

Experience vs first-person/self-related organization.

## Minimal Self vs Narrative Self

Pre-reflective subjectivity vs autobiographical identity.

## Ownership vs Agency

"This is mine" vs "I caused it."

---

# 54. Scientific Guardrails

Do not claim:

1. Consciousness has one universally accepted neural mechanism.
2. Global Workspace is proven.
3. IIT is proven.
4. Higher-Order theories are proven.
5. Predictive Processing completely explains consciousness.
6. Consciousness equals attention.
7. Consciousness equals working memory.
8. Consciousness equals reportability.
9. Consciousness equals selfhood.
10. The self is located in one brain region.
11. Schizophrenia is simply a consciousness disorder.
12. Self-disorder is the sole cause of schizophrenia.
13. Phenomenology directly reveals neural mechanisms.
14. Neural correlates automatically establish causality.
15. Minimal selfhood has one universally accepted definition.

---

# 55. Evidence Hierarchy

For Article 13, distinguish:

### Philosophical arguments

Useful for defining conceptual problems.

### Phenomenological descriptions

Useful for characterizing subjective experience.

### Behavioral experiments

Useful for testing cognitive functions.

### Neuroimaging/electrophysiology

Useful for identifying neural associations and dynamics.

### Causal interventions

Useful for testing causal contributions.

### Computational models

Useful for formalizing proposed mechanisms.

### Meta-analysis

Useful for estimating patterns across studies but dependent on the quality and comparability of included studies.

No single evidence type answers every question.

---

# 56. Central Scientific Position

The article should maintain:

> Consciousness is a multidimensional scientific and philosophical problem involving subjective experience, access, neural dynamics, and first-person organization.

No single theory currently provides universally accepted closure.

# 61. Core Message for the Research Journey

The brain does not merely process information.

It produces a continuously changing field of experience.

That experience normally has structure:

- a world
- a body
- a perspective
- a present moment
- a sense of mineness
- an acting subject

Understanding this structure provides the foundation for studying disturbances of selfhood in schizophrenia.

---

# 62. Final Position

Article 13 should not attempt to solve consciousness or define the self once and for all.

Its purpose is to establish the conceptual and scientific foundation needed for the next stage of the research journey.

The key progression is:

**World**

↓

**Experience**

↓

**Consciousness**

↓

**Perspective**

↓

**Self**

↓

**Agency**

↓

**Self-Disorder**

The next question is therefore:

> **What is the minimal structure that makes an experience feel like it is happening to me, from here, as mine?**

That is the problem of the **minimal self**.

And immediately after that:

> **Why do my actions feel like my actions?**

That is the problem of **agency**.
