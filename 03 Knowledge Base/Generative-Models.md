# Generative Models

## Definition

A generative model represents possible relationships between hidden
causes and observable data.

In neuroscience and computational psychiatry, generative models can
describe how hypothetical causes could produce sensory observations.

Simplified:

**Hidden causes**
↓
**Generative model**
↓
**Predicted observations**

Inference works in the opposite direction:

**Observed information**
↓
**Inference**
↓
**Estimated causes**

---

## Example

Suppose an organism sees an object.

The object is a possible hidden cause.

The visual system receives sensory information generated by that object.

A generative model can represent possible relationships between:

- object identity;
- shape;
- lighting;
- position;
- visual features;
- sensory signals.

The system can then estimate which hidden cause best explains the
available sensory evidence.

---

## Generative Models and Predictive Processing

Generative models are central to many predictive-processing theories.

A generative model allows a system to generate predictions about what
sensory input should look like if a particular hypothesis about the
world were true.

Conceptually:

**Hypothesis**
↓
**Generative model**
↓
**Prediction**
↓
**Sensory comparison**

This provides a computational basis for predictive inference.

---

## Generative Models and Bayesian Inference

Bayesian inference can be used to estimate hidden causes from
observations.

The general relationship is:

**Prior beliefs + likelihood of evidence**
→
**Posterior beliefs**

Generative models can specify the probabilistic relationships required
for such inference.

However, generative models are not inherently Bayesian, and predictive
processing is not identical to Bayesian inference.

---

## Hierarchical Generative Models

A hierarchical generative model represents causes at multiple levels.

For example:

**Context**
↓
**Object**
↓
**Shape**
↓
**Sensory features**

Higher-level variables can influence lower-level predictions.

This is one reason hierarchical generative models are important in
predictive-processing theories.

---

## Perception

Generative models provide one possible computational explanation for
how perception can remain stable despite incomplete or noisy sensory
information.

The system can use:

- sensory evidence;
- prior information;
- context;
- learned regularities;
- uncertainty.

The resulting percept is therefore an inferred representation rather
than a simple copy of sensory input.

This does not imply that perception is unconstrained imagination.

Sensory evidence remains an essential constraint.

---

## Computational Psychiatry

Generative models are increasingly important in computational
psychiatry because they can provide explicit mathematical models of
how individuals:

- perceive;
- learn;
- update beliefs;
- make decisions;
- infer causes;
- respond to uncertainty.

This can allow researchers to estimate computational parameters from
behavioral or neural data.

---

## Relationship to Schizophrenia

Generative-model approaches have been applied to schizophrenia and
psychosis to study possible abnormalities in:

- belief updating;
- sensory inference;
- uncertainty;
- learning;
- salience;
- agency;
- source monitoring.

However, no single generative model explains schizophrenia as a whole.

Schizophrenia remains biologically and phenomenologically
heterogeneous.

---

## Relationship to PIF

Generative models may provide a future computational framework for
testing PIF.

For example, a PIF model could specify whether instability in precision
should appear as changes in identifiable computational parameters.

A scientifically useful PIF model would need to make predictions that
can be compared against competing generative models.

---

## Important Distinction

A generative model is a mathematical or computational representation.

It is not automatically a literal description of how the brain is
implemented biologically.

The distinction between:

**computational model**

and

**neural mechanism**

must therefore be maintained.
