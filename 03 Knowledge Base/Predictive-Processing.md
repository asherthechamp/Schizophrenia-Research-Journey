# Predictive Processing

## Definition

Predictive processing is a family of computational and theoretical
approaches proposing that perception and cognition involve the
continuous generation and updating of predictions about sensory input.

In simplified form:

**Internal model → Prediction → Sensory evidence → Mismatch → Model updating**

The framework emphasizes that perception is not simply a passive
registration of incoming sensory information. Internal expectations,
prior information, context, and sensory evidence can interact during
inference.

Predictive processing is an influential framework in cognitive
neuroscience and computational psychiatry, but it is not a single,
universally accepted theory of brain function.

---

## Core Idea

The nervous system receives incomplete and potentially noisy sensory
information.

Instead of treating every sensory signal independently, the system can
use internal models to estimate the likely causes of those signals.

A simplified process is:

**Prediction**
↓
**Sensory evidence**
↓
**Comparison**
↓
**Prediction error**
↓
**Model updating**
↓
**New prediction**

This process can occur continuously and at multiple levels of
abstraction.

---

## Generative Models

Predictive-processing theories commonly use generative models.

A generative model represents possible relationships between hidden
causes and observable sensory consequences.

For example:

**External cause**
↓
**Generative model**
↓
**Predicted sensory consequences**

The system can then compare predicted consequences with actual sensory
input.

---

## Hierarchical Processing

Many predictive-processing models describe perception as hierarchical.

Higher levels represent more abstract causes and can generate
predictions for lower levels.

Lower levels provide information about discrepancies between predictions
and sensory observations.

Conceptually:

**High-level causes**
↓
**Objects**
↓
**Features**
↓
**Sensory representations**

This is a conceptual model and should not be interpreted as a complete
literal map of cortical organization.

---

## Top-Down and Bottom-Up Processing

Predictive processing emphasizes reciprocal information flow.

### Top-Down

Higher-level expectations can influence lower-level processing.

### Bottom-Up

Sensory information can influence higher-level representations.

The framework therefore does not imply that perception is purely
top-down.

Rather, perception can emerge from interaction between sensory evidence
and internally generated expectations.

---

## Prediction Error

A mismatch between predicted and observed information can be described
as prediction error.

Conceptually:

**Prediction error = observed information − predicted information**

The exact mathematical and biological meaning of prediction error
depends on the model.

Therefore, "prediction error" should not automatically be treated as one
specific neural signal.

Prediction error will be examined in greater detail in Article 5.

---

## Precision

Predictive-processing models often use the concept of precision to
represent the estimated reliability of information.

In statistical terms, precision is commonly related to inverse
variance.

Conceptually:

**High precision → greater estimated reliability**

**Low precision → greater uncertainty**

Precision can influence how strongly different sources of information
contribute to inference.

Precision is related to, but not identical to, attention.

Precision will be examined in greater detail later in the research
journey.

---

## Bayesian Inference

Predictive processing is closely related to Bayesian approaches to
inference but is not identical to Bayesian inference.

Bayesian inference provides a mathematical framework for combining
prior information with evidence.

Predictive processing is a broader family of computational and
neuroscientific approaches that can incorporate probabilistic
generative models and Bayesian principles.

Therefore:

**Bayesian inference ≠ predictive processing**

---

## Free-Energy Principle

Predictive processing is frequently discussed alongside Karl Friston's
free-energy principle and active inference.

These frameworks overlap but should not be treated as synonymous.

Predictive coding, predictive processing, active inference, and the
free-energy principle operate at different theoretical levels and make
different assumptions.

---

## Predictive Processing and Perception

Predictive processing provides a framework for understanding how
perception can remain stable despite incomplete or ambiguous sensory
information.

Examples include:

- recognizing partially obscured objects;
- understanding speech in noise;
- interpreting ambiguous stimuli;
- incorporating contextual information;
- anticipating sensory consequences of actions.

The fact that predictions influence perception does not by itself prove
that the entire predictive-processing framework is correct.

---

## Predictive Processing and Psychosis

Predictive-processing models have been applied to psychosis because
psychotic symptoms can involve disturbances in:

- perception;
- belief;
- salience;
- agency;
- source monitoring;
- uncertainty;
- learning.

Possible applications include models of hallucinations, delusions, and
aberrant salience.

However, predictive processing should currently be treated as a
research framework rather than a complete explanation of schizophrenia.

---

## Hallucinations

Some predictive-processing models propose that hallucinations could
involve altered weighting between prior expectations and sensory
evidence.

Different models propose different mechanisms.

Possible mechanisms include:

- unusually strong prior expectations;
- weakened sensory evidence;
- altered prediction-error signaling;
- altered precision weighting.

Therefore:

**Hallucinations ≠ simply "overly strong priors."**

The precise computational mechanisms remain an active research question.

---

## Schizophrenia

Predictive-processing theories have been applied to several features
of schizophrenia, including:

- hallucinations;
- delusions;
- altered salience;
- agency disturbances;
- decision-making;
- cognitive symptoms.

Schizophrenia is heterogeneous, and no single predictive-processing
abnormality has been established as the explanation for the disorder.

Predictive processing should therefore be considered one possible
explanatory level within a broader multilevel account.

---

## Evidence Status

### Strong / General Evidence

- Prior information can influence perception.
- Context can influence perception.
- Prediction-related processing occurs in the nervous system.
- Mismatches between expectations and observations can influence
  learning and behavior.

### Established Theoretical Framework

- Predictive coding provides a useful computational framework.
- Predictive processing is an influential approach in cognitive
  neuroscience.

### Active Research

- The precise neural implementation of predictive coding.
- Identification and interpretation of neural prediction-error
  signals.
- The role of precision in neural computation.
- The relationship between predictive processing and psychosis.

### Not Established

- Predictive processing as a complete theory of the brain.
- Predictive processing as a complete explanation of schizophrenia.
- Hallucinations as simply the result of excessively strong priors.

---

## Important Distinction

Predictive processing is an established and influential **research
framework**, but many of its strongest claims remain theoretical and
empirically testable.

The project should distinguish:

**Observed phenomenon**

from

**Computational interpretation**

from

**Neural implementation**

from

**Psychiatric hypothesis**

This distinction is essential for scientific rigor.

---

## Relationship to PIF

Predictive processing provides an important conceptual foundation for
the Precision Instability Framework (PIF).

Predictive processing asks:

> How does a system infer the causes of sensory input using predictions,
> sensory evidence, and uncertainty?

PIF asks a narrower question:

> Could instability in the estimation, maintenance, or allocation of
> precision contribute to disturbances across multiple psychological
> domains?

PIF is therefore **not equivalent to predictive processing**.

PIF is a proposed research hypothesis that must be tested against
predictive-processing models and competing explanations.
