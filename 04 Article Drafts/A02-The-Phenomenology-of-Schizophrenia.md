# The Phenomenology of Schizophrenia

## What happens when the organization of experience begins to change?

In our first article, we asked a deceptively simple question:

**What is schizophrenia?**

The answer was not a single gene, brain region, neurotransmitter, or biological mechanism.

Instead, we encountered a complex clinical syndrome with substantial variation between people and multiple biological, psychological, and environmental contributors. [1]

But that leaves another question.

Perhaps an even more fundamental one:

> **What is it actually like to experience schizophrenia?**

A diagnosis tells us something about a person's symptoms.

It does not necessarily tell us what those symptoms mean from the inside.

Someone may be described as experiencing a hallucination.

But what does the experience itself feel like?

Someone may be described as having a delusion.

But what changes in the person's experience of meaning and significance makes a particular interpretation compelling?

Someone may be described as having avolition.

But does that mean they do not want to act—or that they want to act but cannot initiate the action?

And what happens when a person's ordinary sense of being the subject of their own thoughts, perceptions, and actions begins to feel different?

These questions take us into **phenomenology**.

---

# Beyond a List of Symptoms

Psychiatry often begins by identifying observable or reportable symptoms.

Hallucinations.

Delusions.

Disorganized thinking.

Reduced motivation.

Social withdrawal.

Cognitive difficulties.

These descriptions are useful.

But they can hide enormous differences in experience.

Consider two people who both report hearing voices.

One may experience a voice as coming from outside the body.

Another may experience it as somehow located inside the mind.

One may experience the voice as hostile.

Another may experience it as comforting.

One may feel completely convinced that the voice belongs to another person.

Another may recognize that the experience is unusual while still finding it difficult to control.

So "auditory hallucination" is not necessarily one uniform experience.

It is a clinical category containing many different experiences.

Phenomenology tries to investigate that structure.

Instead of asking only:

> **Is the symptom present?**

it asks:

> **What is the structure of the experience?**

---

# When Perception Changes

Conventional definitions describe hallucinations as perception-like experiences occurring without an appropriate external stimulus.

Auditory verbal hallucinations—commonly described as "hearing voices"—are particularly important in schizophrenia.

But phenomenological research has questioned whether the simple description of "hearing something that is not there" captures the diversity of these experiences.

Research has identified substantial variation in perceived location, agency, emotional character, controllability, frequency, and relationship to thought. [7][8]

This immediately raises a deeper question:

> **What distinguishes an internally generated experience from an externally generated perception?**

Ordinarily, we do not consciously determine the origin of every experience.

The distinction is usually implicit.

I see a person standing across the room.

I hear someone speaking.

I recognize these as events occurring in the external world.

But perception is not simply a camera recording reality.

The brain continuously constructs interpretations from sensory information, prior knowledge, expectations, and context.

Understanding hallucinations therefore requires us to understand how internally generated representations can acquire perceptual qualities.

But this does **not** mean that hallucinations have already been explained by predictive processing, dopamine, or any other single theory.

Those remain research questions.

---

# When the World Becomes Meaningful in a Different Way

Delusions are another major component of psychosis.

A delusion is not simply a strange idea.

The deeper phenomenon concerns the relationship between the person, their beliefs, their evidence, and their experience of significance.

Imagine walking down a street and noticing that several strangers seem to look at you.

For most people, this observation may have little significance.

But imagine a state in which ordinary events begin to feel unusually significant.

A glance becomes a message.

A coincidence becomes evidence.

A television program seems personally relevant.

A sequence of unrelated events begins to form a pattern.

The world may begin to feel as though it is **pointing toward something**.

This has motivated research into **aberrant salience**—a proposed framework in which otherwise ordinary stimuli acquire unusually strong significance. [9]

The concept has been influential in psychosis research, particularly in theories linking dopamine dysfunction with altered salience. [9]

But its specific mechanistic claims remain contested.

That distinction matters.

> **An influential hypothesis is not the same thing as an established mechanism.**

The phenomenological question comes first:

> **What does a world that has suddenly become unusually meaningful feel like?**

---

# When Thought Becomes Difficult to Follow

Another domain involves the organization and flow of thought and language.

Clinicians may describe:

- derailment
- tangentiality
- loose associations
- reduced coherence
- poverty of speech
- disorganized communication

These descriptions are clinically useful.

But they leave open another question:

> **What is happening to the underlying organization of thought?**

Is the problem primarily linguistic?

Cognitive?

Attentional?

Associative?

Working-memory related?

A consequence of abnormal neural dynamics?

Or some combination?

Again, the observable symptom does not automatically reveal its mechanism.

The same principle keeps appearing:

**Description is not explanation.**

We first need to understand what is happening before we can determine why it is happening.

---

# When Thoughts No Longer Feel Entirely One's Own

Perhaps the most philosophically striking phenomena associated with schizophrenia-spectrum experiences involve the experience of one's own thoughts and actions.

Most of us experience thoughts as implicitly belonging to us.

I do not normally stop and ask:

> "Did I generate this thought?"

The sense of ownership is simply there.

But some people with schizophrenia-spectrum experiences report phenomena such as:

- thought insertion
- thought broadcasting
- experiences of external control
- passivity experiences

These phenomena raise a profound question:

> **How does the brain generate the experience that a thought is mine?**

And perhaps an even deeper one:

> **What makes an experience feel like it belongs to a self?**

This is where phenomenological research has developed concepts such as **ipseity**, **minimal self**, **self-presence**, and **hyperreflexivity**. [2][3][4][5][6]

These concepts attempt to describe disturbances in the normally implicit first-person structure of experience.

Contemporary research increasingly investigates self-disorder as an important feature of schizophrenia-spectrum psychopathology. [5][6]

But there is a crucial distinction:

> **A phenomenological feature can be important without being established as the biological cause of the disorder.**

The evidence does not justify reducing schizophrenia to a single theory of selfhood.

---

# The Fragility of Agency

Our ordinary experience contains another implicit assumption:

**I am the one doing this.**

I decide to raise my hand.

My hand moves.

The movement feels like something I caused.

That experience is called **agency**.

Schizophrenia-spectrum experiences can sometimes involve disturbances of agency.

A person may experience an action as being controlled by someone else.

A thought may appear to have been inserted.

An intention may feel strangely detached from the resulting action.

These experiences provide an extraordinary window into something we normally take for granted.

They suggest that the feeling of agency is not simply identical to movement itself.

The brain must somehow construct an interpretation:

> **This action belongs to me.**

This makes agency an important bridge between phenomenology and neuroscience.

It also creates a testable question:

> **What computational processes allow the brain to distinguish self-generated events from externally generated events?**

---

# The Changing Sense of Self

Phenomenological researchers have proposed that some schizophrenia-spectrum experiences involve disturbances in the basic sense of self.

This does not necessarily mean that a person consciously thinks:

> "I no longer exist."

The proposed disturbance can be much subtler.

It can involve the background sense that:

- experience is happening to me,
- I am the subject of my thoughts,
- my perceptions originate from my perspective,
- my body belongs to me,
- my thoughts are mine,
- I am naturally situated in the world.

Philosophers and phenomenological psychiatrists sometimes refer to this basic first-person character as the **minimal self** or **ipseity**.

One influential research tradition proposes that disturbances in this basic self-experience may be particularly important in schizophrenia-spectrum disorders.

Whether such disturbances are primary, secondary, causal, or simply associated with other processes remains an active research question.

This distinction is important because:

**phenomenological description ≠ biological mechanism**

and

**association ≠ causation**.

---

# When Motivation Disappears

Now consider a different phenomenon:

**Avolition.**

A person may stop initiating activities.

They may spend much of the day inactive.

They may withdraw socially.

They may stop pursuing goals that previously mattered.

From the outside, this can look like a lack of interest.

But the internal experience may be considerably more complicated.

Perhaps the person wants to act but cannot initiate the action.

Perhaps anticipated pleasure is diminished.

Perhaps effort feels unusually costly.

Perhaps attention cannot be sustained.

Perhaps social interaction has become aversive.

Perhaps depression or another condition is contributing.

This is why researchers distinguish different components of negative symptoms and, importantly, distinguish **primary** negative symptoms from symptoms that may arise secondarily from other causes. [11][12]

The label "negative symptoms" therefore does not provide a complete explanation.

It describes a family of phenomena that require further decomposition.

---

# What Does Anhedonia Actually Mean?

The same problem appears with anhedonia.

"Anhedonia" is often translated as a loss of pleasure.

But pleasure itself has multiple components.

There is pleasure experienced during an event.

There is anticipation of future pleasure.

There is motivation to pursue something pleasurable.

These processes are related but not identical.

So a person who does not pursue an enjoyable activity may not necessarily be incapable of experiencing pleasure once the activity begins.

This distinction illustrates why phenomenology and behavioral science need each other.

Observable behavior can tell us what someone does.

First-person reports can tell us something about what the experience is like. [13]

Neither level is automatically sufficient on its own.

---

# Why First-Person Experience Matters to Neuroscience

At first glance, phenomenology and neuroscience may seem to belong to different worlds.

Phenomenology asks:

> **What is experience like?**

Neuroscience asks:

> **What is happening in the nervous system?**

But schizophrenia forces us to connect them.

Suppose a person experiences a voice.

We could measure brain activity.

But brain activity alone does not tell us what the experience means to that person.

Conversely, a detailed description of the experience does not tell us which neurons or circuits produced it.

We therefore need complementary levels of investigation.

One possible framework is:

**Experience**

↓

**Psychopathology**

↓

**Cognition**

↓

**Computation**

↓

**Neural circuits**

↓

**Cells and synapses**

↓

**Molecular biology**

This is **not an established causal hierarchy**.

It is a research framework.

The arrows represent questions:

How does a molecular change influence a circuit?

How does a circuit change alter computation?

How does computation alter cognition?

How does cognition become experience?

And how can changes in experience help us identify the computations and neural processes that generated them?

---

# Two Dangers

Trying to connect experience and biology creates two opposite dangers.

## Reductionism

We might reduce an experience to a biological measurement.

For example:

> "This brain region is different, therefore we have explained the hallucination."

But a correlation is not automatically an explanation.

## Reification

We might take a phenomenological concept and treat it as though it were already a biological mechanism.

For example:

> "Self-disorder causes schizophrenia."

That goes beyond the evidence.

The better approach lies between these extremes.

We need to preserve the richness of experience while searching for measurable mechanisms.

---

# From Experience to Computation

This is where computational psychiatry becomes particularly interesting.

Instead of asking only:

> **Which brain region is abnormal?**

we can ask:

> **What information-processing operation might be different?**

For example:

How does the brain estimate uncertainty?

How does it decide which information deserves attention?

How does it distinguish self-generated from externally generated information?

How does it update beliefs?

How does it assign significance?

How does it estimate the value of an action?

These are computational questions.

And they can potentially connect subjective experience with measurable neural processes.

This gives us a possible research bridge. Computational psychiatry provides one possible way of connecting phenomenological observations with formal models of cognition and neural processing. [14][15]

**Possible research bridge:**

**Experience**

↓

**Psychological process**

↓

**Computation**

↓

**Neural implementation**

Again, this is a framework for investigation, not a claim that every symptom follows this exact pathway.

---

# A Question for the Future: Precision

There is another possibility we will eventually investigate.

Many computational theories of perception and cognition involve the idea that the brain must determine how much **weight** to give different sources of information. [14][15]

How reliable is the sensory signal?

How reliable is the prior belief?

How certain is the prediction?

How important is the prediction error?

These questions are sometimes described using the concept of **precision**.

Precision is an established concept within several computational and statistical frameworks.

But there is an important distinction.

The **Precision Instability Framework (PIF)** is an original hypothesis developed within this research journey.

It proposes that instability in maintaining or allocating precision might contribute to disturbances across multiple domains.

That hypothesis is **not established science**.

We have not demonstrated that precision instability explains schizophrenia.

The purpose of this research journey is partly to determine whether the hypothesis survives comparison with competing explanations.

For now, PIF remains a question.

Not an answer.

---

# What We Still Don't Know

We still do not know exactly how the diverse experiences associated with schizophrenia fit together.

Are hallucinations and delusions produced by related mechanisms?

How does altered salience interact with belief formation?

Are disturbances of self-experience fundamental or secondary?

How does motivation interact with cognition?

Why do some people experience persistent symptoms while others recover substantially?

Why do medications help some symptoms more than others?

Why can two people with the same diagnosis have such different experiences?

And perhaps most importantly:

> **How do changes in biology become changes in subjective experience?**

These questions remain open.

---

# Conclusion: Experience Is Part of the Science

Schizophrenia is often described through diagnostic categories.

But behind every category is a person experiencing something.

A voice.

A belief.

A strange significance attached to an ordinary event.

A thought that feels unfamiliar.

An action that no longer feels entirely self-generated.

A world that feels distant.

A motivation that seems to have disappeared.

These are not merely philosophical curiosities.

They are phenomena that science must explain.

Phenomenology cannot tell us the complete biological cause of schizophrenia.

Neuroscience cannot simply bypass the experience and assume that a brain measurement tells the whole story.

The challenge is to connect the levels.

**Experience → cognition → computation → neural circuits → biology**

And then travel in the other direction:

**Biology → neural dynamics → computation → cognition → experience**

Somewhere within those relationships lies the scientific problem we began with.

Not simply:

> **What is schizophrenia?**

But:

# **How does biology become experience?**

And perhaps the next question is even more specific:

> **How does an ordinary brain generate the extraordinary experience of psychosis?**

That is where our next article begins.

## Scientific Status

**Established:** Schizophrenia involves diverse clinical phenomena including psychosis, cognitive difficulties, and negative symptoms.

**Established research area:** Phenomenological investigation of subjective experience.

**Important research framework:** Self-disorder, minimal self, ipseity, agency, and related phenomenological concepts.

**Influential but contested hypothesis:** Aberrant salience as a mechanism contributing to psychosis.

**Research framework:** Connecting experience, cognition, computation, neural circuits, and biology.

**Proposed hypothesis:** Precision Instability Framework (PIF).

PIF is not an established theory of schizophrenia.
