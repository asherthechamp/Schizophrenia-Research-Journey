# Article 4 — What Is Predictive Processing?

## How Does the Brain Make Sense of Uncertain Information?

Imagine looking at a familiar object in poor lighting.

The sensory information reaching your eyes is incomplete. Parts of the
object may be hidden. The image may be noisy. Yet you can often
recognize what you are looking at almost immediately.

How?

One possibility is that the brain does not simply wait for sensory
information and then construct a perception from scratch.

Instead, it may continuously generate expectations about what it is
likely to encounter—and compare those expectations with incoming
sensory evidence.

This idea lies at the heart of **predictive processing**.

But predictive processing is often described in ways that make it sound
more certain than the evidence warrants.

So what exactly does it mean?

And why has it become so important in neuroscience and computational
psychiatry?

---

# The Brain Has to Infer

The world does not arrive in the brain as a finished description.

Your eyes receive patterns of light.

Your ears receive changes in air pressure.

Your skin receives mechanical and thermal signals.

These signals are transformed by sensory systems before they become
part of perception.

And sensory information is never perfectly complete.

It can be:

- noisy;
- ambiguous;
- incomplete;
- changing;
- distorted by context.

The brain therefore faces a fundamental computational problem:

> **What is most likely causing the sensory information I am receiving?**

This is an inference problem.

And the idea that perception involves inference is much older than
modern predictive-processing theories.

In the nineteenth century, Hermann von Helmholtz described perception
in terms of what became known as **unconscious inference**.

Modern neuroscience has developed much more sophisticated versions of
this basic idea.

One of them is predictive processing.

---

# A Brain That Predicts

At its simplest, predictive processing proposes that the nervous system
can use internal models to generate predictions about incoming sensory
information.

The basic loop can be represented as:

**Prediction**

↓

**Sensory evidence**

↓

**Comparison**

↓

**Prediction error**

↓

**Model updating**

↓

**New prediction**

This does not mean that the brain consciously says:

> "I predict that I am going to see a chair."

Most prediction-related processing is automatic.

The nervous system can learn regularities from experience and use them
to anticipate what is likely to happen next.

---

# Why Would Prediction Be Useful?

Consider listening to someone speak in a noisy room.

The sound reaching your ears may be incomplete.

Yet you can often understand the sentence.

Why?

Because you already know something about:

- language;
- grammar;
- vocabulary;
- the conversation;
- the speaker;
- what is likely to come next.

Your brain does not have to interpret every sound in isolation.

Context constrains interpretation.

The same principle appears in visual perception.

If an object is partially hidden, your brain can often recognize the
whole object.

The sensory evidence does not contain everything.

Prior information helps fill in the interpretation.

This is one reason prediction can be computationally useful.

---

# From Sensory Signals to Hidden Causes

Predictive-processing theories often use the concept of a
**generative model**.

A generative model represents possible relationships between hidden
causes and observable sensory consequences.

For example:

**Object**

↓

**Visual properties**

↓

**Sensory signals**

The brain can work in the opposite direction.

Given sensory information, it can estimate which hidden causes are most
likely.

So perception can be described as:

**Sensory evidence → inference about causes**

rather than simply:

**Sensory evidence → copy of the world**

This distinction is fundamental.

Perception is not necessarily a photograph of reality.

It is an interpretation constrained by evidence.

---

# The Importance of Hierarchy

Predictive-processing models often describe the brain as a hierarchy.

At lower levels, representations may concern relatively detailed
features.

At higher levels, representations can concern increasingly abstract
causes.

A simplified hierarchy might look like:

**Context**

↓

**Objects**

↓

**Shapes**

↓

**Features**

↓

**Sensory signals**

Higher levels can provide predictions about lower levels.

Lower levels can provide information about discrepancies between
predictions and incoming signals.

This creates reciprocal information flow.

---

# Top-Down Meets Bottom-Up

We often describe perception using two directions of processing.

### Bottom-Up

Information travels from sensory systems toward higher-level
representations.

### Top-Down

Higher-level expectations influence lower-level processing.

Predictive processing emphasizes the interaction between these two
directions.

But this is important:

> **Predictive processing does not mean that the brain ignores sensory
> evidence.**

Nor does it mean that perception is simply imagination.

Sensory information remains an essential constraint.

The interesting question is how the brain combines the two.

---

# What Happens When the Prediction Is Wrong?

Suppose you expect to hear one sound.

Instead, something different arrives.

There is a mismatch.

In predictive-processing terminology, this can be described as a
**prediction error**.

Conceptually:

**Prediction error = observed information − predicted information**

The mismatch contains information.

If the current model successfully predicts what happens, little model
revision may be necessary.

If the mismatch is large or important, the system may need to update
its model.

So prediction error can participate in learning.

The cycle becomes:

**Prediction**

→

**Mismatch**

→

**Update**

→

**Better prediction**

This idea will become the subject of our next article.

---

# Prediction Is Not the Same as Imagination

There is a phrase often associated with predictive theories:

> "The brain hallucinates reality."

It is a memorable phrase.

But taken literally, it can be misleading.

The brain does not simply invent whatever reality it wants.

Perception is constrained by sensory evidence.

A better formulation is:

> **The brain constructs perceptual interpretations through the
> interaction of sensory evidence, prior information, context, and
> inference.**

That distinction matters enormously when we begin applying predictive
processing to schizophrenia.

---

# Is Predictive Processing Bayesian?

Predictive processing is frequently discussed alongside Bayesian
inference.

The connection makes sense.

Bayesian inference provides a mathematical framework for combining prior
information with evidence.

In simplified form:

**Posterior belief**

∝

**Likelihood of evidence × Prior belief**

This gives us a mathematical language for reasoning under uncertainty.

Predictive-processing theories can use Bayesian ideas and probabilistic
generative models.

But the terms are not interchangeable.

**Bayesian inference ≠ predictive processing.**

Bayesian inference is a mathematical framework.

Predictive processing is a broader family of computational and
neuroscientific approaches.

---

# What About the Free-Energy Principle?

Predictive processing is also frequently discussed alongside Karl
Friston's **free-energy principle** and **active inference**.

These approaches overlap substantially.

But they should not simply be treated as different names for exactly
the same theory.

Predictive coding, predictive processing, active inference, and the
free-energy principle operate at different theoretical levels and
involve different assumptions.

For this research journey, the important point is simply that they are
related—but not identical.

We will return to these ideas later.

---

# Where Does Precision Enter?

Now we reach a concept that will become increasingly important.

Suppose your senses provide one piece of information, while your prior
expectations provide another.

How should the brain decide which information deserves more weight?

One answer involves **precision**.

In statistical terms, precision is commonly related to the inverse of
variance.

In intuitive terms:

**High precision → greater estimated reliability**

**Low precision → greater uncertainty**

This means that inference is not only about:

> "What information do I have?"

It is also about:

> **"How much confidence should I place in this information?"**

That question will become central later in our research journey.

---

# Prediction and Psychosis

This is where predictive processing becomes particularly interesting
for schizophrenia research.

Psychosis involves disturbances that can affect:

- perception;
- belief;
- salience;
- agency;
- source monitoring;
- learning;
- uncertainty.

Predictive-processing theories provide a computational language for
asking whether some of these disturbances could arise from altered
inference.

For example:

Could an unusual weighting of sensory evidence affect perception?

Could altered expectations influence interpretation?

Could abnormal belief updating contribute to delusions?

Could altered inference about internally generated information
contribute to disturbances of agency or source monitoring?

These are legitimate scientific questions.

But they are still questions.

---

# Predictive Processing Is Not a Theory of Schizophrenia

This distinction is essential.

Predictive processing is **not an established complete explanation of
schizophrenia**.

Schizophrenia is heterogeneous.

It includes multiple symptom dimensions and involves interactions among
many biological, psychological, developmental, and environmental
factors.

There is no established evidence that one predictive-processing
abnormality explains the entire disorder.

Instead, predictive processing offers one possible computational level
at which certain phenomena might be understood.

That is a much more defensible claim.

---

# What About Hallucinations?

Hallucinations have attracted particular attention from
predictive-processing researchers.

One possibility is that hallucinations could involve an altered
relationship between internal expectations and sensory evidence.

But there are several possible explanations.

Some models emphasize:

- overly precise prior expectations;
- reduced influence of sensory evidence;
- altered prediction errors;
- altered precision weighting.

These possibilities are not equivalent.

Therefore we should be cautious about saying:

> "Hallucinations are caused by strong priors."

That is much stronger than the evidence currently allows.

A better scientific question is:

> **How are sensory evidence, prior expectations, uncertainty, and
> their relative weighting altered during hallucination?**

That question can actually be investigated.

---

# And What About Delusions?

Delusions create a somewhat different problem.

A hallucination is generally discussed as a disturbance of perception.

A delusion is a disturbance involving belief.

Computational models of delusions therefore have to consider things such
as:

- belief updating;
- uncertainty;
- learning;
- prediction error;
- prior beliefs;
- evidence weighting;
- salience.

Predictive-processing ideas may provide a bridge between perception and
belief.

But again, no single computational model currently explains all
delusions.

---

# A Scientific Warning

There is a danger when a powerful theory becomes too flexible.

If every observation can be described as:

> "consistent with predictive processing,"

then the theory becomes difficult to distinguish from competing
explanations.

This is why scientific theories need **discriminating predictions**.

A strong model should tell us:

> "If this mechanism is correct, we should observe X rather than Y."

And ideally:

> "If we observe Y instead, this model would be weakened or rejected."

This principle will become increasingly important as we move from
conceptual neuroscience toward computational psychiatry.

---

# What Do We Actually Know?

We can separate several levels of confidence.

### Relatively well supported

The brain is not a passive recorder of sensory information.

Prior knowledge and context can influence perception.

Prediction-related processes occur throughout the nervous system.

Unexpected information can influence learning and behavior.

### Strong theoretical framework

Predictive coding provides useful computational models of hierarchical
processing.

Predictive processing provides an influential framework for thinking
about perception and inference.

### Still under investigation

Exactly how predictive-processing computations are implemented in neural
circuits.

Exactly what particular neural signals represent.

How precision is biologically implemented.

How predictive mechanisms contribute to specific psychiatric symptoms.

### Not established

That predictive processing is the complete theory of brain function.

That predictive processing explains all of schizophrenia.

That hallucinations are simply caused by overly strong priors.

That one computational abnormality explains all psychotic symptoms.

---

# Where Does PIF Fit?

This brings us back to the question that motivated part of this
research journey.

Predictive processing asks:

> **How does a nervous system infer the causes of sensory input using
> predictions, evidence, and uncertainty?**

The Precision Instability Framework asks a narrower question:

> **Could instability in the estimation, maintenance, or allocation of
> precision contribute to disturbances across multiple psychological
> domains?**

These are not the same question.

And PIF should not be presented as something already established by
predictive-processing research.

It is a **proposed research hypothesis**.

To become scientifically useful, it must eventually make predictions
that can be tested against competing explanations.

For example:

What exactly is "precision instability"?

How could it be measured?

Which computational parameters would represent it?

Which neural mechanisms might implement it?

Which symptoms should it predict?

What findings would falsify it?

Those questions come later.

---

# The Bigger Picture

We began with a simple observation:

The sensory information reaching the brain is incomplete.

Yet we experience a coherent world.

Predictive processing offers one way of understanding how this might
happen.

The nervous system may continuously combine:

**Sensory evidence**

+

**Prior information**

+

**Context**

+

**Predictions**

+

**Uncertainty**

to construct perceptual interpretations.

But this does not mean the brain simply invents reality.

It means that perception may be an ongoing process of constrained
inference.

And that creates our next question.

---

# What Happens When the Prediction Is Wrong?

If the brain continuously predicts what it expects to encounter, then
mismatches between prediction and sensory evidence become enormously
important.

They provide information.

They can drive learning.

They can change beliefs.

They can alter future predictions.

In computational terms, these mismatches are often called:

# Prediction Errors

In the next article, we will slow down and examine them carefully.

What exactly is a prediction error?

How might the brain represent one?

How does prediction error differ from surprise?

And could abnormalities in prediction-error processing help explain
some features of psychosis?

That is where the next stage of the journey begins.
