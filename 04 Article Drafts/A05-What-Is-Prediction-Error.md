# What Is Prediction Error?

## How the Brain Learns When Its Expectations Meet Reality

Imagine expecting one thing and encountering another.

You expect the traffic light to remain green.

It turns yellow.

You expect a familiar voice.

The sound is different.

You expect a reward.

Nothing arrives.

Something has changed.

The difference between what we expected and what actually happens can
carry information.

In computational neuroscience, this discrepancy is often described as
a **prediction error**.

The term sounds as though the brain has made a mistake.

But that is not quite what it means.

A prediction error is not necessarily an error in the ordinary sense.

It is a difference between an expectation and an observation.

And that difference can teach the brain something about the world.

---

## From Prediction to Error

In the previous article, we introduced predictive processing.

The basic idea was that perception may involve an interaction between
incoming sensory evidence and predictions generated by the nervous
system.

We can simplify the process:

**Prediction → sensory evidence → comparison → updating → new prediction**

Prediction error occupies the middle of this cycle.

Suppose your brain predicts that a particular sound will continue.

Then the sound suddenly changes.

The incoming sensory information does not match the prediction.

There is now a discrepancy.

That discrepancy can be represented as a prediction error.

In a simple mathematical example:

**Predicted = 10**

**Observed = 12**

The difference is:

**12 − 10 = +2**

Or:

**Predicted = 10**

**Observed = 7**

The difference is:

**7 − 10 = −3**

The exact mathematics becomes much more sophisticated in real
computational models, but the basic intuition is useful:

> **Prediction error describes a mismatch between expectation and
> observation.**

---

## Why Would the Brain Care About a Mismatch?

Because mismatches contain information.

If everything happens exactly as expected, there may be little reason to
change an existing model of the environment.

But if something unexpected happens, our current model may be incomplete.

Imagine walking through your home at night.

You expect the hallway to be empty.

Then you see a person standing there.

That discrepancy immediately matters.

Your brain now has a reason to reconsider its interpretation of the
situation.

Is it a family member?

A stranger?

A coat hanging from a door?

A shadow?

The prediction error does not automatically tell you what the answer
is.

It tells you that your previous expectation is not sufficient.

The system now has to investigate.

---

# Prediction Error as Information

This gives us a deeper way of thinking about prediction error.

It is not simply something the brain tries to eliminate.

It is also information.

An unexpected observation can tell the system:

> "Your current model may not fully explain what is happening."

That information can contribute to learning.

A simplified cycle looks like this:

**Prediction**

↓

**Observation**

↓

**Mismatch**

↓

**Prediction error**

↓

**Model updating**

↓

**New prediction**

This basic computational structure appears in several different
learning and inference frameworks.

But there is an important qualification.

Not every prediction error means that the model should change.

Sometimes the environment is simply noisy.

Sometimes the unexpected observation is unreliable.

Sometimes an unusual event is irrelevant.

The brain therefore faces another problem:

> **How much should this prediction error matter?**

We will return to that question later.

---

# Prediction Error Is Not the Same as Surprise

The words are sometimes used interchangeably in everyday language.

Scientifically, however, we should be more careful.

A prediction error describes a discrepancy between an expectation and
an observation.

Surprise can refer to how unexpected an observation is.

In information theory, surprise is often represented as:

**Surprise = −log P(observation)**

An event with a very low probability can therefore have high
information-theoretic surprise.

But prediction error and surprise are not identical quantities.

Consider an example.

Suppose you predict that a cup contains coffee.

You open it and discover tea.

There is a mismatch between your prediction and the observation.

But the computational significance of that mismatch depends on the
context.

Perhaps someone told you earlier that they had changed the drink.

Perhaps the lighting made the liquid difficult to identify.

Perhaps the cup was not yours.

The same physical discrepancy can therefore have different meanings
depending on the information available to the system.

And subjective surprise—

> "I can't believe that happened!"

—is also not automatically equivalent to a neural prediction-error
signal.

These distinctions matter when we move from psychological concepts to
neural mechanisms.

---

# Not All Prediction Errors Are the Same

This is one of the most important points in this article.

The phrase **prediction error** is used in several scientific
frameworks.

They share a general computational structure, but they do not
necessarily describe the same biological process.

For example, predictive-coding models may describe discrepancies
between predicted and incoming sensory information.

Reinforcement-learning models use **reward prediction error** to
describe discrepancies between expected and received rewards.

These are related ideas.

But they should not simply be collapsed into one mechanism.

---

# Reward Prediction Error

Consider a simple reward-learning example.

You expect to receive 10 points.

Instead, you receive 15.

Your outcome was better than expected.

A simplified reward prediction error is:

**15 − 10 = +5**

Now imagine expecting 10 and receiving 5.

The prediction error is:

**5 − 10 = −5**

In reinforcement learning, these discrepancies can serve as teaching
signals for updating estimates of value.

This is one reason prediction error is so important in computational
models of learning.

And it brings us to dopamine.

---

# Dopamine and Prediction Error

Research on dopamine neurons has provided some of the most influential
evidence for reward-prediction-error-like signaling.

In classic experiments, unexpected rewards can produce strong
dopaminergic responses.

After learning, similar responses can shift toward cues that predict
the reward.

If an expected reward fails to occur, activity can decrease.

These observations are consistent with reinforcement-learning models of
reward prediction error.

But we need to avoid a common oversimplification:

> **Dopamine is not simply "the prediction-error chemical."**

Dopamine neurons are heterogeneous.

Dopamine participates in multiple processes involving motivation,
learning, movement, behavioral selection, and other functions.

Reward prediction error is therefore one important computational
relationship involving dopamine—not a complete description of dopamine
biology.

---

# Sensory Prediction Error

Now return to perception.

Suppose you are listening to music.

Your brain predicts that the melody will continue in a particular way.

Instead, the next note is unexpected.

The incoming auditory information differs from your expectation.

A sensory prediction error can therefore be understood as a mismatch
between predicted and incoming sensory information.

Predictive-coding theories have proposed that such discrepancies can
play an important role in hierarchical perceptual processing.

A simplified conceptual architecture is:

**Higher-level prediction**

↓

**Lower-level processing**

↕

**Sensory evidence**

↓

**Prediction error**

The lower levels provide information about discrepancies, while higher
levels can provide predictions about what is likely to occur.

This is a computational framework.

It should not be interpreted as a literal diagram of individual neurons
or brain regions.

---

# The Importance of Hierarchy

The brain does not make predictions at only one level.

A very simple sensory feature might be unexpected.

But a larger pattern may be perfectly predictable.

Imagine hearing a single unusual sound in a familiar song.

The sound may be unexpected at one level.

But the overall song remains recognizable at another.

This suggests that prediction errors can operate at different levels
and timescales.

A discrepancy does not necessarily mean that the entire model of the
world has failed.

The system can ask:

> What level of my model needs to change?

That is one reason hierarchical predictive-processing models are
interesting.

---

# But How Much Should the Error Matter?

Now we reach an important concept:

**precision.**

Imagine two prediction errors.

The first comes from a very noisy sensor.

The second comes from a highly reliable measurement.

They may have similar magnitudes.

But it would make sense to treat the reliable measurement as more
informative.

This is where precision enters the picture.

In probabilistic models, precision is commonly related to the inverse
of variance:

**Precision ≈ 1 / variance**

Higher precision means that information is estimated to be more
reliable.

Lower precision means greater uncertainty.

A simplified conceptual expression is:

**Precision-weighted prediction error**

**= precision × prediction error**

The important idea is not the exact equation.

It is the weighting.

A large discrepancy from an unreliable source may deserve little
influence.

A smaller discrepancy from a highly reliable source may deserve much
more.

Therefore:

> **The size of a prediction error is not necessarily the same as its
> computational importance.**

---

# Prediction Error and Attention

This creates an interesting connection with attention.

When we pay attention to something, information from that source may
receive greater processing priority.

Some predictive-processing theories describe aspects of attention in
terms of changing the precision assigned to information.

This provides a possible computational relationship among:

**attention**

**uncertainty**

**precision**

**prediction error**

But these concepts should not be treated as synonyms.

Attention is not simply prediction error.

Precision is not simply attention.

Salience is not simply prediction error.

They are related ideas operating at different explanatory levels.

---

# Prediction Error and Psychosis

Now we can approach schizophrenia.

Psychosis involves disturbances in perception, belief, salience,
agency, and the interpretation of experience.

Predictive-processing theories have therefore asked whether altered
inference could contribute to some of these phenomena.

One possibility is that the problem is not simply the existence of
prediction errors.

The problem could involve **how much weight is assigned to them**.

For example, imagine that a system assigns unusually high precision to
certain sensory discrepancies.

Those discrepancies could have a disproportionate influence on
inference.

Alternatively, a system might give excessive weight to prior
expectations and insufficient weight to incoming sensory information.

Different computational abnormalities could therefore produce
different perceptual or cognitive consequences.

---

# Hallucinations

Hallucinations are sometimes described using predictive-processing
language.

One simplified possibility is that internally generated predictions
receive too much influence relative to sensory evidence.

Another possibility involves altered precision assigned to sensory
prediction errors.

Still other models emphasize hierarchical inference, sensory
attenuation, or interactions among several mechanisms.

This is important:

> **There is no established scientific result saying that
> hallucinations are simply prediction errors.**

Hallucinations are complex phenomena.

Predictive processing provides one framework for investigating them.

It does not yet provide a complete explanation.

---

# Delusions

Delusions raise a different computational question.

A delusion is not simply a strange perception.

It involves belief.

This raises questions about how evidence changes beliefs.

What happens when new information conflicts with an existing belief?

Does the system update too strongly?

Too weakly?

Does it assign inappropriate precision to particular evidence?

Does it fail to represent uncertainty appropriately?

These are computational questions that can be investigated using
formal models.

But delusions are heterogeneous.

There is unlikely to be a single computational mechanism that explains
every delusional experience.

---

# Aberrant Salience

Another important concept is **aberrant salience**.

The hypothesis proposes that otherwise neutral events can acquire
inappropriate or excessive significance.

Dopamine has played an important role in this theory.

Prediction-error concepts have also become connected to computational
accounts of salience.

A simplified conceptual chain might be:

**Unexpected event**

↓

**Prediction-error-related processing**

↓

**Dopaminergic signaling**

↓

**Increased significance**

↓

**Aberrant salience**

But this should be understood as a hypothesis, not as an established
linear pathway.

Psychosis cannot simply be reduced to:

**dopamine → prediction error → schizophrenia**

Modern research considers interactions among dopamine, uncertainty,
learning, precision, context, cognition, and individual differences.

---

# What Do We Actually Know?

At this point, it is useful to separate what is relatively well
supported from what remains uncertain.

We have strong conceptual and experimental foundations for the idea that
organisms learn from discrepancies between expected and observed
outcomes.

Prediction-error computations are central to influential reinforcement
learning models.

Dopamine neurons show responses consistent with reward-prediction-error
computations.

Predictive-coding models provide a powerful theoretical framework for
understanding hierarchical inference.

But important questions remain.

Exactly how sensory prediction errors are represented in neural circuits
is still an active research area.

And when researchers observe a neural response that looks like a
prediction error, we cannot automatically conclude that it implements
one specific computational process.

The same observed response may have multiple possible interpretations.

This distinction becomes especially important in psychiatry.

---

# Prediction Error Is Not the Explanation of Schizophrenia

It may be tempting to build a simple story:

The brain predicts.

Prediction errors occur.

Prediction errors become abnormal.

Psychosis results.

But schizophrenia is much more complicated.

It involves multiple symptom domains, developmental processes, genetic
risk factors, environmental influences, neural systems, and
psychological mechanisms.

Predictive processing is therefore better understood as one
computational framework among several.

The interesting scientific question is not:

> "Can predictive processing explain everything?"

It is:

> **"Which specific computational abnormalities can predictive
> processing models explain, and can those predictions be tested?"**

That is a much stronger scientific question.

---

# Where Does PIF Fit?

This brings us to the Precision Instability Framework.

PIF proposes a more specific hypothesis:

Perhaps an important problem is not simply abnormal prediction.

Perhaps it is instability in the maintenance or allocation of
precision.

The conceptual sequence is:

**Prediction**

↓

**Prediction error**

↓

**Precision weighting**

↓

**Updating**

↓

**Perception and belief**

↓

**Experience**

If precision is unstable, the relative influence of evidence and
expectations could potentially fluctuate.

This could provide a possible computational bridge across different
domains:

- perception;
- attention;
- salience;
- belief updating;
- motivation;
- agency.

But this is where we must be especially careful.

**PIF is a proposed research hypothesis.**

It is not an established scientific theory.

Its value will depend on whether it can be operationalized,
measured, tested, compared with alternative explanations, and
potentially falsified.

---

# The Bigger Picture

We began with a simple observation:

We expected one thing.

Something else happened.

The difference matters.

Prediction error gives us a computational language for describing that
difference.

But prediction error alone is not enough.

The brain also needs to estimate:

How reliable is the information?

How important is the discrepancy?

Which level of the model should change?

Should the belief be updated?

Should the discrepancy be ignored?

And perhaps, in psychiatric disorders:

Has the system become unstable in deciding how much different kinds of
information should matter?

These questions take us beyond prediction error.

They take us toward **precision**.

---

# The Next Question

A prediction error tells us that something does not match our
expectation.

But the brain still has to decide:

> **How much should this mismatch matter?**

A noisy signal may deserve little weight.

A highly reliable signal may demand a major update.

So the next question in our journey is:

# What Is Precision?

Because understanding prediction error may be impossible without
understanding how the brain estimates the reliability of information.
