# 9. What Is Attention?

## How does the brain decide what deserves priority?

Imagine sitting in a crowded café.

There are conversations around you.

Cups clink against saucers.

Music plays in the background.

People move through your field of vision.

Your phone vibrates.

Meanwhile, you may also be thinking about something that happened yesterday, worrying about tomorrow, or trying to read the sentence in front of you.

All of this information is potentially available.

But you are not experiencing every piece of it equally.

At one moment, you focus on the person speaking to you.

A moment later, a sudden loud sound captures your attention.

Then you deliberately return to your book.

This simple experience reveals a fundamental problem faced by the brain:

> **The world contains more information than can be given equal priority.**

The brain must constantly decide:

> **What matters right now?**

Article 8 explored one part of that problem: **salience**.

Some events stand out. They seem important, novel, relevant, threatening, rewarding, or meaningful.

But recognizing that something may matter is not quite the same as giving it priority for processing.

That brings us to the next question:

> **What is attention?**

---

# The Problem of Too Much Information

The brain is constantly surrounded by information.

There is information coming from:

- Vision
- Hearing
- Touch
- The body
- Memory
- Emotion
- Thought
- Other people
- The environment

Even when we appear to be doing nothing, the brain is not receiving a single simple stream of information.

It is dealing with a constantly changing and enormously complex environment.

The problem is not simply:

> How do we receive information?

The deeper problem is:

> **How do we prioritize it?**

If every signal received exactly the same processing priority, behavior would quickly become inefficient.

Imagine trying to cross a busy street while giving equal importance to:

- Every passing car
- Every sound
- Every face
- Every building
- Every memory
- Every bodily sensation

You would be overwhelmed.

To act effectively, the brain must select.

Some information must receive greater priority.

Other information must receive less.

That broad family of processes is what we call:

# Attention

---

# Attention Is Not One Single Thing

It is tempting to imagine attention as a kind of mental spotlight.

You point the spotlight at something.

That thing becomes illuminated.

Everything outside the spotlight becomes dark.

This metaphor is useful.

But it can also mislead us.

There is no literal spotlight inside the brain.

And there is no single “attention center” where all attention happens.

Attention is better understood as a **family of processes** involved in:

- Selection
- Prioritization
- Enhancement
- Maintenance
- Reorientation
- Control

Attention can be directed toward:

- A location
- An object
- A feature
- A sound
- A thought
- A memory
- A task

So when we ask:

> **What is attention?**

we are not asking about one single mechanism.

We are asking how the brain selectively gives some information greater priority than competing information.

A useful working definition is:

> **Attention is the selective prioritization of information for enhanced processing.**

---

# Selective Attention: Choosing Among Competitors

One of the most familiar forms of attention is:

# Selective Attention

Imagine being at a crowded party.

Dozens of voices are present.

Yet you can attempt to follow one conversation.

The other voices do not physically disappear.

Your ears still receive sound.

But one stream of information receives greater priority.

Or imagine searching for a friend in a crowd.

There may be hundreds of faces.

You are not treating every face equally.

Your goal helps determine what you are looking for.

Selective attention allows the brain to prioritize some information relative to competing information.

But an important correction is needed.

> **Attending to one thing does not necessarily mean everything else is completely ignored.**

Information outside the focus of attention can still be processed to varying degrees.

The brain does not simply divide the world into:

> Completely processed

and

> Completely absent.

Instead, processing can be graded.

Some information may be:

- Enhanced
- Deprioritized
- Suppressed
- Processed more weakly
- Processed automatically

Attention is therefore not simply a binary switch.

It is part of a dynamic system of priorities.

---

# Attention Can Be Directed by Goals

Suppose you enter an airport looking for someone wearing a red jacket.

Suddenly, the environment becomes different.

You may now be especially sensitive to:

> Red.

Your goal changes what receives priority.

This is often called:

# Top-Down Attention

Top-down attention is guided by internal factors such as:

- Goals
- Expectations
- Intentions
- Knowledge
- Task demands

You are not simply waiting for the environment to determine what you notice.

Your current purpose helps shape what you prioritize.

If you are looking for your friend, faces may become important.

If you are looking for your luggage, bags may become important.

If you are driving, traffic signals may become important.

The same physical environment can therefore produce different patterns of attention depending on:

> **What you are trying to do.**

---

# But the World Can Interrupt You

Now imagine that you are reading a difficult scientific paper.

Your attention is strongly directed toward the text.

Then:

**BANG.**

A sudden loud sound occurs nearby.

Your attention shifts.

You did not necessarily choose to redirect it.

The event captured it.

This is often described as:

# Stimulus-Driven Attention

or:

# Bottom-Up Attention

Incoming events can influence attention through features such as:

- Sudden change
- Movement
- Novelty
- Sensory prominence
- Behavioral relevance

This is important because attention is not completely under voluntary control.

You can try to concentrate.

But the world can interrupt you.

A healthy cognitive system must therefore balance two competing demands.

It must be stable enough to maintain goals.

But flexible enough to respond to important changes.

Too much stability could make behavior rigid.

Too much flexibility could make sustained action impossible.

The brain must somehow balance:

> **Staying focused**

with

> **Remaining responsive.**

This connects directly to Article 7, where we explored how the brain learns when the world is changing.

The same general problem appears again here.

A good cognitive system must know:

> When to stay with its current priorities.

And:

> When to change them.

---

# Top-Down and Bottom-Up Are Not the Whole Story

The distinction between top-down and bottom-up attention is extremely useful.

But we should not make it too simple.

It is tempting to say:

> Top-down attention is voluntary.

> Bottom-up attention is automatic.

Real cognition is more complicated.

Whether something captures attention can depend on:

- Context
- Expectations
- Learning
- Current goals
- Emotional relevance
- Past experience

A sudden sound may capture your attention.

But its effect may be different depending on where you are.

A loud sound in a fireworks display is different from a loud sound in an otherwise quiet room.

The brain does not interpret sensory events in a vacuum.

Attention emerges from an interaction between:

> **What is happening**

and

> **What the organism expects, needs, and is trying to do.**

---

# Attention and Salience: Closely Related, but Not the Same

At this point, we need to return to Article 8.

That article explored:

# Salience

Salience concerns why something stands out as important, relevant, or significant.

Attention concerns the prioritization of information for processing.

The two are closely related.

But they are not identical.

Consider a sudden loud sound.

The sound may be highly salient.

Because it stands out, it may capture attention.

So we might describe the relationship like this:

> **Salience can influence attention.**

But attention can also be directed toward something that is not naturally attention-grabbing.

Imagine a scientist examining a faint pattern in a complicated dataset.

The pattern may be subtle.

It may not automatically capture attention.

Yet the scientist deliberately directs attention toward it because it is relevant to a goal.

This reveals an important distinction.

> **Something can be salient without being the current focus of attention.**

And:

> **Something can receive attention without being inherently salient.**

A useful summary is:

> **Salience concerns significance or priority in relation to the organism.**

> **Attention concerns the selective allocation of processing priority.**

They interact constantly.

But:

> **Salience is not simply another word for attention.**

In fact, theoretical work in computational neuroscience has explicitly argued that attention and salience should be distinguished even though they strongly influence one another. 1

---

# Attention Changes How Information Is Processed

Attention does not merely determine where we look.

It can influence how information is processed.

When information receives attentional priority, processing may become:

- More efficient
- Faster
- More accurate
- More detailed

Attention can influence:

- Perception
- Memory
- Decision-making
- Action

This is one reason attention is so important.

It sits at the intersection of multiple cognitive processes.

You cannot understand attention simply by asking:

> What are you looking at?

Attention can also be involved in:

> What are you listening to?

> What are you thinking about?

> What information are you trying to remember?

> What action are you preparing to perform?

---

# Attention Is Not the Same as Consciousness

Another common confusion is the idea that:

> Attention = Consciousness.

They are clearly related.

When you attend strongly to something, you will often become consciously aware of it.

But the two concepts should not simply be collapsed into one.

Attention concerns:

> **Prioritization and selection.**

Consciousness concerns:

> **Subjective experience and awareness.**

Experimental work has shown that these processes can sometimes be dissociated. In other words, the relationship between what is attended and what is consciously experienced is more complicated than a simple one-to-one equation. 2

This raises a fascinating question.

If attention selects information:

> **How does some information become part of conscious experience?**

We will return to that question later in the journey.

For now, the important point is simple:

> **Attention and consciousness interact, but they are not identical concepts.**

---

# Attention and the Predictive Brain

Our earlier articles introduced a different way of thinking about the brain.

The brain does not simply wait for sensory information.

It actively generates predictions.

It compares those predictions with incoming information.

Differences between expectation and observation can generate:

# Prediction Errors

The brain must then determine:

> Which errors matter?

This is where our earlier discussion of:

# Precision

becomes relevant.

In some predictive-processing theories, attention is related to the weighting of information according to its expected precision or reliability.

In very simplified terms:

If the brain expects a particular source of information to be reliable, that information may receive greater influence.

This has led some theorists to describe attention partly as a form of:

> **Precision-dependent gain control.**

But we need to be scientifically careful.

This does **not** mean:

> Attention is precision.

It means that within certain computational frameworks, aspects of attentional prioritization can be modeled through the estimation and modulation of expected precision.

So we should preserve the distinction:

> **Attention ≠ Precision**

But:

> **Attention and precision may be connected within predictive-processing theories.**

This is one of the reasons Article 9 belongs naturally after Articles 4, 5, 6, and 7.

The journey has gradually been building toward a deeper question:

> **How does the brain decide which information should have influence?**

Prediction.

Prediction error.

Precision.

Learning.

Salience.

Attention.

These are not identical processes.

But they interact.

---

# Attention Is Distributed Across the Brain

There is no single place in the brain that can simply be called:

> “The attention center.”

Attention involves coordinated activity across distributed systems.

Research on attention has identified important roles for interactions involving:

- Frontal systems
- Parietal systems
- Sensory systems
- Subcortical structures

Different forms of attention can involve partially overlapping systems.

This is another reason we should avoid simplistic explanations.

The question is not:

> Which one part of the brain creates attention?

A better question is:

> **How do multiple brain systems coordinate to prioritize information and behavior?**

That question will become increasingly important later in the Schizophrenia Research Journey, when we explore brain networks and large-scale coordination.

---

# Attention in Schizophrenia

Attention has been studied in schizophrenia for decades.

This is unsurprising.

Schizophrenia can involve experiences that raise profound questions about:

- What information receives priority
- What captures attention
- How distractions are managed
- How goals guide cognition
- How internal and external information compete

But we must avoid a simplistic conclusion.

It would be inaccurate to say:

> **Schizophrenia is an attention disorder.**

It is not.

Schizophrenia is a complex and heterogeneous condition involving different combinations of:

- Psychotic experiences
- Cognitive changes
- Negative symptoms
- Changes in motivation
- Alterations in perception
- Changes in self-experience

Attention is only one part of this much larger picture.

Even within attention itself, the story is complicated.

Research distinguishes multiple components, including:

- Global alertness
- Attentional control
- Attentional selection

And findings can differ depending on which component is measured. A major contemporary review even argues that some people with schizophrenia may show unusually narrow and intense allocation of attention—sometimes described as **hyperfocusing**—rather than simply having “too little attention.” 3

This is an important correction to the simplistic idea of a universal attention deficit.

Sometimes the deeper question may be:

> **Where is attention being directed?**

rather than simply:

> **How much attention does a person have?**

---

# Sustaining Attention Is Also More Complicated Than It Sounds

Consider:

# Sustained Attention

This refers to maintaining attentional engagement over time.

It is important for tasks such as:

- Monitoring
- Vigilance
- Detecting rare events
- Maintaining task engagement

For many years, sustained-attention difficulties have been considered important in schizophrenia research.

But even here, the evidence is more complicated than a simple statement that people with schizophrenia “cannot sustain attention.”

A systematic review found that conclusions depended partly on how sustained attention was measured. Studies assessing overall performance often found deficits, while measures examining decline over time could produce different results. 4

This teaches us an important scientific lesson:

> **A psychological label is not a mechanism.**

Saying someone has an “attention problem” does not automatically tell us:

- What process is altered
- Why it is altered
- Which brain systems are involved
- Whether the same problem occurs in every situation

The same behavioral difficulty can potentially arise through different underlying mechanisms.

---

# Attention, Salience, and Psychotic Experience

Now we can begin to see an important connection between Article 8 and Article 9.

Imagine a sequence like this:

> **An unexpected event occurs.**

↓

> **The event becomes unusually significant.**

↓

> **Attention is captured.**

↓

> **The person searches for an explanation.**

↓

> **Meaning is constructed.**

This is a potentially useful conceptual model.

But it must not be treated as a complete explanation of psychosis.

Different people can develop similar experiences through different pathways.

And the brain mechanisms involved are far more complex than a simple chain of events.

Still, the interaction between:

- Prediction
- Prediction error
- Precision
- Salience
- Attention
- Meaning

is becoming increasingly important in computational approaches to psychosis. Contemporary reviews of predictive processing continue to investigate how disruptions at different levels of inference might relate to psychotic symptoms. 5

This is one reason our journey is taking these concepts one step at a time.

We are building a vocabulary before attempting to explain the disorder.

# Attention Is a Problem of Priorities

Perhaps the simplest way to understand attention is this:

The brain must constantly answer:

> **What should receive priority right now?**

The answer can depend on:

- What you want
- What you expect
- What suddenly changes
- What is dangerous
- What is rewarding
- What is relevant
- What you have learned
- What your body needs

Attention therefore sits at the intersection of:

> **The world**

and

> **The organism's goals.**

It is where incoming information meets:

- Expectation
- Motivation
- Context
- Action

The brain does not simply observe everything equally.

It continually organizes a hierarchy of priorities.

And those priorities can change from moment to moment.

---

# But Selection Creates Another Problem

If attention selects some information for priority, what happens to everything else?

The brain cannot simply enhance everything.

Selection requires competition.

And competition requires some form of:

- Suppression
- Filtering
- Ignoring
- Deprioritization

This brings us to the next question in the Schizophrenia Research Journey.

Attention asks:

> **What should I prioritize?**

The next problem is:

> **What should I ignore?**

That question takes us to:

# Article 10 — How Does the Brain Decide What to Ignore?

The brain must constantly protect itself from informational overload.

But what happens when filtering fails?

What happens when irrelevant information continues competing for attention?

And what might this tell us about experiences of cognitive overload, distractibility, perception, and schizophrenia?
