# 13. What Is Consciousness — and What Is the Self?

## How does experience become conscious and organized from a particular point of view?

After exploring prediction, salience, attention, filtering, inhibition, and cognitive control, we have reached a deeper question:

**How does experience become something that is not only conscious, but experienced from a particular point of view?**

The brain does not merely process information about the world.

It processes sensory signals, predicts what may be happening, selects what matters, suppresses competing information, maintains goals, monitors errors, and adapts behavior.

But somewhere within this continuous activity lies something fundamentally different:

**experience itself.**

We see.

We hear.

We feel.

We think.

We remember.

We imagine.

We experience the world as happening.

And normally, these experiences do not feel like anonymous information being processed somewhere in the brain.

They feel as though they are happening **to someone**.

They are experienced from **here**.

They are experienced as **mine**.

This brings two enormous scientific questions together:

> **What is consciousness?**

and

> **What is the self?**

These questions are related, but they are not the same.

Understanding the distinction is essential for understanding the mind—and eventually, schizophrenia.

---

# 1. From Cognitive Control to Conscious Experience

In the previous two articles, we examined increasingly sophisticated forms of regulation.

Attention asks:

> **What deserves priority?**

Filtering asks:

> **What should receive less priority?**

Inhibition asks:

> **What should be constrained or stopped?**

Cognitive control asks:

> **How should thought and behavior remain organized around goals?**

These processes help organize neural activity.

But organization alone does not answer another question:

> **What is it like to experience the result?**

A person can perform information-processing operations without necessarily having conscious access to every piece of information involved.

This creates a crucial conceptual transition:

**Control → Consciousness → Subjective Experience → Self**

The question is no longer simply how the brain regulates information.

It is how regulated neural activity becomes part of a lived experience.

---

# 2. What Is Consciousness?

Consciousness is one of the most difficult concepts in neuroscience because it refers to something that is simultaneously biological, computational, psychological, and phenomenological.

At its most basic level, consciousness concerns **subjective experience**.

There is something it is like to see a red object.

There is something it is like to hear music.

There is something it is like to feel pain.

There is something it is like to remember a childhood event.

There is something it is like to imagine tomorrow.

The important point is that consciousness is not simply equivalent to neural activity.

Neural activity is necessary for ordinary human consciousness, but the scientific problem is to understand how particular patterns of neural activity relate to particular forms of experience.

This is why consciousness science asks questions such as:

- Which neural processes are associated with conscious experience?
- Which information becomes consciously accessible?
- Why do some stimuli reach awareness while others do not?
- What determines the contents of experience?
- How does the brain maintain a conscious state?
- Why does experience have a first-person character?

There is currently no universally accepted answer to all of these questions.

---

# 3. Conscious and Unconscious Processing

A major discovery in cognitive neuroscience is that the brain can process information without that information necessarily becoming conscious.

Stimuli can influence behavior without producing ordinary conscious awareness.

The brain can extract features.

Detect patterns.

Prepare responses.

Activate memories.

Process emotional significance.

And influence decisions.

All without the person necessarily being consciously aware of every underlying operation.

This means:

**Information processing ≠ consciousness.**

The distinction is important because earlier articles in this journey focused heavily on information processing.

Prediction involves information processing.

Attention involves information processing.

Filtering involves information processing.

Inhibition involves information processing.

Cognitive control involves information processing.

But consciousness asks a different question:

> **When does processing become part of subjective experience?**

---

# 4. Access Consciousness and Phenomenal Consciousness

One influential distinction separates two aspects of consciousness.

## Access consciousness

Information is consciously accessible when it can become available for functions such as:

- reasoning
- decision-making
- verbal report
- working memory
- deliberate action
- flexible cognitive control

In this sense, conscious information is information that can participate in a broad range of cognitive operations.

## Phenomenal consciousness

Phenomenal consciousness concerns the subjective character of experience.

It asks:

> **What is the experience actually like?**

Seeing blue is not merely having information corresponding to a wavelength.

Pain is not merely detecting tissue damage.

A melody is not merely processing acoustic frequencies.

The distinction between access and phenomenal consciousness has become one of the central organizing ideas in consciousness research.

But it also reveals why consciousness is so difficult to explain.

Even if we explain how information becomes globally available, we may still ask:

> Why does that availability have a subjective character?

---

# 5. Why Attention Is Not Consciousness

The previous articles focused extensively on attention.

Attention prioritizes information.

Consciousness concerns experience.

These processes interact, but they should not be treated as identical.

We can attend to something without necessarily having a rich conscious experience of every aspect of it.

Conversely, some experiences can enter consciousness without being the primary target of deliberate attention.

This distinction matters for schizophrenia research.

If someone reports an unusually intense experience, it does not automatically follow that their attention mechanism is responsible.

Likewise, an attentional abnormality does not automatically explain the entire structure of conscious experience.

The relationship is more complicated.

A useful distinction is:

**Attention asks: What should receive priority?**

**Consciousness asks: What is experienced?**

---

# 6. The Global Workspace Idea

One influential family of theories proposes that conscious information becomes broadly available across distributed cognitive systems.

The basic idea is often described using the metaphor of a **global workspace**.

Many processes may operate locally and outside conscious awareness.

But when particular information gains sufficient access to a broader network, it becomes available for multiple cognitive functions.

This can help explain why conscious information can influence:

- memory
- reasoning
- decision-making
- language
- planning
- voluntary behavior

Global workspace approaches have generated substantial empirical research.

But they are theories of consciousness, not established final explanations.

A theory can explain important functional properties of conscious access without necessarily solving every aspect of phenomenal experience.

This distinction will remain important throughout our journey.

---

# 7. Higher-Order Theories

Another family of theories proposes that consciousness depends, in some form, on representations of one's own mental states.

The basic idea is that a mental representation may become conscious when the system represents itself as being in that state.

For example:

A visual system processes a stimulus.

A higher-order representation represents that the organism is seeing something.

The details vary considerably across higher-order theories, and the scientific debate remains active.

But these theories introduce an important concept for our journey:

> **The brain can represent not only the world, but aspects of its own processing.**

That becomes especially interesting when we later consider:

- self-reference
- metacognition
- hyperreflexivity
- internally generated thoughts
- self-awareness
- disturbed self-experience

However, higher-order representation should not simply be equated with the self.

A representation of a mental state is not automatically a complete theory of personal identity.

---

# 8. Recurrent Processing

Another major approach emphasizes recurrent interactions within the brain.

Rather than consciousness being produced by a single forward sweep of information processing, conscious perception may depend on interactions in which neural signals are repeatedly exchanged between levels or regions.

This idea draws attention to the temporal dynamics of neural processing.

The brain is not simply:

**input → output**

It is a continuously interacting system.

Signals move forward.

Signals move backward.

Representations are updated.

Predictions interact with incoming information.

Higher-level and lower-level processes influence one another.

Recurrent processing theories therefore emphasize the importance of feedback and ongoing neural interaction.

Again, this is an influential theoretical approach rather than a universally established final answer.

---

# 9. Integrated Information Theory

Integrated Information Theory, or IIT, begins from a different direction.

Instead of asking only how information becomes globally available, IIT attempts to characterize consciousness in terms of the structure of information generated by a system.

Its central idea is that consciousness is associated with information that is both:

- differentiated, and
- integrated.

The theory has generated extensive theoretical and empirical debate.

It is particularly important because it attempts to provide a principled framework for relating physical systems to properties of experience.

But IIT is not universally accepted, and major disagreements remain about both its assumptions and its empirical implications.

For our purposes, the important lesson is broader:

> **Consciousness can be studied using multiple theoretical frameworks, and these frameworks emphasize different explanatory principles.**

---

# 10. Predictive Processing and Conscious Experience

Predictive processing provides another important perspective.

The brain continuously generates predictions about incoming signals and updates those predictions when sensory evidence differs from expectation.

This framework can help explain perception as an interaction between:

**prior expectations + incoming sensory evidence**

rather than as passive registration of the external world.

It also provides a possible bridge between several topics in our journey:

**Prediction → Prediction Error → Precision → Attention → Perception → Conscious Experience**

But predictive processing should not be treated as a complete or universally accepted theory of consciousness.

It is a broad computational framework that can be applied to many aspects of perception and cognition.

The fact that a framework can describe conscious experience computationally does not by itself solve the deeper question of why experience exists.

---

# 11. Comparing Theories of Consciousness

The major theories of consciousness emphasize different questions.

| Approach | Central emphasis |
|---|---|
| Global Workspace | Broad availability of information |
| Higher-Order Theories | Representation of one's own mental states |
| Recurrent Processing | Feedback and recurrent neural interactions |
| Integrated Information Theory | Integrated and differentiated information |
| Predictive Processing | Prediction, inference, and model updating |

These theories are not simply interchangeable.

They make different assumptions about what needs to be explained.

Some emphasize cognitive access.

Some emphasize representation.

Some emphasize neural dynamics.

Some emphasize information structure.

Some emphasize inference.

The scientific challenge is therefore not merely to choose a favorite theory.

It is to determine:

> **Which observations does each theory explain, what does it predict, and where does it fail?**

---

# 12. Consciousness Science Is Still an Open Problem

Consciousness research has made enormous progress.

Researchers can manipulate awareness experimentally.

They can compare conscious and unconscious processing.

They can investigate anesthesia, sleep, neurological disorders, perception, metacognition, and neural correlates of consciousness.

Yet major theoretical disagreements remain.

This is not a weakness of science.

It is a feature of a field studying one of the most difficult phenomena we know.

The important scientific attitude is therefore:

> **Distinguish what has been measured from what has been inferred.**

A neural correlate of consciousness is not automatically a neural cause of consciousness.

A computational model is not automatically a biological mechanism.

A phenomenological description is not automatically a neural explanation.

And a compelling theory is not automatically a settled theory.

---

# 13. The Hard Problem

The deepest philosophical challenge is often called the **hard problem of consciousness**.

Science can investigate many questions about consciousness:

- How does the brain discriminate stimuli?
- How does information become globally accessible?
- How are decisions made?
- How is attention allocated?
- How are memories formed?
- How are reports generated?

These are sometimes called the "easy problems" because they concern functions that can, in principle, be studied through ordinary scientific methods.

The hard problem asks something different:

> **Why should any of these physical processes be accompanied by subjective experience at all?**

Why should neural activity feel like anything?

Why should red look like red?

Why should pain hurt?

Why should music sound like something?

Neuroscience can increasingly describe relationships between brain activity and experience.

Whether those descriptions completely explain why experience exists remains a deeper philosophical question.

---

# 14. Consciousness Is Not the Self

Now we reach the second major question.

If consciousness concerns experience, is consciousness the same thing as the self?

Not necessarily.

A person can be conscious without explicitly thinking:

> "I am myself."

You can become absorbed in music.

You can become immersed in a landscape.

You can experience a sudden sensation before identifying its source.

You can dream without constructing a coherent autobiographical identity.

Consciousness therefore does not require a fully developed narrative self.

But ordinary conscious experience usually has another property.

It feels like experience is happening:

**from here.**

That brings us to subjectivity.

---

# 15. What Makes Experience Feel Like It Is Happening From Here?

Consider the difference between these two descriptions:

> "The visual system is processing a red object."

and

> "I am seeing something red."

The first describes a process.

The second describes an experience.

The experience appears organized around a point of view.

There is a perspective from which the world is encountered.

This does not mean that the brain contains a tiny observer sitting somewhere inside it.

There is no need for a little person inside the brain watching neural activity.

Instead, the first-person character of experience may emerge from the organization of perception, embodiment, memory, interoception, action, and self-related processing.

The self may therefore be better understood as a **layered organization of experience** rather than as a single object in the brain.

---

# 16. From Consciousness to Subjectivity

Subjectivity refers to the first-person character of experience.

The world is not merely represented.

It is experienced from a perspective.

This introduces a distinction:

**Consciousness = there is experience.**

**Subjectivity = experience is organized from a particular point of view.**

The distinction is subtle but extremely important.

It allows us to ask a more precise question:

> What makes an experience feel like it is happening to me?

That question brings us toward the concept of **mineness**.

---

# 17. Mineness

Mineness is the sense that an experience is one's own.

If you feel pain, the pain normally feels like **your pain**.

If you see an object, the visual experience normally feels like **your experience**.

If a thought occurs, it normally feels like **your thought**.

This does not require an explicit verbal judgment.

You do not normally need to say:

> "This thought belongs to me."

The experience simply arrives with a sense of ownership.

This is one reason the self may be more fundamental than autobiographical identity.

Before asking:

> "Who am I?"

there may already be a more basic structure:

> **"This experience is happening here, as mine."**

---

# 18. The Self May Have Multiple Layers

The word "self" can refer to many different things.

It can mean:

- the embodied organism
- the first-person perspective
- the sense of ownership
- the sense of agency
- autobiographical memory
- personality
- social identity
- narrative identity
- beliefs about oneself
- one's life story

These layers should not automatically be treated as one mechanism.

A useful conceptual distinction is:

**Minimal self**

→ immediate first-person experience

**Embodied self**

→ experience organized around the living body

**Agentive self**

→ experience of initiating or controlling actions

**Narrative self**

→ autobiographical identity and personal story

**Social self**

→ identity as represented in relation to other people

This layered view helps us avoid the mistake of searching for a single "self center" in the brain.

---

# 19. Self-Reference and Self-Experience

The brain can represent itself.

We can think about our own thoughts.

Evaluate our own decisions.

Remember our own past.

Imagine our future selves.

Monitor our own performance.

Ask what other people think about us.

These processes involve self-reference and metacognition.

But self-reference is not necessarily the same thing as the minimal self.

A person can explicitly think about themselves while also experiencing a more basic sense of being a subject.

This distinction becomes particularly important when studying unusual experiences of thought.

A thought can be experienced as:

- self-generated
- surprising
- intrusive
- unfamiliar
- externally caused
- unusually important

The content of the thought and the person's experience of its ownership are therefore separable questions.

---

# 20. Why This Matters for Schizophrenia

This distinction becomes especially important in schizophrenia.

Schizophrenia is not simply a disorder of consciousness.

Nor is it simply a disorder of the self.

It is a heterogeneous condition involving multiple domains of cognition, perception, motivation, affect, behavior, and experience.

But some people with schizophrenia report experiences that directly concern the structure of selfhood.

Examples described in phenomenological research include:

- disturbances in the sense of being the subject of experience
- unusual self-awareness
- excessive self-consciousness
- experiences of thoughts becoming unusually salient
- disturbances in the boundary between self and world
- altered experiences of agency
- feelings of alienness or detachment
- hyperreflexive awareness of normally implicit processes

These experiences are important because they suggest that the organization of subjective experience itself can become unusual.

---

# 21. From Consciousness to Self-Disturbance

If consciousness normally provides an ongoing field of experience, and that experience normally has a first-person organization, then disturbances in selfhood may involve changes in how this organization operates.

This does not mean that schizophrenia has one single self mechanism that becomes "broken."

The evidence is much more complicated.

Different people can experience different forms of disturbance.

Some may experience excessive self-monitoring.

Others may experience altered agency.

Others may experience unusual body-related experiences.

Others may experience changes in how thoughts feel related to the self.

The important question is therefore not:

> "Is the self broken?"

A better scientific question is:

> **Which components of self-experience are altered, under what conditions, and through which mechanisms?**

---

# 22. Hyperreflexivity

One influential phenomenological concept is **hyperreflexivity**.

The term refers broadly to an unusual tendency for normally implicit aspects of experience to become unusually explicit or object-like in awareness.

Normally, we use our bodies without continuously monitoring every aspect of bodily experience.

We speak without consciously inspecting every step involved in producing speech.

We think without normally watching each thought as though it were an external object.

But if normally background processes become unusually salient to awareness, the relationship between experience and self-monitoring may change.

This concept has been particularly influential in phenomenological accounts of schizophrenia.

However, hyperreflexivity should not be treated as a complete explanation of schizophrenia.

It is one conceptual tool for describing particular forms of experience.

---

# 23. Diminished Self-Affection

Another phenomenological concept concerns **self-affection**.

Self-affection refers broadly to the immediate sense of being present as a subject of experience.

It is not the same as self-esteem.

It is not simply liking oneself.

Rather, it concerns the basic sense that experience is intrinsically one's own.

Some phenomenological accounts of schizophrenia propose that this basic self-presence can become unstable or diminished.

Again, this is a theoretical and phenomenological framework, not a complete biological explanation.

Its importance lies in directing attention toward something that conventional cognitive tasks may not capture easily:

> **the structure of lived experience itself.**

---

# 24. The Grip on the World

Another important idea is that ordinary experience gives us an immediate practical grip on the world.

We usually do not need to consciously reconstruct every aspect of reality before acting.

We simply find ourselves already situated within a meaningful environment.

A chair is something to sit on.

A door is something to open.

A person's expression is something to interpret.

A sound may immediately demand attention.

The world is encountered as meaningful before we explicitly analyze it.

Phenomenological approaches to schizophrenia have explored how this ordinary "grip" on the world can become altered.

The experience may become unusually reflective, uncertain, fragmented, or detached.

This provides another bridge between:

**self → world → meaning → action**

---

# 25. Consciousness and Internally Generated Experience

So far, we have mostly discussed experience of the external world.

But consciousness also contains internally generated material.

We remember.

Imagine.

Plan.

Daydream.

Rehearse conversations.

Generate explanations.

Experience emotions.

Produce spontaneous thoughts.

These processes are particularly important for understanding schizophrenia.

Some unusual experiences in schizophrenia involve internally generated mental events that are experienced in unusual ways.

A thought may feel unexpectedly vivid.

An image may feel unusually significant.

An internal verbal event may feel less self-generated than expected.

A memory may acquire unusual emotional or perceptual force.

The crucial scientific question is not simply whether the brain generated the experience internally.

The brain generates all ordinary thoughts internally too.

The question is:

> **How does the brain determine the source, ownership, significance, and agency of internally generated experience?**

That question will become central later in this journey.

---

# 26. Consciousness, Self, and Agency

At this point, three concepts must be kept separate.

**Consciousness**

> There is subjective experience.

**Self**

> Experience is organized from a first-person perspective and can carry a sense of mineness.

**Agency**

> An action or event is experienced as generated or controlled by oneself.

These concepts overlap.

But they are not identical.

You can consciously experience something without causing it.

You can experience ownership of your body without deliberately controlling every movement.

You can consciously observe an event without feeling that you caused it.

This gives us an important conceptual map:

**Consciousness → Subjectivity → Mineness → Agency**

The relationships among these components are complex.

Understanding them requires moving beyond the question of consciousness alone.

---

# 27. A Neurophenomenological Bridge

This is where neuroscience and phenomenology need to meet.

Neuroscience can measure:

- neural activity
- connectivity
- oscillations
- network dynamics
- sensory processing
- behavior
- physiological signals

Phenomenology can describe:

- what an experience is like
- how the self is experienced
- how the world appears
- how agency feels
- how thoughts are experienced
- how bodily presence changes

Neither perspective is sufficient by itself.

A neural measurement does not automatically tell us what an experience means.

A phenomenological description does not automatically identify its neural mechanism.

The scientific challenge is to connect them carefully.

This is the basic idea behind a **neurophenomenological bridge**:

**First-person description ↔ computational characterization ↔ neural measurement**

Such a bridge could be especially valuable in psychiatry because psychiatric symptoms are fundamentally experiential.

---

# 28. A Computational Perspective

The computational perspective developed throughout this journey can now be extended.

Earlier we considered:

**Prediction**

**Precision**

**Attention**

**Filtering**

**Inhibition**

**Cognitive Control**

Now we can ask:

> How might these processes contribute to the organization of conscious experience?

A simplified conceptual architecture might look like:

**Sensory and Internal Signals**
↓
**Prediction and Inference**
↓
**Precision Weighting**
↓
**Attention and Selection**
↓
**Cognitive Control**
↓
**Integrated Representations**
↓
**Conscious Experience**
↓
**Self-Related Interpretation**
↓
**Action and Agency**

This is not a complete theory of consciousness.

It is a research architecture.

Its value is that it lets us connect different levels of explanation.

---

# 29. Consciousness and Cognitive Control

Cognitive control also interacts with consciousness in important ways.

Conscious goals can guide behavior.

Working memory can maintain task-relevant information.

Attention can prioritize particular contents.

Inhibition can constrain competing responses.

Monitoring can detect errors.

But consciousness is not reducible to cognitive control.

A person can consciously experience something without actively controlling it.

Likewise, some forms of control can operate without full conscious awareness.

The relationship is therefore better represented as:

**Consciousness ↔ Cognitive Control**

rather than:

**Consciousness = Cognitive Control**

This distinction preserves the conceptual progression of our journey.

---

# 30. The Self Is Not Yet Fully Explained

We have now moved from:

**information processing**

to

**conscious experience**

to

**subjectivity**

to

**selfhood**

But we have not yet explained the self.

We have only decomposed the question.

The self may involve:

- first-person perspective
- mineness
- embodiment
- agency
- self-reference
- memory
- narrative identity
- social identity

These components may depend on partially distinct mechanisms.

The important lesson is therefore:

> **The self may be an organized process rather than a single entity.**

This idea does not make the self unreal.

It makes the scientific problem more precise.

---

# 31. From Consciousness to the Architecture of Thought

We began this article with cognitive control.

Control organizes behavior around goals.

Consciousness introduces subjective experience.

Subjectivity introduces a first-person perspective.

The self organizes aspects of experience around a sense of mineness, embodiment, and agency.

This gives us a larger conceptual progression:

**World**
↓
**Attention**
↓
**Filtering**
↓
**Inhibition**
↓
**Cognitive Control**
↓
**Conscious Experience**
↓
**First-Person Perspective**

**Self–World Relationship**

And now we are approaching one of the central questions of schizophrenia research:

> **What happens when this normally stable organization of self-experience becomes unstable?**

---

# 32. Why This Matters for the Schizophrenia Research Journey

The goal of this journey is not to reduce schizophrenia to one neurotransmitter, one brain region, one network, or one computational variable.

Instead, we are gradually building a layered scientific model.

At the level of experience:

**What does the person experience?**

At the cognitive level:

**How is information selected, interpreted, remembered, and controlled?**

At the computational level:

**What kind of inference, prediction, precision, or control process could generate the observed pattern?**

At the neural level:

**What brain systems implement those computations?**

And at the behavioral level:

**How does the resulting organization influence action and adaptation?**

The self sits at an unusual intersection of all these levels.

It is experienced phenomenologically.

Represented cognitively.

Modeled computationally.

Implemented neurally.

And expressed behaviorally.

That makes it especially important for computational psychiatry.

---

# 33. The Larger Question

The question of consciousness initially appears to be a question about awareness.

But it becomes progressively deeper.

First:

> **What is being processed?**

Then:

> **What becomes conscious?**

Then:

> **What is the experience like?**

Then:

> **From whose point of view is the experience organized?**

Then:

> **Why does it feel like mine?**

Then:

> **How do I experience myself as the source of action?**

And finally:

> **What happens when these relationships become unstable?**

These questions take us from consciousness science directly into the phenomenology and computational neuroscience of schizophrenia.

---

# 34. The Transition to Article 14

Article 13 has taken us from consciousness to the self.

But the self is still too broad.

If we want to understand self-disturbance, we need to identify what may be more fundamental than autobiographical identity, personality, or social identity.

Before the story of who I am, there may be something simpler:

> **I am here.**

Before the narrative:

> "This is my life."

there may be a more basic organization:

> **This experience is happening to me.**

Before the explicit judgment:

> "I caused this action."

there may be an immediate sense of being the subject of experience and action.

This brings us to the concept of the **minimal self**.

Article 14 will therefore ask:

> **What is the minimal self—and how does agency emerge from it?**

We will examine:

- first-person perspective
- mineness
- embodiment
- body ownership
- agency
- self-location
- self-identification
- action ownership
- the relationship between perception and action
- the sense of being the author of one's own behavior
- and what happens when these normally integrated processes become unstable.

The question will become increasingly precise:

**What is the smallest structure that makes an experience feel like it belongs to a subject?**

And from there:

**What makes an action feel like something I did?**

---

# Conclusion

Consciousness is not simply the brain processing information.

It concerns the presence and organization of subjective experience.

Science has developed multiple theories to explain different aspects of consciousness, including global availability, higher-order representation, recurrent processing, integrated information, and predictive inference.

None currently provides universally accepted closure on the entire problem.

But consciousness is only the beginning.

Ordinary conscious experience is normally organized from a particular point of view.

It feels embodied.

It feels like something is happening here.

It feels like something is happening to me.

Experiences normally carry a sense of mineness.

Actions can carry a sense of agency.

Together, these features form part of what we call the self.

The self, however, is not necessarily a single object or mechanism.

It may be a layered organization involving first-person perspective, embodiment, ownership, agency, memory, self-reference, and narrative identity.

This distinction matters profoundly for schizophrenia research.

Some experiences associated with schizophrenia appear to involve unusual changes in the organization of self-experience itself.

Understanding those experiences requires connecting phenomenology with cognitive science, computational models, and neuroscience without collapsing one level into another.

The scientific journey therefore continues:

**Consciousness → Subjectivity → Minimal Self → Agency → Self-Disturbance**

And that brings us to the next question:

> **What is the minimal self—and what makes an action feel like my own?**

That is where we go next.
