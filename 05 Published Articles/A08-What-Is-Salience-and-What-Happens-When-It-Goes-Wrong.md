# What Is Salience—and What Happens When It Goes Wrong?

Imagine walking through a crowded street.

Cars move past you. People are talking. Someone is carrying a brightly colored bag. A dog barks in the distance. Your phone vibrates. You notice the sound of your name.

Your brain cannot process every piece of information with equal importance.

It has to decide what deserves priority.

Some things are ignored.

Some things capture your attention.

Some things feel important.

And some things become impossible to ignore.

This problem is closely related to a concept called **salience**.

But salience is more complicated than it first appears.

It is not simply another word for attention.

It is not simply another word for surprise.

It is not simply another word for dopamine.

And understanding the difference matters—especially when we begin asking what may happen when ordinary events acquire an unusual degree of significance.

## The Brain's Prioritization Problem

At every moment, the brain is surrounded by far more information than it can process equally.

Light enters the eyes.

Sounds enter the ears.

The body generates internal signals.

Memories become active.

Thoughts appear.

Predictions are constantly being compared with incoming evidence.

The brain therefore faces a fundamental problem:

> What should matter right now?

A sudden sound may deserve immediate attention.

A familiar background noise may be ignored.

The smell of food may become important when you are hungry.

A message from someone you care about may immediately capture your attention.

The importance of information depends not only on what is happening in the outside world.

It also depends on:

- context;
- goals;
- expectations;
- internal state;
- motivation.

Salience is one of the concepts scientists use to think about this problem of prioritization.

## What Does "Salience" Mean?

Broadly speaking, salience refers to the degree to which something stands out or acquires priority.

But that definition hides an important complication.

Scientists use the word **salience** in several different ways.

It can refer to:

- perceptual prominence;
- attentional capture;
- behavioral relevance;
- motivational importance;
- incentive value;
- personal significance.

These are related.

But they are not identical.

A bright flashing light may be perceptually salient.

A sudden explosion may capture attention.

A glass of water may become motivationally important when you are thirsty.

A message from a loved one may have deep personal significance.

Calling all of these things "salience" does not mean they are produced by exactly the same mechanism.

## Perceptual Salience: What Stands Out?

Some things stand out because of their physical properties.

A bright object against a dark background.

A sudden movement.

A loud sound.

A flashing light.

These features can make a stimulus perceptually prominent.

Your attention may be drawn toward them automatically.

But something can capture your attention without being personally important.

A car horn may interrupt your thoughts.

That does not mean you believe the horn contains a message specifically for you.

This distinction becomes important later.

**Attention being captured is not the same thing as personal significance.**

## Motivational Salience: What Matters to You?

Now consider hunger.

You may walk past a restaurant without thinking much about it.

But after many hours without food, the same restaurant may suddenly become much more important.

The building has not changed.

Your internal state has.

The information has acquired greater motivational relevance.

The same thing happens with water when you are thirsty.

Or shelter when you are cold.

Or safety when you perceive danger.

Salience therefore does not depend only on the external world.

It also depends on the needs of the organism.

The brain is not simply asking:

> What is out there?

It is also asking:

> What matters to me?

## Incentive Salience: Wanting Is Not the Same as Liking

One particularly important form of salience is called **incentive salience**.

This idea is strongly associated with the work of Kent Berridge and Terry Robinson.

Incentive salience refers to the motivational power that certain cues can acquire.

A cue associated with a reward can become:

- attractive;
- attention-grabbing;
- motivationally powerful;
- capable of triggering approach behavior.

This led researchers to an important distinction:

**Liking** is the experience of pleasure.

**Wanting** is the motivational drive to obtain something.

These are not always the same.

You can enjoy something.

But you can also feel strongly driven toward something without experiencing proportionally greater pleasure.

This distinction became particularly important in addiction research.

A cue associated with a drug can become extremely powerful.

Seeing the cue may trigger intense wanting—even if the actual experience is no longer as pleasurable as it once was.

In other words:

> Motivation is not simply pleasure.

And salience is not simply pleasure either.

## Where Does Dopamine Fit In?

Dopamine is often described in popular culture as the brain's "pleasure chemical."

That description is too simple.

Dopamine participates in several important processes, including:

- learning;
- motivation;
- movement;
- reward processing;
- action selection.

Different dopamine systems can do different things.

Their functions depend on where they are in the brain, when they are active, and what the organism is doing.

So we should avoid saying:

> Dopamine is pleasure.

But we should also avoid replacing that oversimplification with another:

> Dopamine is salience.

That is also too simple.

The relationship between dopamine and salience depends on which kind of salience we are discussing.

## Dopamine and Prediction Error

In previous articles, we encountered the concept of **prediction error**.

A prediction error occurs when something differs from what was expected.

Conceptually:

Prediction Error = Observation − Prediction

Some influential neuroscience research has shown that dopamine activity can relate to **reward prediction errors**.

Imagine expecting a small reward but receiving a large one.

The outcome is better than expected.

That produces a positive reward prediction error.

If you expect a reward and it does not arrive, the outcome is worse than expected.

That produces a negative reward prediction error.

These ideas became deeply influential in computational neuroscience and reinforcement learning.

But prediction error and salience are not the same thing.

A surprising event may produce a prediction error without being particularly important.

And something can be extremely important without being surprising.

A child may predict that their parent will arrive home at the usual time.

The arrival is expected.

But it can still be deeply important.

So:

**Surprise is not the same as importance.**

## What Happens When Importance Goes Wrong?

This brings us to one of the most influential ideas connecting salience with psychosis.

In 2003, psychiatrist and researcher Shitij Kapur proposed the **aberrant salience hypothesis**.

The central idea is not simply that a person "believes strange things."

Instead, the hypothesis begins with experience.

What if ordinary events begin to feel unusually important?

A glance from a stranger.

A phrase on television.

A coincidence.

A sound.

A pattern that most people would ignore.

Suppose these events begin to feel unusually significant.

The person may naturally begin asking:

> Why does this feel important?

> What does this mean?

> Why does this seem connected to me?

Kapur proposed that dysregulated dopamine signaling could contribute to inappropriate assignment of salience.

A simplified version of the idea looks like this:

Dysregulated dopamine signaling

↓

Ordinary events acquire unusual significance

↓

The person attempts to understand the experience

↓

Meaning-making

↓

Possible development of unusual beliefs

This is the **aberrant salience hypothesis**.

## From Salience to Meaning

One of the most interesting aspects of the hypothesis is that it connects biology with subjective experience.

Imagine that something feels intensely important.

You may not simply ignore that feeling.

You may try to explain it.

Humans are meaning-making organisms.

We constantly ask:

Why did that happen?

What does it mean?

Is it connected to something?

Is there a pattern?

If ordinary events repeatedly acquire an unusual feeling of significance, the search for an explanation may become increasingly important.

This creates a possible pathway:

Event

↓

Unusual significance

↓

Attention

↓

Interpretation

↓

Meaning

↓

Belief

But this should not be understood as a universal sequence.

People interpret experiences through:

- culture;
- religion;
- personal history;
- memory;
- emotion;
- social environment;
- existing beliefs.

The same unusual experience may therefore be interpreted differently by different people.

## Aberrant Salience Is Not the Same as Delusion

This distinction is essential.

Aberrant salience does not automatically produce a delusion.

An unusual feeling of significance is an experience.

A delusion is a belief.

Between experience and belief are many processes.

A person may experience something unusual.

They may search for an explanation.

They may consider several possibilities.

Their interpretation may be influenced by their prior beliefs and social environment.

So a more careful model is:

Unusual experience

+

Interpretation

+

Prior knowledge

+

Context

+

Cognitive processes

↓

Possible contribution to belief formation

This is one reason schizophrenia cannot be reduced to a single chemical imbalance or one computational error.

## Prediction Error, Precision, and Salience

We can now connect this discussion to the ideas from previous articles.

Article 5 introduced **prediction error**.

Article 6 introduced **precision**.

Article 7 introduced **uncertainty, volatility, and learning**.

Article 8 adds another question:

> What information receives priority?

Prediction error tells us that something differs from expectation.

Precision concerns how reliable or influential information is estimated to be.

Salience concerns, more broadly, how much priority or importance information receives.

These processes may interact.

A simplified conceptual picture might look like this:

Prediction

↓

Prediction Error

↓

Precision Weighting

↓

Processing Priority

↓

Attention and Updating

But this is not a complete model of the brain.

And it is important not to collapse all of these concepts into one.

**Prediction error is not salience.**

**Precision is not salience.**

**Attention is not salience.**

They interact—but they are different concepts.

## The Salience Network

There is another reason the word "salience" can become confusing.

Neuroscientists often refer to a large-scale brain system called the **salience network**.

Important regions associated with this network include:

- the anterior insula;
- the dorsal anterior cingulate cortex.

The network has been associated with detecting behaviorally relevant information and coordinating responses across different brain systems.

It has also been discussed as helping coordinate transitions between different modes of processing.

For example, the brain sometimes focuses on internal thoughts.

At other times, it focuses on the outside world.

The salience network may participate in coordinating these shifts.

But here again, we need to be careful.

The existence of a "salience network" does not mean that every form of psychological salience is produced by one network.

The word **salience** is being used at different levels:

- psychological;
- computational;
- motivational;
- neural.

These levels are connected.

But they are not identical.

## The Triple-Network Model

The salience network is often discussed alongside two other large-scale networks:

The **default mode network**, associated with internally oriented processing.

The **central executive network**, associated with cognitive control and goal-directed processing.

Together, these have been described in the **triple-network model**.

A simplified picture looks like this:

Default Mode Network

→ Internal thought and self-related processing

Salience Network

→ Detection of relevant information and coordination

Central Executive Network

→ Goal-directed control and problem-solving

This framework has become influential in psychiatric neuroscience.

Researchers have investigated whether altered interactions among these networks may contribute to disorders including schizophrenia.

But again, there is no single network abnormality that explains schizophrenia.

## Salience and Schizophrenia

Research into schizophrenia has examined several possible forms of altered salience.

These include:

- unusual assignment of personal significance;
- motivational abnormalities;
- altered attention;
- dopamine dysregulation;
- large-scale network differences.

These findings may be related.

But they should not automatically be treated as one mechanism.

A person can experience unusual significance in some situations while simultaneously struggling to find motivation for ordinary goals.

This is one of the reasons schizophrenia is so complex.

The same disorder can involve:

**Too much importance assigned to some things**

and

**Too little motivation directed toward other things**

That is not a simple global increase or decrease in salience.

It suggests that different systems of prioritization may be functioning differently.

## A More Careful Question

Instead of asking:

> Does schizophrenia involve too much salience?

A better question might be:

> How are different forms of priority, importance, motivation, attention, and significance regulated—and how might those processes become disrupted?

That question is more difficult.

But it is also more scientifically useful.

It allows us to distinguish:

- surprise from importance;
- attention from motivation;
- wanting from liking;
- experience from belief;
- dopamine from a single universal function.

## The Precision Instability Framework

This brings us back to the developing idea behind the Precision Instability Framework, or PIF.

PIF is not an established theory of schizophrenia.

It is a proposed research hypothesis.

The basic question is whether instability in the regulation of precision could contribute to instability in how information influences perception, learning, attention, and belief.

One possible conceptual extension is:

Instability in precision regulation

↓

Instability in prediction-error weighting

↓

Inconsistent processing priority

↓

Potential instability in salience assignment

↓

Altered attention and belief updating

This is a hypothesis.

It needs to be operationalized and tested.

For example:

Can we measure trial-to-trial instability?

Can we measure how strongly surprise influences attention?

Can we distinguish average differences from dynamic instability?

These are empirical questions.

## What Would a Salience Experiment Look Like?

Imagine an experiment containing several kinds of events.

Some events are:

- predictable and important;
- predictable and unimportant;
- surprising and important;
- surprising and irrelevant.

Other cues might be associated with reward or motivation.

Researchers could measure:

- reaction time;
- attention;
- choices;
- learning;
- subjective ratings of importance.

A computational model could estimate:

- prediction errors;
- uncertainty;
- volatility;
- learning rates.

The central question would be:

> What makes information important to the brain?

And an additional question might be:

> Is the problem an average difference—or instability over time?

That distinction could become important for future research.

## Salience Is Part of a Larger Story

The brain is constantly solving a prioritization problem.

It must decide:

What should I notice?

What should I ignore?

What should I learn from?

What should I act on?

What matters?

Salience is part of the machinery involved in answering those questions.

But there is no single salience system.

There are multiple interacting processes.

Perception.

Attention.

Prediction.

Precision.

Motivation.

Learning.

Meaning.

Belief.

The challenge for neuroscience is to understand how these processes work together.

And the challenge for schizophrenia research is to understand how disruptions across these systems may produce profoundly altered experiences of reality.
