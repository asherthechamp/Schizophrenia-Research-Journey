# 9. What Is Attention?

## How does the brain decide what deserves priority?

**Published on Medium:** [PASTE ARTICLE 9 MEDIUM LINK HERE]

---

Imagine sitting in a crowded café.

Conversations surround you.

Cups clink against saucers.

Music plays in the background.

People move through your field of vision.

Your phone vibrates.

At the same time, you may be thinking about something that happened yesterday, worrying about tomorrow, or trying to read the sentence in front of you.

All of this information is potentially available.

But you are not experiencing—or processing—every part of it equally.

At one moment, you focus on the person speaking to you.

A moment later, a sudden sound captures your attention.

Then you deliberately return to your book.

This ordinary experience reveals an extraordinary problem:

> **The world contains more information than the brain can treat equally.**

The brain must constantly determine:

> **What deserves priority right now?**

Article 8 explored one part of that problem: **salience**.

Some things stand out.

They may seem important, novel, relevant, threatening, rewarding, or meaningful.

But something standing out is not exactly the same as giving it priority for processing.

That brings us to the next question:

> **What is attention?**

---

# The Problem of Too Much Information

The brain is constantly dealing with information from:

- Vision
- Hearing
- Touch
- The body
- Memory
- Emotion
- Thought
- Other people
- The surrounding environment

Even when we appear to be doing nothing, the brain is not receiving one simple stream of information.

It is dealing with a changing, complex, and often overwhelming world.

The problem is not simply:

> **How do we receive information?**

The deeper problem is:

> **How do we prioritize it?**

Imagine trying to cross a busy street while giving equal importance to:

- Every passing car
- Every sound
- Every face
- Every building
- Every memory
- Every bodily sensation

Effective behavior would become extremely difficult.

To act in the world, some information must receive greater priority than other information.

That broad family of processes is what we call:

# Attention

---

# Attention Is Not One Single Thing

It is tempting to imagine attention as a mental spotlight.

You point the spotlight at something.

That thing becomes illuminated.

Everything outside the spotlight becomes less important.

The metaphor is useful.

But it can also mislead us.

There is no literal spotlight inside the brain.

And there is no single brain location that simply produces:

> **Attention.**

Attention is better understood as a family of processes involved in:

- Selection
- Prioritization
- Enhancement
- Maintenance
- Reorientation
- Control

Attention can be directed toward:

- A location
- An object
- A feature
- A sound
- A thought
- A memory
- A task

So when we ask:

> **What is attention?**

we are not asking about one single mechanism.

We are asking how the brain selectively prioritizes some information relative to competing information.

A useful working definition is:

> **Attention is the selective prioritization of information for processing.**

---

# Selective Attention: Choosing Among Competitors

One of the most familiar forms of attention is:

# Selective Attention

Imagine being at a crowded party.

Dozens of voices are present.

Yet you can try to follow one conversation.

The other voices do not physically disappear.

Your ears still receive sound.

But one stream receives greater priority.

Or imagine searching for a friend in a crowd.

There may be hundreds of faces.

You are not treating every face equally.

Your goal influences what you are looking for.

Selective attention allows the brain to prioritize some information relative to competing information.

But an important correction is needed.

> **Attending to one thing does not necessarily mean everything else is completely ignored.**

Information outside the current focus of attention may still influence processing.

Attention is therefore not simply a switch between:

> Completely processed

and

> Completely absent.

Instead, information may receive different degrees of priority.

Some information may be:

- Enhanced
- Deprioritized
- Suppressed
- Processed more weakly
- Processed automatically

Attention is therefore part of a dynamic system of priorities.

---

# Attention Can Be Guided by Goals

Suppose you enter an airport looking for someone wearing a red jacket.

Suddenly, the environment changes.

Not physically.

But cognitively.

The color red may now become especially relevant to you.

Your goal influences what receives priority.

This is often called:

# Goal-Directed Attention

or:

# Top-Down Attention

Top-down attention is influenced by internal factors such as:

- Goals
- Expectations
- Intentions
- Knowledge
- Task demands

You are not simply waiting for the environment to decide what you notice.

Your current purpose helps organize your priorities.

If you are looking for your friend, faces may become important.

If you are looking for your luggage, bags may become important.

If you are driving, traffic signals may become important.

The same physical environment can therefore produce different patterns of attention depending on:

> **What you are trying to do.**

---

# But the World Can Interrupt You

Now imagine that you are reading a difficult scientific paper.

Your attention is directed toward the text.

Then:

**BANG.**

A sudden loud sound occurs nearby.

Your attention shifts.

You may not have chosen to redirect it.

The event captured your attention.

This is often described as:

# Stimulus-Driven Attention

Incoming events can influence attention through factors such as:

- Sudden change
- Movement
- Novelty
- Sensory prominence
- Behavioral relevance

This is important because attention is not completely under voluntary control.

You can try to concentrate.

But the world can interrupt you.

A successful cognitive system must therefore balance two competing demands.

It must be stable enough to maintain its goals.

But flexible enough to respond to important changes.

Too much stability could make behavior rigid.

Too much flexibility could make sustained action difficult.

The brain must somehow balance:

> **Staying focused**

with

> **Remaining responsive.**

---

# Attention and Salience

Salience concerns why something stands out as important, relevant, or significant.

Attention concerns the selective prioritization of information for processing.

The two are closely related.

But they are not identical.

> **Salience can influence attention.**

But attention can also be deliberately directed toward something subtle that is relevant to a goal.

So:

> **Something can be salient without being the current focus of attention.**

And:

> **Something can receive attention without being inherently salient.**

A useful summary is:

> **Salience concerns why something stands out.**

> **Attention concerns how processing priority is allocated.**

---

# Attention Is Not the Same as Consciousness

Attention and consciousness are closely related.

But they are not identical concepts.

Attention concerns:

> **Selection and prioritization.**

Consciousness concerns:

> **Subjective experience and awareness.**

The relationship between them remains one of the fascinating questions in neuroscience and philosophy.

---

# Attention and the Predictive Brain

Our earlier articles introduced predictive processing.

The brain continuously generates predictions about the world.

Incoming information can differ from those predictions.

Those differences can generate:

# Prediction Errors

But not every prediction error should have equal influence.

Some signals are noisy.

Some are reliable.

Some are relevant.

Some are not.

This brings us back to:

# Precision

Within some predictive-processing frameworks, aspects of attention may be related to how strongly particular signals are weighted according to their expected reliability.

But:

> **Attention is not simply precision.**

Instead:

> **Attention and precision may be connected within particular computational theories.**

---

# Attention in Schizophrenia

Attention has been studied in schizophrenia for decades.

But schizophrenia should not be reduced to a simple:

> **Attention deficit.**

Schizophrenia is a complex and heterogeneous condition that can involve:

- Psychotic experiences
- Cognitive difficulties
- Negative symptoms
- Changes in motivation
- Alterations in perception
- Changes in self-experience

Attention is one part of this larger picture.

And attention itself includes multiple processes.

A more useful question is:

> **Which attentional processes are altered?**

And:

> **Under what conditions?**

---

# Beyond the Simple Idea of an Attention Deficit

One important contemporary hypothesis is:

# Hyperfocusing

The hyperfocusing hypothesis proposes that, in some contexts, attention may become unusually narrow and intensely concentrated.

The issue may therefore not always be:

> **Too little attention.**

It may sometimes involve:

> **Attention that is too narrowly concentrated.**

This does not mean hyperfocusing explains schizophrenia as a whole.

It is one hypothesis within a much larger and more complex scientific picture.

But it teaches an important lesson:

> **The question is not simply how much attention someone has.**

It may also be:

> **Where is attention directed?**

> **How narrowly is it focused?**

> **How flexibly can it be redirected?**

---

# Attention, Salience, and Meaning

We can now see an important conceptual relationship.

Imagine:

> **An unexpected event occurs.**

↓

> **The event becomes unusually significant.**

↓

> **Attention is captured.**

↓

> **The person searches for an explanation.**

↓

> **Meaning is constructed.**

This is not a complete explanation of psychosis.

Psychotic experiences are heterogeneous.

Different people may reach similar experiences through different pathways.

But the interaction between:

- Prediction
- Prediction error
- Precision
- Salience
- Attention
- Meaning

is an increasingly important area of computational psychiatry.

---

# Attention Is a Problem of Priorities

Perhaps the simplest way to understand attention is this:

The brain must constantly answer:

> **What should receive priority right now?**

The answer depends on many things:

- Goals
- Expectations
- Sudden changes
- Danger
- Reward
- Relevance
- Learning
- Bodily needs

Attention sits at the intersection of:

> **The world**

and

> **The organism's goals.**

The brain continually organizes priorities.

And those priorities can change from moment to moment.

---

# But Selection Creates Another Problem

If attention selects some information for priority, what happens to everything else?

The brain cannot enhance everything.

Selection creates competition.

And competition requires some information to receive less priority.

This involves processes related to:

- Suppression
- Filtering
- Ignoring
- Deprioritization

This brings us to the next question.

Attention asks:

> **What should I prioritize?**

The next question is:

> **What should I ignore?**
