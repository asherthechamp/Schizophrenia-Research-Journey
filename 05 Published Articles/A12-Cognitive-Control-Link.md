## Article Content

### Central Question

> How does the brain maintain a goal when many competing signals are active at once?

### Central Idea

Cognitive control is the capacity to organize thought and behavior according to currently relevant goals and task demands.

It involves multiple interacting processes, including:

- maintaining goals,
- selecting relevant information,
- managing competition,
- suppressing inappropriate responses,
- monitoring performance,
- updating information,
- changing strategies,
- adapting to changing circumstances.

Cognitive control is therefore broader than inhibition.

## Key Distinctions

**Attention**  
What deserves priority?

**Filtering**  
What should receive less priority?

**Inhibition**  
What should be constrained or stopped?

**Cognitive Control**  
How should processing and behavior be organized around the goal?

**Monitoring**  
What is happening, and is it consistent with the goal?

**Adaptation**  
What should change because of what just happened?

---

## Conceptual Formula

**Control = Selection + Maintenance + Monitoring + Suppression + Adaptation**

This is a conceptual decomposition, not a literal mathematical equation.

The components represent interacting operations within a broader control architecture.

> Inhibition is one operation within cognitive control, not the definition of cognitive control itself.

---

## Central Scientific Message

Cognitive control is not simply the ability to stop an action.

It is the broader capacity to organize cognition and behavior around goals while dealing with competition, maintaining relevant information, monitoring what happens, and adapting when circumstances change.

The brain must solve a continuous problem:

> **Stay on course—but remain capable of changing course.**

---

## Schizophrenia Perspective

Cognitive-control abnormalities have been extensively studied in schizophrenia, but schizophrenia should not be reduced to a single "cognitive-control deficit."

Different people may show different patterns involving:

- attention,
- working memory,
- inhibition,
- goal maintenance,
- conflict processing,
- error monitoring,
- cognitive flexibility,
- motivation,
- learning,
- decision-making,
- network organization.

The scientifically useful question is therefore:

> **Which control processes change, under what conditions, and through which computational mechanisms?**

---

## Computational Perspective

A behavioral observation such as slower or less accurate performance can potentially arise from different underlying processes.

Possible explanations include:

- slower evidence accumulation,
- altered decision thresholds,
- weaker goal maintenance,
- unstable working-memory representations,
- altered learning,
- motivational differences,
- altered allocation of control.

Computational psychiatry provides a framework for separating these possibilities.

The broader research goal is to connect:

**Behavior**

↕  

**Cognition**

↕  

**Computation**

↕  

**Neural implementation**

## Transition to the Next Stage

The article ends by moving from control of behavior toward control of internally generated cognition.

The brain does not only respond to the external world.

It also:

- remembers,
- imagines,
- predicts,
- plans,
- speaks internally,
- evaluates itself,
- generates thoughts that can become objects of further thought.

This raises the next question:

> **How does the brain control cognition when the signals it must regulate are generated from within?**

This provides the conceptual bridge toward later articles on:

- working memory,
- internal cognition,
- metacognition,
- self-monitoring,
- agency,
- internally generated thought.
