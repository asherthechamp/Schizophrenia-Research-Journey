# Core References

## 1. Aberrant Salience and Psychosis

Kapur, S. (2003).

Psychosis as a state of aberrant salience: A framework linking biology,
phenomenology, and pharmacology in schizophrenia.

American Journal of Psychiatry, 160(1), 13–23.

DOI:
10.1176/appi.ajp.160.1.13

### Why this reference matters

This is the central reference for the aberrant salience hypothesis.

Kapur proposed that dysregulated dopamine signaling may contribute to the
inappropriate assignment of significance to environmental events and
internal representations.

The framework attempts to connect:

- dopamine biology;
- subjective experience;
- psychosis;
- delusion formation;
- antipsychotic pharmacology.

### Article use

This reference supports discussion of:

- aberrant salience;
- unusual experiences of significance;
- dopamine and psychosis;
- meaning-making;
- the possible relationship between anomalous experience and delusional
  belief formation.

# 2. Incentive Salience and Dopamine

Berridge, K. C., & Robinson, T. E. (1998).

What is the role of dopamine in reward: Hedonic impact, reward learning,
or incentive salience?

Brain Research Reviews, 28(3), 309–369.

DOI:
10.1016/S0165-0173(98)00019-8

### Why this reference matters

This landmark review developed the distinction between:

Liking

and

Wanting.

The authors argued that dopamine should not simply be understood as
mediating pleasure.

Instead, dopamine may play an important role in incentive salience—the
motivational properties that make reward-related cues attractive and
capable of generating wanting.

### Article use

This reference supports discussion of:

- incentive salience;
- wanting versus liking;
- motivation;
- dopamine;
- why dopamine should not simply be called the "pleasure chemical."

# 3. Dopamine, Prediction, and Reward

Schultz, W., Dayan, P., & Montague, P. R. (1997).

A neural substrate of prediction and reward.

Science, 275(5306), 1593–1599.

DOI:
10.1126/science.275.5306.1593

### Why this reference matters

This landmark paper helped establish the influential connection between
dopamine activity and reward prediction errors.

The framework became foundational for:

- computational neuroscience;
- reinforcement learning;
- models of reward;
- prediction-error research.

### Article use

This reference supports discussion of:

- reward prediction errors;
- expected versus received outcomes;
- surprise;
- learning;
- the distinction between prediction error and salience.

# 4. Goal-Directed and Stimulus-Driven Attention

Corbetta, M., & Shulman, G. L. (2002).

Control of goal-directed and stimulus-driven attention in the brain.

Nature Reviews Neuroscience, 3, 201–215.

10.1038/nrn755

### Why this reference matters

This influential review describes different systems involved in:

- goal-directed attention;
- stimulus-driven attention;
- attentional control;
- detection of behaviorally relevant events.

It provides an important foundation for distinguishing attention from
salience.

### Article use

This reference supports discussion of:

- selective attention;
- top-down attention;
- stimulus-driven attention;
- attentional capture;
- the distinction between attention and salience.

# 5. Salience Network and Insula Function

Menon, V., & Uddin, L. Q. (2010).

Saliency, switching, attention and control: A network model of insula
function.

Brain Structure and Function, 214(5–6), 655–667.

DOI:
10.1007/s00429-010-0262-0

### Why this reference matters

This paper provides an influential network-level account of the anterior
insula and its role in:

- detecting salient events;
- switching between large-scale networks;
- attention;
- cognitive control.

The paper discusses the anterior insula and anterior cingulate cortex as
major components of a salience-related network.

### Article use

This reference supports discussion of:

- the salience network;
- anterior insula;
- anterior cingulate cortex;
- network switching;
- interactions between internal and externally oriented processing.

# Conceptual Distinctions Used in Article 8

The article emphasizes that the following concepts should not be treated
as interchangeable.

## Prediction Error

The difference between expectation and observation.

Primary reference:

Schultz, Dayan, & Montague (1997).

---

## Attention

The selective allocation of processing resources.

Primary reference:

Corbetta & Shulman (2002).

---

## Incentive Salience

The motivational properties that can make reward-related cues attractive
and capable of generating wanting.

Primary reference:

Berridge & Robinson (1998).

---

## Aberrant Salience

The inappropriate assignment of significance or importance, proposed as
one possible mechanism contributing to psychosis.

Primary reference:

Kapur (2003).

---

## Salience Network

A large-scale brain network involving regions including the anterior
insula and anterior cingulate cortex and associated with detecting
relevant information and coordinating processing.

Primary reference:

Menon & Uddin (2010).

# Scientific Cautions for Article 8

## 1. Dopamine is not simply the "pleasure chemical."

Dopamine participates in multiple processes, including motivation,
learning, movement, and reward-related processing.

---

## 2. Dopamine is not a single universal salience signal.

Different dopamine pathways and neuronal populations may have different
functions.

---

## 3. Prediction error is not identical to salience.

Something can be surprising without being important.

Something can be important without being surprising.

---

## 4. Attention is not identical to salience.

Salience can influence attention, but attention can also be voluntarily
directed.

---

## 5. Incentive salience is not identical to perceptual salience.

A visually prominent stimulus is not necessarily motivationally
important.

---

## 6. Aberrant salience is not identical to delusion.

An unusual experience of significance may contribute to a search for
meaning, but delusion formation involves multiple psychological and
social processes.

---

## 7. The salience network is not the entire neural basis of salience.

The term "salience" is used across:

- psychology;
- computational neuroscience;
- motivation research;
- network neuroscience.

These levels should not automatically be collapsed into one mechanism.

# Article 8 Core Scientific Narrative

The scientific narrative of Article 8 is:

The brain must prioritize information.

↓

Some information is perceptually prominent.

↓

Some captures attention.

↓

Some becomes motivationally important.

↓

Some reward-related cues acquire incentive salience.

↓

Dopamine contributes to several of these processes in different ways.

↓

In psychosis, the aberrant salience hypothesis proposes that ordinary
events may acquire inappropriate significance.

↓

The person may attempt to understand these unusual experiences.

↓

Meaning-making and belief formation can then interact with prior beliefs,
context, culture, and cognition.

The central conclusion is:

Salience is not one thing.

It is a family of related problems involving priority, attention,
motivation, significance, and behavioral relevance.

# Note for Future Research

The Precision Instability Framework (PIF) is not represented in the
literature as an established theory.

Article 8 uses PIF only as a proposed research hypothesis.

Any claims about PIF should therefore be clearly labeled as:

- conceptual;
- hypothetical;
- testable;
- unvalidated.

Future work should attempt to operationalize whether instability in
precision weighting could contribute to instability in:

- prediction-error influence;
- processing priority;
- attentional allocation;
- salience assignment;
- belief updating.
