# Core References

## 1. Verbruggen & Logan (2008)

**Verbruggen, F., & Logan, G. D. (2008).**  
*Response inhibition in the stop-signal paradigm.*  
Trends in Cognitive Sciences, 12(11), 418–424.

DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2008.07.005

PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18799345/

### Why this source is important

This is a foundational review of response inhibition and the Stop-Signal paradigm.

### Key contribution

Response inhibition involves suppressing actions that are no longer required or are inappropriate. The Stop-Signal paradigm provides an experimental framework for studying the timing and mechanisms of stopping.

### Supports in Article 11

- definition of response inhibition
- Stop-Signal Task
- action cancellation
- race-model framework
- SSRT
- relationship between stopping and cognitive control

## 2. Aron (2011)

*From reactive to proactive and selective control: Developing a richer model for stopping inappropriate responses.*  
Biological Psychiatry, 69(12), e55–e68.

DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.biopsych.2010.07.024

PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20932513/

### Why this source is important

This paper expands the traditional view of stopping beyond simple reactive response cancellation.

# Scientific Guardrails for Article 11

The following claims should NOT be made:

### Do not say:

> Schizophrenia is an inhibition disorder.

### Better:

> Some people with schizophrenia show abnormalities on particular measures of inhibitory control, but these findings do not establish a single inhibitory deficit as the cause of schizophrenia.

---

### Do not say:

> The right inferior frontal cortex is the brain's inhibition center.

### Better:

> The right inferior frontal cortex is an important component of distributed neural systems involved in inhibitory control.

---

### Do not say:

> Go/No-Go and Stop-Signal tasks measure exactly the same form of inhibition.

### Better:

> Different inhibitory paradigms emphasize partially different mechanisms, including action restraint and action cancellation.

---

### Do not say:

> A slow SSRT proves impaired inhibition.

### Better:

> A slower SSRT indicates poorer performance on a measure designed to estimate stopping latency, but performance can reflect multiple underlying processes.

---

### Do not say:

> Inhibition is the same as filtering.

### Better:

> Filtering and inhibition are related but distinguishable processes. Filtering concerns the allocation of processing priority, whereas inhibition concerns constraining or stopping competing responses or processes.

---

### Do not say:

> Predictive processing proves why inhibition fails in schizophrenia.

### Better:

> Predictive-processing frameworks provide one possible computational perspective for understanding how expectations, action preparation, and control may interact.

---

# Evidence Hierarchy

For this article, evidence should be interpreted approximately in the following order:

1. Meta-analyses and systematic reviews
2. Large or well-designed empirical studies
3. Established experimental paradigms
4. Computational models
5. Network-level neuroscience
6. Theoretical frameworks
7. Conceptual interpretations

The article should clearly distinguish empirical findings from theoretical interpretation.

# Core Conceptual Chain

Article 11 develops the following progression:

**Attention**

What deserves priority?

↓

**Filtering**

What should receive less priority?

↓

**Inhibition**

What should be constrained or stopped?

↓

**Cognitive Control**

How are priorities maintained in pursuit of a goal?

This progression prepares the transition to Article 12.

# Transition to Article 12

Article 11 asks:

> How does the brain stop something that it has already started?

Article 12 will move one level upward:

> How does the brain maintain a goal when many competing signals are active at once?

The conceptual progression becomes:

**Attention → Filtering → Inhibition → Cognitive Control**
