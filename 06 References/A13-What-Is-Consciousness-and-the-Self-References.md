# 3. Global Workspace and Conscious Access

Global Workspace approaches propose that some information becomes conscious when it becomes broadly available to distributed cognitive systems.

These approaches are discussed in the general consciousness literature reviewed by:

Seth, A. K., & Bayne, T. (2022).

**Theories of consciousness.**

PubMed:
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35505255/

Purpose:

Used to introduce:

- Global Workspace ideas
- Conscious access
- Broad availability of information
- Relationship between consciousness and cognitive functions

Scientific caution:

Global Workspace approaches are influential theoretical frameworks, not a universally established final explanation of consciousness.

# 4. Higher-Order Theories

Higher-Order theories propose, in different forms, that conscious states involve representations of one's own mental states.

These theories are reviewed comparatively in:

Seth, A. K., & Bayne, T. (2022).

**Theories of consciousness.**

PubMed:
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35505255/

Purpose:

Used for the section:

**Higher-Order Theories**

and for introducing the distinction between:

**Representation of a mental state**

**The self.**

Scientific caution:

A higher-order representation of a mental state should not automatically be treated as a complete explanation of selfhood.

# 5. Recurrent Processing

Recurrent Processing approaches emphasize feedback and recurrent interactions within neural systems.

**Theories of consciousness.**

# 6. Integrated Information Theory

Integrated Information Theory (IIT) proposes that consciousness is associated with particular forms of integrated and differentiated information.

Contemporary discussions and critiques of IIT are included in the broader consciousness literature reviewed by:

Seth, A. K., & Bayne, T. (2022).

PubMed:
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35505255/

Purpose:

Used to introduce IIT as an influential but actively debated theoretical framework.

Scientific caution:

The article does not present IIT as established fact or as the final theory of consciousness.

---

# 7. Predictive Processing

Predictive processing describes perception and cognition in terms of predictions, incoming evidence, and updating.

It provides a useful computational framework for connecting:

**Prediction → Prediction Error → Precision → Attention → Perception**

and eventually to questions concerning conscious experience.

The broader theoretical relationship between predictive approaches and consciousness is discussed in:

Seth, A. K., & Bayne, T. (2022).

PubMed:
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35505255/

Purpose:

Used to connect Article 13 with the conceptual progression established in Articles 1–12.

Scientific caution:

Predictive processing is not presented as a complete or universally accepted theory of consciousness.

---

# 8. Neural Correlates of Consciousness

# 15. Consciousness and Phenomenology

Phenomenology is important because consciousness is partly defined by the structure of lived experience.

Phenomenological analysis can investigate:

- First-person perspective
- Mineness
- Embodiment
- Agency
- Self-awareness
- Self-world relationship
- Meaning
- Alterations of experience

The schizophrenia self-disorder literature above provides the main phenomenological foundation for Article 13.

Scientific guardrail:

Phenomenological descriptions do not automatically identify their neural mechanisms.

---

# 16. Neurophenomenology

Article 13 uses the following conceptual bridge:

**First-person description**
↕
**Computational characterization**
↕
**Neural measurement**

This is a conceptual framework rather than a claim that phenomenology can be directly reduced to a particular neural measurement.

The purpose is to integrate:

**Experience → Computation → Brain**

while preserving the differences between explanatory levels.

---

# 17. Consciousness, Self, and Agency

Article 13 deliberately distinguishes:

**Consciousness**

> There is subjective experience.

**Self**

> Experience is organized from a first-person perspective and can carry a sense of mineness.

**Agency**

> An action or event is experienced as generated or controlled by oneself.

These concepts overlap but are not identical.

This distinction prepares the transition to Article 14.

# 19. Major Scientific Guardrails

Article 13 follows these principles:

1. Do not claim that one neural mechanism explains consciousness.
2. Do not present one consciousness theory as conclusively established.
3. Do not equate consciousness with attention.
4. Do not equate consciousness with working memory.
5. Do not equate consciousness with reportability.
6. Do not equate consciousness with selfhood.
7. Do not reduce selfhood to one brain region.
8. Do not describe schizophrenia simply as a disorder of consciousness.
9. Do not describe schizophrenia simply as a disorder of the self.
10. Do not treat self-disorder theories as complete causal explanations.
11. Do not reduce phenomenology directly to neural measurements.
12. Do not treat neural correlates as automatically causal.
13. Do not treat hyperreflexivity as a universal explanation of schizophrenia.
14. Do not assume that all forms of agency or ownership depend on one mechanism.
15. Distinguish phenomenological description, cognitive explanation, computational modeling, and neural implementation.

# 20. Evidence Hierarchy

When interpreting claims in this article, distinguish:

**Level 1 — Phenomenological observation**

What people report or how experiences are described.

↓

**Level 2 — Behavioral evidence**

How these experiences relate to measurable task performance.

↓

**Level 3 — Computational explanation**

A model describing how the observed pattern could arise.

↓

**Level 4 — Neural implementation**

Brain mechanisms that may implement the relevant processes.

↓

**Level 5 — Causal mechanism**

Evidence that changing the mechanism changes the phenomenon.

The existence of evidence at one level does not automatically establish evidence at another.

# 21. Core Scientific Position

The central scientific position of Article 13 is:

> **Consciousness is a multidimensional scientific and philosophical problem involving subjective experience, access, neural dynamics, and first-person organization. No single theory currently provides universally accepted closure.**

The self should likewise be treated as a layered construct involving potentially distinguishable components such as:

- first-person perspective,
- mineness,
- embodiment,
- ownership,
- agency,
- self-reference,
- autobiographical identity,
- and social identity.

# 23. Transition to Article 14

Article 13 ends with a more precise question:

> **What is the minimal structure that makes an experience feel like it belongs to a subject?**

And then:

> **What makes an action feel like something I did?**

Article 14 will investigate:

**Minimal Self → Embodiment → Ownership → Agency**

The next article will therefore move from the broad concept of "self" toward the more fundamental structure of first-person experience.

# 25. Final Research Statement

Article 13 does not attempt to solve consciousness.

Instead, it establishes the conceptual foundation for the next stage of the Schizophrenia Research Journey:

**Conscious experience → First-person perspective → Minimal self → Agency → Self-disturbance**

The key question moving forward is:

> **If consciousness gives us experience, what gives that experience a subject—and what makes an action feel like my own?**
