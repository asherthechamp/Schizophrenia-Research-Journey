# Article 04 — Visual Concept

## How Does the Brain Predict?

### Core visual idea

The image should visually communicate predictive processing as an
interaction between:

**sensory evidence ↔ prediction ↔ inference**

The central message is that perception is neither a passive recording
of sensory input nor unconstrained imagination.

Instead, the brain can combine incoming sensory evidence with
expectations and prior information to construct a perceptual
interpretation.

---

# Scientific Message

Perception occurs under uncertainty.

Sensory signals can be incomplete, noisy, ambiguous, and constantly
changing.

Predictive-processing theories propose that the nervous system can use
internal models to generate predictions about sensory input and compare
those predictions with incoming evidence.

A simplified computational cycle is:

**Prediction**
↓
**Sensory evidence**
↓
**Comparison**
↓
**Prediction error**
↓
**Model updating**
↓
**New prediction**

The image should communicate this as a continuous interaction rather
than a simple one-way pipeline.

---

# Central Concept

The visual should avoid portraying:

**World → Brain → Picture**

as though perception were simply a recording process.

Instead, it should communicate:

**World**
↓
**Sensory evidence**
↕
**Internal predictions**
↓
**Inference**
↓
**Perception**
↓
**Experience**

The sensory evidence constrains the inference.

The prediction influences how the evidence is interpreted.

Neither side should visually dominate so strongly that the other
appears irrelevant.

---

# Recommended Composition

A horizontal or slightly diagonal scientific composition.

### Left — External World

Show a recognizable but slightly ambiguous real-world scene.

Possible example:

A person standing in a partially obscured environment, with an object
or figure that can be interpreted from incomplete sensory information.

The scene should remain realistic.

It should not be surreal or dreamlike.

The purpose is to represent the source of sensory information.

↓

### Sensory Interface

Show sensory information entering a stylized brain.

Possible elements:

- light entering the visual system;
- sound waves;
- simplified sensory signals;
- neural activity.

The representation should emphasize that the brain receives signals,
not a finished description of reality.

↓

### Central Brain

A scientifically inspired human brain or cortical network.

Inside the brain, show two interacting streams.

### Bottom-Up Stream

**Sensory evidence**

moving toward higher-level representations.

### Top-Down Stream

**Predictions**

moving back toward lower-level processing.

The arrows should visibly interact.

Avoid making the top-down arrows overwhelmingly stronger than the
bottom-up arrows.

---

# Prediction

The top-down pathway should be labeled:

**Prediction**

or:

**Internal model / prediction**

It should visually originate from higher-level representations and move
toward sensory-processing areas.

Possible visual language:

- subtle neural pathways;
- translucent arrows;
- hierarchical nodes;
- predictive signals.

The prediction should appear as an expectation rather than a visual
fantasy.

---

# Sensory Evidence

The bottom-up pathway should be labeled:

**Sensory evidence**

It should originate from the external environment and enter the brain.

The sensory evidence should be visibly incomplete or noisy.

This is important.

The image should communicate that the brain is solving an inference
problem because the sensory signal alone does not provide a complete
description.

---

# Prediction Error

Where the two pathways interact, introduce a small but clearly visible
element labeled:

**Prediction error**

This should represent mismatch between:

**what was expected**

and

**what was observed.**

Do not make prediction error look like an explosion, error warning, or
computer malfunction.

It is not simply a mistake.

It is information about mismatch.

---

# Inference

After prediction and sensory evidence interact, show:

**Inference**

as the process that integrates the available information.

The inference should lead toward:

**Perception**

rather than directly toward "hallucination."

This distinction is scientifically important.

---

# Perception

The resulting perceptual representation should appear coherent and
recognizable.

It should still be connected visually to both:

**sensory evidence**

and

**prediction.**

The image should communicate that perception is constrained
construction.

---

# Experience

The final stage can transition subtly from:

**Perception**

to:

**Experience**

This should not look like a completely separate object.

Instead, it should feel like the perceptual representation becoming
part of the organism's experienced world.

---

# Visual Flow

The overall conceptual flow should be:

**External world**

↓

**Sensory evidence**

↕

**Prediction**

↓

**Inference**

↓

**Perception**

↓

**Experience**

with a feedback loop:

**Prediction ↔ Sensory evidence**

and:

**Mismatch → Prediction error → Model updating**

---

# Important Scientific Constraint

Do NOT depict:

> "The brain hallucinates reality."

Do NOT show a fantasy world being generated inside the brain.

Do NOT imply that sensory evidence is unimportant.

Do NOT imply that predictive processing has been proven to be the
complete mechanism of perception.

Instead communicate:

> **Perception emerges from the interaction of sensory evidence,
> predictions, context, and inference.**

---

# Relationship to Schizophrenia

Schizophrenia should NOT be the central visual subject.

Article 4 establishes the general computational problem first.

The image should therefore represent ordinary perception.

Only subtle visual cues may suggest that this framework could later be
applied to psychiatric phenomena.

Do not include:

- hallucination imagery;
- psychosis imagery;
- distorted faces;
- threatening figures;
- stereotypical "schizophrenic" imagery.

This avoids stigmatizing or scientifically misleading visual language.

---

# Relationship to PIF

PIF should NOT appear prominently in the image.

The conceptual progression is:

**Prediction**

↓

**Prediction error**

↓

**Inference**

↓

**Precision**

↓

**Psychosis**

↓

**PIF**

Article 4 establishes the predictive-processing framework.

The precision concept will be developed later.

PIF is an original proposed research hypothesis and should not be
presented as established science.

---

# Visual Style

Scientific editorial illustration.

Characteristics:

- sophisticated;
- clean;
- modern;
- intellectually serious;
- neuroscience-inspired;
- visually understandable;
- suitable for a Medium research article.

Avoid:

- cartoon style;
- horror imagery;
- psychedelic effects;
- excessive neon;
- science-fiction interfaces;
- overly technical brain scans;
- stereotypical depictions of mental illness.

The image should feel like a conceptual scientific figure rather than
a medical advertisement.

---

# Color and Lighting

Use restrained, professional colors.

Sensory evidence and prediction can have subtly distinct visual
identities, but they should converge naturally during inference.

The contrast should help readers understand the computational process,
not imply that one pathway is "good" and the other is "bad."

---

# Typography

If text is incorporated into the image, keep labels minimal.

Recommended labels:

**Sensory Evidence**

**Prediction**

**Prediction Error**

**Inference**

**Perception**

**Experience**

Avoid paragraphs of text inside the image.

---

# Central Visual Metaphor

The strongest metaphor is:

### "The brain as an inference engine constrained by evidence."

Not:

### "The brain as a reality generator."

The distinction is central to the article.

---

# Suggested Caption

**How does the brain make sense of uncertain sensory information?**

Predictive-processing theories propose that perception emerges through
the interaction of sensory evidence, predictions, and inference.

---

# Accessibility Description

A conceptual neuroscience illustration showing how sensory evidence
and top-down predictions interact within a hierarchical brain model.
Bottom-up sensory information meets top-down predictions, producing
prediction errors that can update internal models and contribute to
perception and conscious experience.

---

# Scientific Status

The visual represents a conceptual synthesis of predictive-processing
ideas.

It is not a literal anatomical diagram.

It does not establish predictive processing as the complete mechanism
of perception.

It does not represent schizophrenia as a predictive-processing
disorder.

It does not present PIF as an established theory.

PIF remains a proposed research hypothesis.
