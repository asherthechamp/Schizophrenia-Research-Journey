# Article 06 — Visual Concept

## What Is Precision?

### Core visual message

Not all prediction errors carry equal weight.

A sensory signal can be strong or weak, reliable or uncertain. The
brain's inference depends partly on how much weight is assigned to
that information.

The visual progression is:

**Sensory Evidence → Uncertainty → Precision → Weighting → Inference → Experience**

---

## Conceptual continuity

Article 6 follows the conceptual progression established in Articles
1–5:

**Article 1:** Biology → Brain → Experience

**Article 2:** Experience → Self → World

**Article 3:** Evidence → Prediction → Inference → Perception → Experience

**Article 4:** Prediction → Error correction → Updating

**Article 5:** Expectation ↔ Evidence → Prediction Error

**Article 6:** Prediction Error × Reliability → Influence → Inference

---

## Visual composition

The image should be a single continuous conceptual scene rather than
a collection of disconnected boxes.

### 1. Sensory evidence

Show several incoming signals representing information from the
environment.

Possible examples:

- visual information;
- sound;
- environmental sensory signals.

Some signals should appear clear and strong while others appear faint,
diffuse, or noisy.

The visual distinction should communicate:

**Evidence ≠ equally reliable evidence**

---

### 2. Uncertainty

Represent uncertainty as part of the sensory information.

Clear information can have a narrow, sharply defined representation.

Noisy information can have a diffuse or blurred representation.

The conceptual relationship is:

**Greater uncertainty → Lower precision**

**Lower uncertainty → Higher precision**

---

### 3. Precision weighting

This should be the visual centerpiece.

Do not represent precision as a literal numerical gauge.

Instead, show different information streams receiving different
degrees of influence.

For example:

**Strong / reliable signal → Stronger influence**

**Weak / noisy signal → Weaker influence**

Several information streams should converge toward a central inference
process.

The visual metaphor should communicate weighted information flow rather
than a mechanical calculator.

---

### 4. Prediction and prediction error

Connect the image to Article 5.

Show prediction coming from a top-down direction and sensory evidence
coming from a bottom-up direction.

Where they disagree, represent prediction error.

Crucially, the prediction error should then be shown as being weighted
according to estimated precision.

This creates the conceptual bridge:

**Prediction Error → Precision Weighting → Influence on Inference**

---

### 5. Inference

The weighted information should converge into a central perceptual
inference.

The system should appear to be resolving uncertainty rather than
simply copying sensory input.

Core message:

> Inference depends not only on what information says, but on how
> reliable that information is estimated to be.

---

### 6. Experience

At the right side, show a coherent perceptual experience emerging from
the inference process.

It should feel continuous with the same external world rather than
representing an imaginary world disconnected from sensory evidence.

This maintains the principle established in Article 3:

> The brain constructs perceptual experience, but construction is
> constrained by evidence.
