# Article 07 — Visual Concept

## How Does the Brain Learn When the World Is Changing?

### Core visual idea

The image represents the brain as an adaptive inference system that
must determine whether unexpected observations are random noise or
evidence that the underlying environment has changed.

The central visual progression is:

**Stable Environment → Unexpected Evidence → Prediction Error →
Volatility Inference → Adaptive Learning → Updated Prediction**

The image should communicate that learning is dynamic.

The brain does not simply react to every surprising event. It estimates
whether the world is stable or changing and adjusts the strength of
belief updating accordingly.

---

## Scientific message

The world contains both uncertainty and change.

An unexpected observation can arise because:

- the sensory observation is noisy;
- the current state is uncertain;
- or the underlying statistical structure of the environment has
  changed.

Adaptive cognition requires distinguishing these possibilities.

The key computational question is:

> **Is this unexpected observation noise, or evidence that the world
> itself has changed?**

This distinction allows the system to regulate its learning rate.

A stable environment favors relatively conservative updating.

A volatile environment may require faster updating.

The image should therefore represent **adaptive belief updating**, not
simply "more prediction error."

---

## Primary visual sequence

### 1. Stable Environment

Begin with a visually ordered and relatively predictable environment.

Repeated observations should follow a recognizable pattern.

For example:

**A → A → A → A**

The visual should communicate stability without implying absolute
certainty.

↓

### 2. Unexpected Observation

Introduce an observation that violates the established pattern.

For example:

**A → A → A → B**

This represents a prediction error.

The unexpected event should be visually noticeable but not chaotic.

↓

### 3. Accumulating Evidence

The image should show that a single prediction error does not
automatically prove that the environment has changed.

For example:

**A → A → B → A → B → B**

As unexpected outcomes accumulate, evidence for a changing environment
increases.

↓

### 4. Volatility Inference

The system evaluates whether the underlying statistical structure is
changing.

This should be represented as a higher-level inference rather than as
another sensory signal.

Conceptually:

**"Maybe the rules have changed."**

↓

### 5. Adaptive Learning

The brain adjusts the degree to which new evidence changes its beliefs.

Represent this visually as a dynamic learning gate or adjustable
weight.

Stable environment:

**small update**

Volatile environment:

**larger update**

The visual should make clear that the appropriate learning rate depends
on inferred environmental conditions.

↓

### 6. Updated Prediction

The system incorporates the new information into its model.

The resulting prediction should become appropriate for the new
environment.

The visual sequence should therefore form a loop:

**Prediction → Observation → Error → Volatility Inference → Learning →
Updated Prediction**

rather than a one-way pipeline.

---

## Central visual metaphor

A useful metaphor is an **adaptive balance between stability and
flexibility**.

One side represents:

**Stability**

"Don't change your model because of every fluctuation."

The other represents:

**Flexibility**

"Change your model when the evidence indicates that the environment
has changed."

The brain should appear to continuously adjust the balance.

This is more scientifically informative than depicting volatility as
simply chaos.

---

## Visual representation of uncertainty

Uncertainty should appear as a degree of ambiguity surrounding the
current state.

For example, several possible interpretations could be shown with
different levels of opacity or visual weight.

Avoid representing uncertainty as total confusion.

The computational problem is not:

**"The brain knows nothing."**

It is:

**"The brain has incomplete information and must estimate what is most
likely."**

---

## Visual representation of volatility

Volatility should be represented as **changing structure**, not simply
randomness.

For example:

Stable:

**● ● ● ● ● ●**

Changing:

**● ● ○ ● ○ ○**

The visual should suggest that the relationship generating observations
is itself changing.

Avoid:

- explosions;
- visual noise;
- chaotic brain imagery;
- broken neural networks.

Volatility is not synonymous with disorder.

It is a statistical property of the environment.

---

## Visual representation of learning rate

Learning rate can be represented by the size of the update between two
successive belief states.

Low learning rate:

**Old belief ──→ slightly updated belief**

High learning rate:

**Old belief ─────────→ substantially updated belief**

This gives the viewer an intuitive representation of adaptive updating.

---

## Relationship to Articles 4–6

Article 4:
**Predictive Processing**

The brain continuously generates predictions.

Article 5:
**Prediction Error**

Unexpected observations create discrepancies between prediction and
evidence.

Article 6:
**Precision**

The brain estimates how much weight information should receive.

Article 7:
**Uncertainty + Volatility + Learning**

The brain must determine whether uncertainty reflects noise or whether
the underlying environment has changed, and then adjust belief updating.

The visual progression is therefore:

**Prediction**
↓
**Error**
↓
**Precision**
↓
**Uncertainty**
↓
**Volatility**
↓
**Learning**

This should make Article 7 feel like a genuine continuation of the
research journey.

---

## Relationship to schizophrenia

The image should NOT depict schizophrenia directly.

Avoid:

- distorted faces;
- hallucination imagery;
- "broken brain" imagery;
- red or damaged neural circuits;
- psychosis stereotypes.

Instead, establish the normal computational problem first.

A small secondary annotation could indicate:

**Psychosis research asks whether this adaptive system behaves
differently under uncertainty and changing environments.**

This keeps the visual scientifically neutral.

---

## Relationship to PIF

PIF should appear only subtly, if at all.

The image should establish the computational problem before introducing
the hypothesis.

The conceptual progression is:

**Uncertainty**

↓

**Precision regulation**

↓

**Prediction-error weighting**

↓

**Learning-rate regulation**

↓

**Belief updating**

PIF then asks whether the temporal regulation of these quantities may
become unstable in psychosis.

PIF should NOT be represented as an established mechanism.

If included, it should appear as a small research question:

> **Could instability in precision regulation matter?**

---

## Preferred composition

Use a wide horizontal scientific illustration.

### Left side

A relatively stable external environment.

Repeated observations form a recognizable pattern.

### Center-left

An unexpected observation appears.

A prediction-error signal connects the observation to the internal
model.

### Center

A stylized computational system evaluates:

**Noise?**

or

**Environmental change?**

### Center-right

A volatility estimate changes.

An adjustable learning gate changes its weighting.

### Right side

A new internal model produces an updated prediction.

### Bottom

A subtle feedback loop returns from the updated prediction to the
environment.

This creates the visual impression of continuous adaptive inference.

---

## Color and style

Maintain the visual identity of the Schizophrenia Research Journey:

- clean scientific illustration;
- sophisticated editorial aesthetic;
- restrained color palette;
- dark or neutral background;
- luminous information pathways;
- minimal text;
- no cartoon brain;
- no medical horror imagery;
- no exaggerated psychedelic effects.

The image should feel suitable for a serious neuroscience article.

---

## Typography

If text is included inside the image, keep it minimal.

Primary labels:

**Prediction**

**Observation**

**Prediction Error**

**Uncertainty**

**Volatility**

**Learning Rate**

**Updated Belief**

Avoid long paragraphs inside the image.

---

## Key scientific distinction

The visual must clearly distinguish:

**Prediction Error**

from

**Volatility**

Prediction error:

> "What I observed differs from what I expected."

Volatility:

> "The underlying relationship may itself be changing."

The image should make this distinction one of its central teaching
features.

---

## Key conceptual distinction

The image should also distinguish:

**Uncertainty**

from

**Volatility**

Uncertainty:

> "I am not very certain about the current state."

Volatility:

> "The underlying state or statistical relationship may be changing."

These are related but not identical.

---

## What the image should communicate at a glance

A viewer should be able to understand:

> The brain encounters surprising information.

> It does not automatically change its beliefs.

> It evaluates whether the surprise is noise or evidence of change.

> It estimates uncertainty and volatility.

> It adjusts how strongly it learns.

> It updates its model.

> And then it predicts again.

---

## Avoid

Do NOT show:

- "the brain hallucinates reality";
- schizophrenia as a broken brain;
- prediction error as synonymous with psychosis;
- volatility as chaos;
- high learning rate as schizophrenia;
- PIF as established science;
- one brain region responsible for volatility;
- a simplistic bottom-up/top-down binary.

---

## Suggested image caption

**How does the brain know when to change its mind?**

Learning requires more than detecting prediction errors. The brain must
estimate uncertainty and environmental volatility and adjust how strongly
new evidence changes its beliefs.

---

## Accessibility description

A conceptual scientific illustration showing an adaptive learning loop.
A stable pattern of sensory observations is disrupted by unexpected
events, producing prediction errors. The system evaluates uncertainty
and whether the environment is becoming volatile, adjusts its learning
rate, updates its beliefs, and generates a new prediction. The image
emphasizes adaptive belief updating rather than depicting volatility as
chaos or schizophrenia as a damaged brain.

---

## Evidence status

The visual represents established and actively researched computational
concepts including prediction error, uncertainty, volatility, Bayesian
belief updating, and adaptive learning.

It does not represent a validated biological model of schizophrenia.

The Precision Instability Framework (PIF) remains a proposed research
hypothesis.
