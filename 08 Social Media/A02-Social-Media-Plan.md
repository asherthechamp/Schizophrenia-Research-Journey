# Core Message

Schizophrenia is not only a collection of diagnostic symptoms.

It is also a disturbance—or possible disturbances—in how perception,
thought, agency, self, meaning, and motivation are experienced.

Phenomenology asks an important question:

> What is schizophrenia like from the inside?

Understanding that experience may help neuroscience build better
connections between subjective experience, cognition, computation,
and biology.

# LinkedIn / Facebook Post

### What is schizophrenia like from the inside?

A diagnosis can tell us what symptoms are present.

But it does not necessarily tell us what those symptoms feel like.

Two people may both report "hearing voices" while experiencing
fundamentally different phenomena.

One may experience a voice as external.
Another may experience it as internal.
One may experience it as threatening.
Another may experience it as comforting.

The same principle applies to delusions, thought disorder,
disturbances of agency, changes in self-experience, and negative
symptoms.

This is where **phenomenology** becomes important.

Instead of asking only:

> Is the symptom present?

we can ask:

> What is the structure of the experience?

Article 2 of the *Schizophrenia Research Journey* explores:

• hallucinations  
• delusions and salience  
• thought ownership  
• agency  
• minimal self  
• ipseity  
• negative symptoms  
• anhedonia  
• computational psychiatry  
• the relationship between experience and biology

The central question is:

> **How does biology become experience?**

## Post 2

Two people can both report "hearing voices" while experiencing
very different things.

The voice may feel external or internal.

Threatening or comforting.

Controllable or uncontrollable.

So "auditory hallucination" is not necessarily one experience.

---

## Post 3

Delusions raise another question.

What happens when ordinary events begin to feel unusually meaningful?

A glance becomes a message.

A coincidence becomes evidence.

The world begins to feel as though it is pointing toward something.

---

## Post 4

Some schizophrenia-spectrum experiences involve disturbances in
the ordinary sense that thoughts belong to us.

This raises a profound question:

> How does the brain generate the experience that a thought is mine?

---

## Post 5

The same question applies to agency.

Normally:

I decide to move.

I move.

The movement feels like something I caused.

But the experience of agency can become disturbed.

That gives neuroscience an unusual window into the construction
of the self.

---

## Post 6

Phenomenology also investigates the basic sense of self:

• first-person perspective  
• self-presence  
• ipseity  
• ownership  
• agency  
• situatedness in the world

These concepts may help us describe experiences that diagnostic
categories alone cannot fully capture.

---

## Post 7

But we need scientific discipline.

Phenomenological description ≠ biological mechanism.

Association ≠ causation.

An influential theory ≠ an established explanation.

The goal is to connect levels without collapsing one into another.

---

# Scientific Positioning

The social-media package must not:

- present PIF as established science;
- claim that self-disorder causes schizophrenia;
- imply that schizophrenia has one biological mechanism;
- present aberrant salience as a proven complete explanation;
- reduce subjective experience to a single neural correlate.

The social-media material should communicate scientific curiosity,
uncertainty, and the distinction between evidence and hypothesis.
