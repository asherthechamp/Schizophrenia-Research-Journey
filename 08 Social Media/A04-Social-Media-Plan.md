The brain does not simply receive a finished description of the world.

Sensory information is incomplete and uncertain.

Predictive-processing theories propose that the nervous system may
combine sensory evidence with prior information, predictions, context,
and uncertainty to construct perceptual interpretations.

But prediction is not imagination.

And predictive processing is not an established complete explanation
of schizophrenia.

The important scientific question is how evidence and expectations
interact.

### What Is Predictive Processing?

How does the brain construct a coherent experience from incomplete
sensory information?

Our brains constantly encounter uncertainty.

Light, sound, touch, and other sensory signals do not arrive as a
finished description of the world.

Predictive-processing theories propose that the nervous system can
combine incoming sensory evidence with prior information and
expectations.

A simplified cycle is:

Prediction

↓

Sensory evidence

↓

Comparison

↓

Prediction error

↓

Model updating

↓

New prediction

This framework has become influential in neuroscience and
computational psychiatry.

But scientific caution is essential.

Predictive processing is not a complete theory of the brain, and it is
not an established complete explanation of schizophrenia.

Instead, it offers a computational framework for asking questions
about perception, belief, uncertainty, learning, hallucinations,
delusions, and agency.

Article 4 of the Schizophrenia Research Journey explores these ideas
and introduces the role of precision in inference.

How does the brain make sense of an uncertain world?

The sensory information reaching the brain is incomplete, noisy, and
ambiguous.

Yet we experience a coherent world.

Predictive-processing theories propose that the brain may continuously
combine sensory evidence with predictions, prior information, context,
and uncertainty.

This creates a fascinating computational cycle:

Prediction → Sensory Evidence → Prediction Error → Updating → New
Prediction

But there is an important distinction.

Predictive processing does not mean that the brain simply invents
reality.

And it does not mean that schizophrenia is simply a disorder of

## Post

The fourth article in my Schizophrenia Research Journey explores
predictive processing.

The basic idea is relatively simple:

The brain receives incomplete and uncertain sensory information.

It can also use prior information and expectations.

Predictive-processing theories propose that perception may emerge from
the interaction between these sources of information.

A simplified cycle is:

Prediction → sensory evidence → prediction error → model updating →
new prediction.

The article also looks at an important distinction between predictive
processing and Bayesian inference, discusses precision, and considers
why predictive-processing theories have become relevant to research on
psychosis.

I have tried to keep the distinction between evidence and hypothesis
explicit.

Predictive processing is an influential framework, but it is not a
complete explanation of schizophrenia.

The article also introduces how this framework may eventually connect
with the Precision Instability Framework (PIF), which remains a proposed
research hypothesis rather than an established theory.
