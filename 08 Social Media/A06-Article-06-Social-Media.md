# LinkedIn

## Post

Not all information carries the same weight.

When the brain encounters a mismatch between prediction and sensory evidence, the mismatch itself isn't enough to determine how strongly the system should update.

It also matters how reliable that information is estimated to be.

This is the idea behind **precision**.

In statistical models, precision is commonly represented as the inverse of variance:

**Precision = 1 / Variance**

In predictive-processing models, precision can influence how strongly prediction errors affect inference.

That gives us a useful progression:

**Prediction → Prediction Error → Precision → Inference → Experience**

But precision is not the same as truth, and abnormal precision should not simply be equated with schizophrenia or psychosis.

Some predictive-processing theories propose that altered precision weighting may contribute to aspects of psychosis, but the underlying mechanisms remain an active research question.

Article 6 of the Schizophrenia Research Journey explores precision, uncertainty, prediction-error weighting, and the deeper question of whether the *dynamics* of precision might matter.

I call the latter possibility the **Precision Instability Framework (PIF)**—a proposed research hypothesis that remains to be tested.

## Post

Not all information carries the same weight.

The brain is constantly dealing with incomplete and uncertain sensory information.

A faint sound in a crowded room shouldn't necessarily overturn your current interpretation of the world.

A clear alarm probably should.

What differs?

Partly, the estimated reliability of the evidence.

This is the intuition behind **precision**.

In many statistical models:

**Precision = 1 / Variance**

Higher precision means lower estimated uncertainty.

In predictive-processing models, precision can influence how strongly prediction errors affect inference.

So our conceptual sequence becomes:

**Prediction**

↓

**Prediction Error**

↓

**Precision**

↓

**Inference**

↓

**Experience**

But there is an even deeper question.

Precision itself can change over time.

A healthy brain needs this flexibility. The problem, if one exists in psychosis, would not simply be that precision changes.

It might involve changes that are poorly regulated or insufficiently coupled to the actual reliability of information.

This leads to the proposed **Precision Instability Framework (PIF)**.

PIF is not an established explanation of schizophrenia. It is a hypothesis that needs to generate testable predictions.

## Post

Why doesn't the brain treat every piece of information as equally reliable?

Imagine hearing a faint sound in a crowded room.

It could be meaningful—or it could simply be noise.

Now imagine hearing a loud alarm.

Both are sensory signals.

Both can produce prediction errors.

But they shouldn't necessarily have the same influence on your interpretation of the world.

This is where **precision** becomes important.

In many statistical models, precision is represented as the inverse of variance:

**Precision = 1 / Variance**

In predictive-processing theories, precision can influence how strongly prediction errors affect inference.

This gives us a new way of thinking about perception:

**Evidence → Prediction Error → Precision Weighting → Inference → Experience**

When we apply these ideas to psychosis, however, we need to be careful.

There is no simple scientific rule saying:

**"Schizophrenia = too much precision."**

Different computational accounts emphasize different combinations of priors, sensory evidence, prediction errors, uncertainty, attention, and hierarchical inference.

Article 6 explores these ideas and introduces a further research question:

**Could the dynamics of precision itself matter?**

This is the starting point for the proposed **Precision Instability Framework (PIF)**.

PIF is a research hypothesis, not an established theory.

# Discussion questions

1. Is precision best understood as reliability, certainty, or something more specific?

2. How might the brain distinguish reliable prediction errors from sensory noise?

3. Could attention partly involve changing the effective precision of information?

4. Is abnormal precision sufficient to explain psychosis?

5. Could the *stability* of precision over time be more informative than its average level?

6. How could precision instability be experimentally distinguished from ordinary neural or behavioral noise?

# Evidence / communication note

Avoid presenting PIF as established science.

Preferred wording:

> "The Precision Instability Framework is a proposed research hypothesis."

Avoid:

> "PIF explains schizophrenia."

Avoid:

> "Schizophrenia is caused by precision instability."

The social-media package should preserve the distinction between established computational concepts, active scientific debates, and the proposed PIF hypothesis.
