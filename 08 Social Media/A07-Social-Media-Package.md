LinkedIn Post

How does the brain know when to change its mind?

Every unexpected event creates a computational problem.

Was it simply noise?

Or has something about the world actually changed?

This is where prediction error, uncertainty, volatility, and learning come together.

A prediction error tells us that something didn't match our expectation.

But the brain needs to go further.

It must estimate:

• How reliable is this information?
• How uncertain am I?
• Is the environment changing?
• How much should I update my beliefs?

In a stable environment, changing our beliefs after every surprising event would make us vulnerable to noise.

In a volatile environment, refusing to update would leave us stuck with an outdated model.

Adaptive cognition therefore requires a balance between stability and flexibility.

This is particularly interesting in schizophrenia and psychosis research.

Studies suggest that some aspects of volatility estimation and belief updating may differ in psychosis, but the evidence does not support a simple explanation such as "schizophrenia means learning too quickly."

The more interesting question may be how the brain regulates updating across time and changing contexts.

This also motivates an open research question within the Precision Instability Framework:

Could temporal instability in precision and uncertainty regulation contain information that average computational parameters miss?

That is a hypothesis to test—not an established theory.

Post 2

Imagine a stable environment.

You observe:

A → A → A → A

Then suddenly:

A → A → A → B

That's a prediction error.

But should you completely change your model?

Not necessarily.

The event could simply be noise.

Post 3

Now imagine:

A → A → B → A → B → B

Repeated unexpected observations provide stronger evidence that something about the environment may have changed.

This is where volatility becomes important.

Post 4

Uncertainty and volatility aren't the same thing.

Uncertainty:

"I don't know the current state very well."

Volatility:

"The underlying state or statistical relationship may itself be changing."

The distinction matters.

Post 5

Learning rate connects these ideas.

A simplified rule is:

New belief = Old belief + Learning rate × Prediction Error

A stable environment may favor conservative updating.

A volatile environment may require faster updating.

Post 6

This creates a fundamental trade-off:

Too little updating → rigidity.

Too much updating → instability.

Adaptive cognition requires both stability and flexibility.

Post 7

This is particularly interesting in schizophrenia and psychosis research.

Some studies report altered volatility estimation or belief updating.

But there is no simple computational signature such as:

"Schizophrenia = high learning rate."

The findings are more complex.

Post 8

An important possibility is that the problem lies partly in the regulation of updating over time.

Two people can have the same average learning rate while showing very different trial-by-trial dynamics.

Average parameters may therefore hide important temporal structure.

Post 9

This motivates an open question within the Precision Instability Framework:

Could instability in precision and uncertainty regulation provide information beyond average computational parameters?

That's a hypothesis—not an established theory.

Facebook Post

How does the brain learn when the world is changing?

The brain constantly encounters unexpected information.

But an unexpected event doesn't automatically mean that the world has changed.

It could simply be noise.

The brain therefore has to solve a deeper problem:

Is this surprise random, or is the underlying environment becoming different?

This involves several interconnected computational concepts:

Prediction error → Precision → Uncertainty → Volatility → Learning → Belief updating.

In a stable environment, updating too strongly can make us chase noise.

In a volatile environment, updating too weakly can leave us trapped in an outdated model.

Healthy cognition therefore requires a balance between stability and flexibility.

This becomes particularly interesting in schizophrenia and psychosis research, where researchers are investigating differences in uncertainty estimation, volatility inference, and belief updating.

The important scientific question is not simply whether people update "too much" or "too little."

It may be how effectively the brain regulates updating as circumstances change.
