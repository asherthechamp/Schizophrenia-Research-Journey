**How the brain decides what matters—and why schizophrenia may involve a disturbance in that process**

## LinkedIn Post

What does the brain mean when it decides that something “matters”?

A sound, a face, a coincidence, a memory, or an internal thought can suddenly become highly significant.

But salience is not simply attention.

The brain has several overlapping systems for determining what deserves priority:

- Sensory salience
- Attentional salience
- Emotional salience
- Motivational or incentive salience

And dopamine is not simply a “pleasure chemical.” Modern neuroscience distinguishes between liking, wanting, and learning.

This becomes especially interesting in schizophrenia.

One influential idea is **aberrant salience**: ordinary events may sometimes acquire an unusual sense of significance, potentially contributing to the search for meaning that can occur during psychosis.

But salience is not the same as:

- Prediction error
- Precision
- Attention
- Dopamine
- Belief

These concepts interact, but they are not interchangeable.

This distinction matters because we are trying to understand something deeper:

**How does biology become experience?**

The path may look something like:

molecular mechanisms → neural signaling → circuit dynamics → network coordination → information weighting → salience → interpretation → subjective experience → belief and behavior.

In this article, I explore salience as one possible bridge between neural mechanisms and lived experience—and consider how disturbances in information weighting might relate to schizophrenia.

This also connects to the broader Precision Instability Framework (PIF), which remains a hypothesis to be tested rather than an established theory.

Something can be salient without becoming the focus of conscious attention.

• Sensory salience

### 4/10

Dopamine is also more complicated than the popular phrase “reward chemical” suggests.

Researchers distinguish between:

**Liking** — pleasure  
**Wanting** — motivational value  
**Learning** — updating what we expect

Dopamine is deeply involved in these processes, but it doesn't simply equal pleasure.

One influential hypothesis is **aberrant salience**.

Events that would normally seem insignificant may sometimes acquire an unusually strong sense of significance.

### 6/10

That unusual significance could contribute to a person's search for explanations.

A coincidence may no longer feel like merely a coincidence.

An ordinary event may suddenly seem personally meaningful.

Importantly, aberrant salience is a theory—not a complete explanation of schizophrenia.

### 7/10

We also need to avoid collapsing different concepts into one.

Salience ≠ attention  
Salience ≠ prediction error  
Salience ≠ precision  
Salience ≠ dopamine  
Salience ≠ belief

These processes interact, but they describe different levels of the system.

But schizophrenia cannot be reduced to one brain region or one network.
