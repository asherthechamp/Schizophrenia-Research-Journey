## Post

How does the brain stop something that it has already started?

Imagine beginning an answer and suddenly realizing that it is wrong.

The brain has to interrupt the response already underway and replace it with something better.

That is one of the problems studied under **inhibitory control**.

But inhibition is not simply "doing nothing."

Researchers distinguish between processes such as:

• Action restraint  
• Action cancellation  
• Reactive control  
• Proactive control  
• Selective stopping  
• Interference control  

These processes interact with attention, working memory, and broader cognitive control.

This distinction matters in schizophrenia research.

It is tempting to describe schizophrenia as an "inhibition deficit," but the evidence is much more complicated. Differences on inhibitory-control tasks do not establish a single inhibitory mechanism as the cause of schizophrenia.

A better question is:

**How does the brain maintain appropriate control when many competing signals, actions, and goals are active at the same time?**

That question takes us from inhibition toward cognitive control.

### Inhibition and Cognitive Control

How does the brain stop something that it has already started?

The brain doesn't only need to decide what to do.

It also needs to decide:

• what not to do  
• when to stop  
• what to suppress  
• what to maintain  
• when to change course  

This is where inhibitory control becomes important.

But inhibition is not one single mechanism. Researchers study different forms of control, including action restraint, action cancellation, reactive control, proactive control, and selective stopping.

These processes interact with attention, filtering, working memory, and cognitive control.

This distinction is particularly important when studying schizophrenia.

It would be misleading to simply say that schizophrenia is an "inhibition disorder." People with schizophrenia may show differences on particular inhibitory-control tasks, but those differences can arise from multiple interacting processes.

The broader question is much more interesting:

**How does the brain maintain appropriate control when many competing signals, actions, and goals are active at the same time?**

That question takes us from inhibition to cognitive control.

And inhibition does not operate alone.

It interacts with attention, filtering, working memory, and cognitive control.

A useful progression is:

**Attention → Filtering → Inhibition → Cognitive Control**

Attention determines what deserves priority.

Filtering reduces the influence of competing information.

Inhibition constrains or stops competing responses.

Cognitive control helps maintain goals despite competition.

This also gives us a more careful way to think about schizophrenia.

Rather than saying:

"Schizophrenia is an inhibition deficit,"

we should ask:

**Which form of control is affected, under what conditions, and why?**
