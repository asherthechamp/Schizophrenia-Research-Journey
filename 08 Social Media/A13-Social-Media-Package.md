# 1. Core Message

Consciousness is not simply information processing.

It concerns the presence and organization of subjective experience.

But conscious experience normally arrives from a particular point of view. It can feel like experience is happening *here*, to *me*, and from *my* perspective.

This leads to a deeper question:

**What is the self?**

The self may not be a single object or brain region. It may be a layered organization involving subjectivity, mineness, embodiment, self-reference, and agency.

# 3. X / Twitter Thread

### Post 1

What is consciousness?

A difficult question begins with a simple observation:

There is a difference between information being processed and information being experienced.

Consciousness concerns the presence of subjective experience.

🧵

### Post 2

But consciousness is not the same thing as attention.

Attention concerns priority:

**What should receive processing resources?**

Consciousness concerns experience:

**Is there something it is like to experience this?**

The two can interact without being identical.

### Post 3

Consciousness is also not the same thing as the self.

An experience can be conscious without requiring a fully developed autobiographical identity.

This suggests that selfhood may have layers.

### Post 4

At the most basic level, experience may have a first-person structure.

It can feel like:

**“This experience is happening here.”**

That basic perspectival organization is related to what researchers call the **minimal self**.

### Post 5

The self may therefore involve multiple layers:

- subjective experience
- first-person perspective
- mineness
- embodiment
- self-reference
- agency
- autobiographical identity

These layers should not automatically be treated as one mechanism.

### Post 6

Agency adds another dimension.

Ownership asks:

**“Is this experience or body mine?”**

Agency asks:

**“Did I cause or control this?”**

These can come apart.

### Post 7

Why does this matter for schizophrenia?

Some people with schizophrenia experience disturbances involving the sense of self, agency, thought ownership, or the relationship between inner experience and the external world.

These phenomena raise questions about how normally stable first-person organization is constructed.

### Post 8

But self-disturbance should not be reduced to a single brain region or one simple mechanism.

The scientifically useful question is:

**Which components of self-experience are altered, under what conditions, and through which mechanisms?**

### Post 9

This creates a bridge between phenomenology and neuroscience.

We can describe:

**What the experience is like**

while also investigating:

**What computational and neural processes may contribute to it.**

Neither level automatically replaces the other.

### Post 10

The conceptual progression of this research journey is becoming:

**Prediction → Attention → Filtering → Inhibition → Control → Consciousness → Self → Agency → Self-disturbance**

The next question is:

**What is the minimal self—and what makes an action feel like my own?**

# 4. LinkedIn Post

### What Is Consciousness — and What Is the Self?

Consciousness is one of the deepest problems in neuroscience.

The brain processes enormous amounts of information, but processing alone does not explain why some processes are accompanied by subjective experience.

Consciousness therefore raises a fundamental question:

**How does experience become something that is experienced from a particular point of view?**

This brings us to the self.

The self may not be a single object in the brain. Instead, it may involve several partially distinguishable layers:

- first-person perspective
- mineness
- embodiment
- self-reference
- agency
- autobiographical identity

This distinction matters in schizophrenia research.

Some experiences associated with schizophrenia involve disturbances in the sense of self, agency, thought ownership, or the boundary between internally generated and externally attributed experience.

But these phenomena should not be reduced to a single “self disorder” mechanism.

A more useful scientific question is:

**Which components of self-experience are altered, under what conditions, and through which computational and neural mechanisms?**

This creates a bridge between phenomenology, cognitive neuroscience, and computational psychiatry.

The broader journey is:

**Attention → Filtering → Inhibition → Cognitive Control → Consciousness → Self → Agency → Self-disturbance**

The next step:

**What is the minimal self—and what makes an action feel like my own?**
